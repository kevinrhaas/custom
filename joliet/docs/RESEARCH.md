# Old Joliet Prison — Art & Level-Design Research Dossier

**Subject:** Illinois State Penitentiary–Joliet / Joliet Correctional Center ("Old Joliet Prison"),
1125 Collins Street, Joliet, Will County, Illinois 60432.

**Purpose:** single source of truth for every art, material, layout and narrative decision in the game.

**Compiled:** 2026-08-01. All claims carry an inline source URL, or are explicitly flagged as unverified,
inferred, or invented.

---

## ⚠️ Read this before anything else

**1. This is NOT Stateville.** Old Joliet Prison is the 1857–68 Gothic Revival limestone castle by
**Boyington & Wheelock** (W. W. Boyington also designed the Chicago Water Tower). **Stateville
Correctional Center** is a *different, separate* prison ~4 miles north in Crest Hill, opened 1925–26,
famous for its Benthamite round **"roundhouse" panopticon** cell house. There is **no roundhouse and no
panopticon at Old Joliet.** Between 1933 and 1973 the two prisons were administered as a *single branch*
under one warden, which is why sources constantly conflate them
([NRHP nomination, p. 4](https://dnrhistoric.illinois.gov/content/dam/soi/en/web/dnrhistoric/documents/llinois%20State%20Penitentiary%20-%20Joliet%20Historic%20District.pdf)).
Anything round, glass-domed, or panoptic in a reference image is Stateville. Reject it.

**2. Address correction.** The brief gives "1858 Collins St". **1858 is the opening year, not the street
number.** The address is **1125 Collins Street** (some records: 1125 *West* Collins Street)
([NRHP nomination, Section 2](https://dnrhistoric.illinois.gov/content/dam/soi/en/web/dnrhistoric/documents/llinois%20State%20Penitentiary%20-%20Joliet%20Historic%20District.pdf);
[Joliet Area Historical Museum](https://www.jolietmuseum.org/old-joliet-prison.html)).

**3. There is no HABS documentation of this prison.** See [§0.2](#02-what-does-not-exist-negative-findings).

### 0.2 What does NOT exist (negative findings)

State these plainly to anyone who asks for "the HABS drawings":

- **There is no HABS / HAER / HALS documentation of the Illinois State Penitentiary at Joliet.** I queried
  the Library of Congress HABS/HAER/HALS collection directly via its JSON API. A collection search for
  `joliet` returns **29 items**, none of which is the penitentiary (they are the town survey, the Joliet
  Army Ammunition Plant, the Iron & Steel Works, four railroad depots/bridges, the I&M Canal guard-locks,
  Brandon Road Lock & Dam, and the Will County TB Sanitarium). A collection search for `prison` filtered to
  Illinois returns **4 items** — Cahokia Courthouse, Thebes Courthouse, and two Fort Sheridan buildings.
  ([LOC HABS collection search](https://www.loc.gov/collections/historic-american-buildings-landscapes-and-engineering-records/?q=joliet))
  The nearest relevant record is **HABS IL-320, "Town of Joliet"** (call no. HABS ILL,99-JOL,1-), which is
  **unprocessed field-note material** — "Data Page(s): 443", field notes FN-206, no measured drawings, no
  photographs catalogued for the prison ([LOC item il0584](https://www.loc.gov/item/il0584/)).
  **⇒ The NRHP nomination is the *only* measured-and-surveyed architectural record. Treat it as the HABS
  substitute.**
- **No published measured floor plans of the cell houses are online.** The nomination reproduces historic
  blueprints of the Administration Building's 1st and 2nd floors only, and notes that no blueprints exist
  for its 3rd and 4th floors (they are inaccessible after the roof collapse).
- **No published dimension for cell-house overall length.** See [§2](#2-site-plan--building-footprints) —
  the nomination contains an obvious typographical error here.
- **The underground is barely documented.** See [§6](#6-underground).
- **The "sanctioned mural programme in the dining hall" could not be verified.** See [§4](#4-current-decayed-condition).

## 1. Timeline & institutional history

All entries sourced to the [NRHP nomination](https://dnrhistoric.illinois.gov/content/dam/soi/en/web/dnrhistoric/documents/llinois%20State%20Penitentiary%20-%20Joliet%20Historic%20District.pdf)
unless another URL is given.

### 1.1 Founding & construction (1857–1868)

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **19 Feb 1857** | Illinois General Assembly passes "An Act to Locate and Build an Additional Penitentiary" (*Laws of the State of Illinois*, 20 G.A. 1857, p. 131). The existing Alton penitentiary was condemned as "in every respect unsuited for the purpose of a prison." |
| **Mar–May 1857** | Commission formed. Chicago architects **W. W. Boyington (1818–1898)** and **Otis Leonard Wheelock (1816–1893)** selected. Wheelock and commissioners tour prisons in New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Ohio. Preliminary plans drawn by May. |
| **19 May 1857** | Governor **William H. Bissell** authorises purchase. A **72-acre tract** north of Joliet bought from **Anson H. Taylor, Jr.** at **$100/acre = $7,219**. The site contained a limestone quarry and a natural spring, and lay between the **Illinois & Michigan Canal** and the **Chicago & Alton Railroad**. |
| **Jul 1857** | First-phase contract awarded to **Zadock Casey for $129,000**, transferred to **Lorenzo P. Sanger & Samuel K. Casey** (who also leased Alton prison). By statute the work was to be done **by prison labour under private contractors**. |
| **10 Aug 1857** | Construction begins. ~100 men employed on the kitchen/chapel/dining/hospital block. Work on the East Cell Block stalls because **"initial work at the quarry had not produced enough quality stone."** |
| **22 May 1858** | **OPENING.** The first **53 convicts** arrive from Alton. By December, **226** are on site — building their own prison. With no cells finished they are all housed together in the dining room; disease spreads; there is no permanent hospital. |
| 1859 | $200,000 more appropriated. Women's prison building, east and west workshops, **two corner towers**, part of the north workshop and NE engine house complete. **390 prisoners**; double-celling begins immediately; 190 men sleep in the room intended as a chapel. |
| **1860** | **East Cell Block complete — four tiers, 400 cells.** North and West Prison Walls complete. Southeast, Northeast and Northwest Guard Towers complete. |
| 1858–61 | Five original limestone workshop buildings erected: Center-West (#7), Northwest (#8), Marble Cutting Shop (#9), North (#10). |
| **1862** | **Separate System Prison / Solitary Building (#15)** — the Pennsylvania-system punishment block. |
| c. 1860–65 | Southwest Guard Tower completed. |
| **1866** | **West Cell Block complete — five tiers, 500 cells.** |
| **1867–68** | **Administration Building (#2a)** completed — four storeys + basement; offices below, **warden's residence on the third floor**. |
| 1872 | Population reaches **1,239 — a record for a single prison in the United States** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joliet_Correctional_Center)). |
| 1873 | Warehouse/Stable (#18) and West Warehouse (#33a) present on the Will County Atlas bird's-eye view. |
| **1874** | **Reservoir (#16)** built — 40 ft dia., 8 ft deep, 8,800 gal (1886 Sanborn); 89,000 gal by the 1898 Sanborn. Fed by artesian wells, distributing through **underground pipelines**; primarily held wastewater. |
| **1870** | Women inmates moved to the **fourth floor of the Administration Building** (they had been in the 1859 Women's Cellblock, #24). |

**Design intent, for flavour text:** Boyington & Wheelock chose Gothic Revival as "**the most applicable and
appropriate style of architecture**" for the public-facing elements, because — as at Eastern State
Penitentiary — its medieval-fortification references "conveyed a sense of austerity and security."
Buildings *inside* the walls, out of public view, were deliberately plain, built "in a permanent, safe and
wholesome manner, at the least possible cost of construction." **The prison was designed to be looked at
from Collins Street.** That aesthetic hierarchy — ceremonial south face, utilitarian yard — is the single
most useful level-design insight in this document.

Cell doors were specified as **iron bars, "so that when shut they will admit nearly as much light as when
open."**

### 1.2 Nineteenth century: labour, punishment, scandal

- **Contract/lease labour** dominated until Illinois outlawed contract prison labour in **1904**. Sanborn
  maps name the lessees: **Selz, Schwab & Co.** (boots & shoes, in shops #8 and #10), **E. R. Brainard &
  Co.** and the **Burlington Manufacturing Co.** (marble cutting, #9), **Frankfort Chair Company**
  (woodworking, #9/#10), **J. H. Winterbotham & Son** (cooperage/barrels, #18), **Joliet Ratan & Reed
  Company**, **Ashley Wire Company**, **W. S. Bruce & Company** (saddle & harness, #23), **A. F. Risser &
  Co.**, **Smith & Jones** (broom corn).
- Prisoners quarried and cut stone for the **new Illinois State Capitol**.
- **Gus Reed**, a Black formerly enslaved man, died in the prison after being subjected to the "water
  cure"/shower bath on the orders of **Deputy Warden Benjamin Mayhew**; the ensuing inquiry showed the
  punishment had been used on multiple prisoners (see Thomas Bahde, *The Life and Death of Gus Reed*,
  Ohio University Press, 2014, cited in the nomination).
- **1886:** Illinois governor-to-be **John P. Altgeld** publishes *Our Penal Machinery and its Victims*
  (Chicago: A. C. McClurg), a direct attack on the system Joliet embodied.
- **1893:** Powerhouse & Well Pump House (#13) built — the prison's first central power source.
- **1895:** **Hospital (#27)**, three-and-a-half storeys, carved lintel "HOSPITAL ERECTED A.D. 1895".
- **1896:** **Women's Prison** built east of Collins Street, also Boyington & Wheelock, also on-site
  limestone — "an exact mini-replica of the male prison" per Wikipedia.
- **c. 1888–90:** West Schoolhouse (#4) and East Schoolhouse (#28) appended to the outer ends of the cell
  blocks, completing the ceremonial south front.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **1904** | Contract labour in Illinois prisons outlawed; shops convert to state-use prison industries. |

| **1953** | East Schoolhouse remodelled — **stone memorial plaque above the north entrance recording the 1953 remodelling.** |

| **1959** | Hospital remodelled (cornerstone on the south facade). |
| **1966** | **School (#5)** and **Chapel (#6)** by **Loebl, Schlossman, Bennett & Dart** (see conflict #6/#7). |
| **1961** | Ragen leaves to become Director of Public Safety; **Frank J. Pate** becomes warden. Quarries close. |
| Late 1960s | **Medical experimentation.** Malaria studies (with the US Army) ran at Stateville; hepatitis experiments involving Vietnam-draft conscientious objectors are reported at Joliet. Malaria research on Illinois inmates was halted in 1974 ("Prison Official in Illinois Halts Malaria Research on Inmates," *New York Times*, 28 Apr 1974, p. 50). |
| **Jul 1969 / 1970** | **Illinois Department of Corrections** created; Joliet renamed **Joliet Correctional Center**. |

| **22 Apr 1975** | **THE JOLIET RIOT.** Finkbeiner orders five gang instigators transferred to Menard. On transfer day a third inmate refuses at the west gate, walks back into the **west cellblock**, and **~70 prisoners — mostly Blackstone Rangers** — follow. By 1:00 pm they hold the cellblock and have seriously injured a guard. Superintendent **Robert Buchanan** and Jaycee photographer **Herbert Catlett** enter and are taken hostage with other guards and ~12 unaffiliated inmates. Finkbeiner has **tear gas lobbed through the cellblock windows**; an entry team retreats after finding **Catlett's body on the third tier**. Governor Daniel Walker orders negotiation. The rioters' three demands: no transfer that day; no physical retaliation; a Black TV reporter from Chicago admitted. Finkbeiner agrees and **records a statement broadcast over a local Joliet radio station**; once the prisoners hear it on the radio they release the hostages. The transfers happen anyway within days. Source: Illinois Legislative Investigating Commission, *The Joliet Correctional Center Riot of April 22, 1975* (Chicago, 1975); Useem & Kimball, *States of Siege* (Oxford UP, 1991), pp. 68–73. |
| **1976** | Fire destroys the second storey of the **Harness Shop (#23)**; it is rebuilt one storey with a flat roof. |
| **Dec 1977** | Illinois "**Class X**" law — determinate sentencing, no parole. Beginning of mass incarceration in the state. |
| **1987** | Last major construction: **Dining Hall / Cafeteria (#25)** and **Gymnasium / Multipurpose Building (#26)** — the prison's first purpose-built recreation space, with a regulation basketball court, weight room and showers, on the site of the demolished 1903 dining hall. |
| **1990s** | Northern Reception and Classification Center processes **up to 23,000 inmates a year**. Remaining industries: **mattress factory** (in the Marble Shops #21/#22), **data entry for the Illinois Secretary of State**, and the **automotive garage**. |
| **2000** | Only **400 inmates** are permanently housed — all in the **west cellhouse**. $5 M spent on a new 300-bed wing at the former women's prison. |
| **Nov 2001** | Governor **George Ryan** announces closure to help close a $500 M budget gap. Warden **Ron Matrisciano**: "Since they built this, they've found better ways to do things, just like any industry" (*Chicago Tribune*, 1 Dec 2001, p. 19). |
| **16 Feb 2002** | **The last permanent inmates are transferred to Stateville.** *Chicago Tribune*, 15 Feb 2002: "Prison in Joliet Closing its Doors After 144 Years." |

- **Collins Street runs along the EAST side.** The **east prison wall is set back ~18 ft** from it, with a
  narrow grass parkway and concrete sidewalk between.
- **The I&M Canal Trail and the railroad run along the WEST side**, between the prison and the Des Plaines
  River. The tracks are the former **Chicago & Alton**, now **Union Pacific**, carrying **Amtrak** and
  **Metra Heritage Corridor**. Abandoned Chicago, Rock Island & Pacific switch tracks outside the west wall
  have been paved over.
- The complex sits **~1.5 miles north of downtown Joliet, just east of the Des Plaines River.**
- **North wall:** Hills Avenue runs east–west just outside it.
- The historic yard was divided by an east–west "**Main Street**" (still a paved road, entrance-to-entrance)
  and a north–south "**Broadway**" from the Administration Building to the north end — **Broadway is gone.**
  A **rail spur ran into the yard along Main Street to deliver coal.**
- **Historically the WEST entrance was the busy one**, being nearer the river and the railroad.

## 3. Cell house interiors

**The player is walking through the 1947–1953 fabric, not the 1860s fabric.** Every 19th-century cell was
demolished in that campaign — except the one Warden Ragen preserved as a museum piece in the Solitary
Building courtyard.

### 3.1 Layout logic

- **Cells are free-standing blocks in the middle of the building**, "**set back from the exterior walls**"
  and **arranged back-to-back in two horizontal lines, facing the north and south walls of the building**
  (NRHP, both cell blocks). So each tier has **two galleries**, one along the north wall and one along the
  south wall, with the cell stack sandwiched between them. **There is no central nave and no roundhouse.**
- **The gallery runs between the cell fronts and the exterior window wall** — you look out of a cell,
  across a narrow walkway, at a wall of glazed openings. This is the defining visual of the space and is
  clearly readable in NRHP survey photo 12 ("West Cell Block (#3), second-floor south cellblock tier, view
  west").
- **"Walkways between each stacked tier feature metal railings"** (NRHP, East Cell Block entry).
- Cell doors are **barred sliding metal doors** — "a barred metal door" (NRHP). The 1857 spec insisted on
  bars specifically so cells would "admit nearly as much light as when open."

### 3.3 ⚠️ The cell-door mechanism — read this before modelling it

The brief specifies "the lever/rack sliding gang mechanism." **I could not verify this for Joliet.** No
NRHP text, tour source, or photo essay I located names the manufacturer or mechanism type. The general
typology is real and well documented in the patent literature — a jailer's **operating lever** drives a
**rack bar**, which throws locking dogs on a whole row of sliding doors at once (see e.g.
[US1110470A, "Lock-box mechanism for jail-cells"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US1110470A/en);
[US1840623A, "Jail door locking mechanism"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US1840623);
[US1739897A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US1739897A/en)) — and a 1947–53 American cell-house
renovation would certainly have used a gang-locking system of this family.

**Ruling: build a period-plausible lever-and-rack gang lock, but label it in your art bible as
TYPOLOGY-INFERRED, not Joliet-documented.** If accuracy matters for a hero asset, the Joliet Area
Historical Museum is the only body that can confirm it.

### 3.4 Tier heights

**❌ Not documented.** The only published vertical figure is the **7-ft** original cell height. The 1947–53
cells are described only as "larger." The buildings read externally as "two-storey and basement" while
containing four or five tiers, which means the tier-to-tier dimension is small — consistent with roughly
**8–9 ft** floor-to-floor for a 7-ft cell plus structure. **Treat 8'-6" as an extrapolation.**

## 5. Material & colour palette

### 5.1 Method — how these hex values were derived

Three independent inputs, reconciled:

1. **Owner's directly-observed calibration notes** (treated as primary reference per the brief).
2. **Numerical sampling of the 41 NRHP survey photographs** (July & Oct 2022, Ramsey Historic Consultants /
   Bauer Latoza Studio). I extracted the embedded JPEGs at native resolution from the nomination PDF and ran
   region-limited median-cut quantisation, reporting the dominant clusters per region. **These are
   defensible, reproducible readings off documented photography** — but they are *as-lit*: the July shots
   are hard summer sun with deep blue sky (blue fill in shadows, warm highlights); the October shots are
   flat overcast (neutral, desaturated); the cell-house interior is unlit with a strong daylight backlight
   through translucent glazing, so **all its readings run 20–40% darker than material albedo.**
3. **Published material science** on Joliet-Lemont dolomite.

### 5.2 The stone — get the name right

The stone is **Joliet-Lemont limestone**, marketed historically as "**Athens Marble**" (after Athens, the
early name for Lemont). It is a **true dolomite** — calcium *magnesium* carbonate — not a simple limestone,
and is harder and finer-grained than ordinary limestone. Geological unit: **Sugar Run Dolomite**. It was
discovered during the digging of the Illinois & Michigan Canal, and by the 1890s **25 quarries** worked it
in the Des Plaines Valley. Same stone as the **Chicago Water Tower** (Boyington again), Holy Name Cathedral,
St James Cathedral, Second Presbyterian, Rosehill Cemetery and the Union Stock Yard Gate.
([ESCONI](https://esconi.org/2013/04/what-do-you-know-about-joliet-lemont-limestone/);
[Village of Lemont](https://www.lemont.il.us/residents/about-lemont/history);
[Wikipedia: Sugar Run Dolomite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_Run_Dolomite);
[Landmarks Illinois monograph PDF](http://www.landmarks.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/Joliet_Lemont_Limestone_book.pdf))

Illinois State Geological Survey description of the on-site quarry stone: "**thin-bedded, fine-grained,
buff to gray**" (quoted in the NRHP nomination).

**Two shader-critical facts:**

- **The nomination consistently calls it "cream-coloured," not gold.** Every building entry says
  "**rough-cut, cream-colored Joliet limestone**." My sampling agrees: it reads **cream-buff to buff-grey**,
  noticeably less saturated than the owner's #C9AD74. Both readings are defensible under different light —
  **give the shader a warm/cool exposure range rather than a single value.**
- **The exterior stone was SANDBLASTED during the 1947–53 modernisation** (NRHP significance narrative).
  This is why the stone reads bright and open-pored rather than uniformly sooted, and why weathering since
  1953 is *differential* — 70 years of restaining over a cleaned surface, not 165 years of continuous
  accumulation.

"**It is a concrete structure that housed an entrance to an underground tunnel that connected the Illinois State Penitentiary-Joliet on the west side of Collins Street with the Joliet Women's Prison on the east side of Collins Street. (NRHP) |

A central boiler house serving a 15-acre walled campus *must* have had a distribution network. **But no source describes it.** |

### 6.2 What is NOT documented — and what a game must therefore invent

**None of the following appears in any source I could find. All of it is invention:**

- ❌ **Any map, plan, length, cross-section, or diameter of the tunnel system.**
- ❌ **Whether "tunnels" (NRHP uses the plural, once) means more than the single Collins Street trunk with a
  branch to the Yard Tower.** The plural may be loose usage.
- ❌ **The existence of a steam-conduit / utilidor network** linking the Powerhouse to the cell houses,
  hospital, dining hall etc. Highly likely on engineering grounds; **entirely undocumented.**
- ❌ **Any cistern.** I found **no cistern reference of any kind.** The reservoir (#16) is an **above-ground
  earth-and-stone mound structure**, not a cistern. Do not call anything a cistern and cite this document.
- ❌ **Storm/sanitary drainage layout.** Only "primarily held wastewater" for the reservoir.
- ❌ **Tunnel construction, lining, headroom, lighting, sump arrangement, or current accessibility.**
- ❌ **Anything under the cell houses beyond "there is a basement."**
- ❌ **Whether any tunnel is enterable today.** The Collins Street tunnel was **sealed between 1979 and
  2002** and is not on any published tour route.

### 6.3 Design guidance

**Anchor the underground to the two documented facts and invent outward from them:**

1. **The Yard Tower has no door.** This is a gift. A four-storey tower at the dead centre of the yard that
   can *only* be entered from underground is a complete level-design premise, and it is **true**.
2. **The tunnel ran under Collins Street to the women's prison** — a 700+ ft run under a public street,
   with a documented head-house at each end, and it **flooded**.

Everything else — junctions, steam mains, valve chambers, sump pits, the water depth — is yours to invent.
**Label it as invented in the art bible.** See [§11](#11-invented--extrapolated--not-historical).

## 8. Resolving the "glass-paneled central guard tower"

### The concern, stated fairly

"Glass-panelled central guard tower" *sounds* like Stateville's F House roundhouse — a circular cell house
with a glazed observation post at its centre. **Joliet has no roundhouse and no panopticon.** If the design
outline meant *that*, it is wrong and must be cut.

### But the good news: Joliet has a real central tower, and it is better than the invented one

**Yard Tower (#12), built 1940**, is documented as follows (NRHP):

> "The Yard Tower was constructed in 1940 and was accessed via underground tunnels. The yard tower was used
> as a guard house and was located at **the central point of the enclosed prison grounds**… The four-story
> tower has a **round form** with original brick walls laid in a four-course common bond… The third story
> has **four regularly-spaced slit window openings** containing aluminum-frame, one-over-one sashes with
> transoms. **The fourth story contains an observation room with concrete block cladding and
> regularly-spaced rectangular window openings with aluminum-frame, one-over-one sashes.** The tower has a
> **low domed roof with standing seam metal cladding. NO VISIBLE ENTRANCES ARE WITHIN THE TOWER.** The
> one-story block **historically housed access to an underground tunnel.**"

Read that again. Joliet's real central structure is:

- **central** ✅ — literally at the centre point of the yard, at the SW corner of the NE quadrant
- **round** ✅
- **a guard tower** ✅
- **with a fully glazed observation room ringing the top storey** ✅
- **entered only from an underground tunnel that flooded** ✅
- **crowned with a low dome** ✅

### RECOMMENDATION — firm

## ✅ Take option (a), and take it in the strongest form: **the "glass-paneled central guard tower" IS the Yard Tower (#12). Rename it, model it from the NRHP description, and change nothing about its role in the level.**

**Reasoning:**

1. **You lose nothing.** Every dramatic property the design outline wanted — a central, elevated,
   all-seeing, glass-walled guard position dominating the yard — is present in the real building. You are
   not trading spectacle for accuracy; you are getting both.
2. **You gain the best mechanic on the site for free.** *There is no door.* A watchtower reachable only
   through a flooded tunnel is a stronger level-design premise than anything a writer would have invented,
   and it is documented.
3. **It kills the Stateville conflation at the root.** Once the central tower is explicitly the 1940 brick
   Yard Tower with its domed roof, no reviewer, journalist or historian can accuse the game of importing
   the roundhouse. A game about Joliet that contains a panopticon will be criticised for it; a game that
   contains the Yard Tower will be praised for it.
4. **It is silhouette-distinct.** Round brick shaft + soldier-brick vertical stripe + slit windows + glazed
   CMU observation drum + low metal dome is a *memorable, unique* silhouette. A generic glass box is not.
5. **Option (b) — "labelled dramatic liberty" — is strictly worse here.** Dramatic liberties are worth
   taking when reality is duller or less legible. Reality is neither. Spend your liberty budget on the
   underground ([§6](#6-underground)), where the record is genuinely empty.

**What to actually build:**

- 4 storeys, **round**, red-brown brick, four-course common bond.
- A **one-storey brick block projecting south** with a **concrete shed roof** — this is the tunnel head.
- A **vertical column of stacked soldier-brick courses** running up the south face of both block and tower,
  terminating at the sill of a **3rd-storey slit window**.
- 3rd storey: **four evenly spaced slit windows**, aluminium 1/1 sashes with transoms.
- 4th storey: **observation room, concrete-block clad, regularly spaced rectangular windows all round,
  aluminium 1/1 sashes.**
- **Low domed standing-seam metal roof.**
- **No exterior door anywhere.**

**Also do this:** if you want a *second* glazed observation cab elsewhere in the level, the **East and West
Guard Towers (c. 1948/1950)** are exactly that — square limestone bases with a **stuccoed pebbledash
observation storey carrying projecting polygonal bay windows** and a polygonal hipped roof, sitting right
on the wall at each sallyport. And the **four corner towers** carry **cream-brick observation levels with
paired 1/1 windows** on octagonal, bartizan-corbelled stone shafts. **Joliet is not short of glazed
observation cabs. It is only short of panopticons.**
