# What this town did about wells

**Opened and closed by T-0592.** G. Spring's For-Sale notice names two structures on lot 7
of block 16 — *"a large Dwelling-House **and fine well**"* — and the well is documented as
plainly as the house. The address was seated by T-0423 (`docs/LOT-ADDRESS.md`, liberty
**L216**); the well was recorded as documented-and-absent, with the reason, and the reason
given at the time was an absence: *"this town has no well."*

That was true of the model and it was the wrong question. The ticket said so itself: what it
owed was not *draw a well here* but **what does this town do about wells** — because a well
placed on the one lot whose address happens to resolve would be the only well in Chicago, and
that is a distribution no source supports.

This file is that reading. It is answered, and the answer is not a well.

## 1 · The finding, in one paragraph

**Chicago in July 1835 got its drinking water from the lake, by cart.** Andreas's *History of
Chicago* opens its Water Works section with the town's first public act on the question —
the Board of Trustees paying **$95.50 on 10 November 1834 for the digging of a well in
Kinzie's Addition** — and then says what happened next: *"the settlers early cast longing
eyes towards the lake … For some years private enterprise reaped a comfortable little
financial harvest in the operation of water carts, which ran to and from the lake. These
carts were two wheeled vehicles, upon which hogsheads were mounted. Having driven into the
lake, generally at the foot of Randolph Street, the watermen loaded up their reservoirs by
means of pails, and then commenced their journeys 'around town.' Backing their carts up to
the doors of their customers' houses, with a short leathern hose they filled the barrels or
other receptacles placed there for the purpose. The price per barrel varied, according to
competition, from five to ten cents."*

Both sentences are now claims —
`data/research/civic/claims/town_findings_andreas_v1.json#c012` and `#c013` — cut verbatim
out of the OCR whose sha256 that file already commits, with their offsets. The bracket at
the far end is `#c014`: the Chicago Hydraulic Company was not incorporated until **18 January
1836**, six months after the scene date, was stalled by the panic of 1837, and did not
complete its reservoir until **1842**. So the cart era contains July 1835 whole.

**A well was therefore a private thing, and a rare enough one to advertise.** That is the
whole ruling, and §4 is what follows from it.

## 2 · Every well this project can name

Three, in a town the census puts at 3,265 people. They are worth listing because a reader who
finds one of them later should not have to re-derive that it is not new.

| Well | Where | What says so | Grade it could carry |
|---|---|---|---|
| The fort's | *"in the outer inclosure and near the south gate"* | `harrison_1830_river_mouth` draws and labels **Well** on the fort's ground; `hubbard_autobiography_1911` puts it in the same place (leaves 75-76, printed pp. 37-38, ll. 2411-2420) | position `inferred` — two witnesses agreeing, on a plan with no scale bar |
| Lot 7, block 16 | on the lot, unlocated within it | G. Spring's notice, 6 printings 1834-06-18 to 1834-11-19 (`chicago_democrat_1834_06_18#c006`, `_09_03#c005`, `_10_15#c006`, `_11_19#c010`) | existence `documented`, position **nothing** |
| The town's | *"in Kinzie's addition"* | Andreas, the trustees' payment of 10 November 1834 (`town_findings_andreas_v1#c012`) | existence `documented`, position **an addition, not a lot** |

**Both witnesses to the fort's well are pre-1835 and that has to be said out loud.** The
Harrison plan is dated 24 February 1830, and Hubbard's own narrative — the half of that
volume that is a participant's recollection rather than McIlvaine's 1911 compilation —
stops in November 1830. So the fort's well is attested in 1830 and its standing on 1 July
1835 rests on continuity: the garrison was not withdrawn for good until 29 December 1836,
and a fort does not fill in its well while it is still drinking from it. That is a
reasonable inference and it is an inference.

Two of the three cannot be positioned at all. The third is positioned only relative to a gate
on a plan the source record itself says is *"used for ARRANGEMENT AND PROPORTION rather than
for absolute size."*

**And the second one is the argument against a class.** G. Spring's notice prints the well as
a selling point, in the same breath as the size of the house, in all six printings. A vendor
advertises what distinguishes his lot from the lot next door. That is not proof that wells
were scarce, and it is offered here as an argument and not as a fact — but it points the same
way the trustees' single $95.50 well and the water carts point, and nothing in this corpus
points the other way.

## 3 · What was read, including what said nothing

The ticket named three bodies of evidence. All three were read.

**The ordinances say nothing about water, and they had every occasion to.** This town
legislated about fire four times over — an ordinance of 1 November 1833 (repealed), the ward
division and four fire wardens of 20 September 1834 (`chicago_democrat_1834_10_01#c001-c003`),
the ordinance of 13 October 1834 that authorised a warden *"to command any bystander or
bystanders at or near the fire to aid and assist in extinguishing the same, whether it be to
enter the ranks or lines"* (`chicago_democrat_1834_10_29#c001-c002`), and the comprehensive
code of 5 August 1835. **Not one of them provides a drop of water.** The 1834 system is a
bucket line made of whoever is standing there; the water it passes is not legislated, not
sited, and not paid for. The 1835 code's Sec. 21 appoints a fire warden per district to
inspect *"all buildings, chimneys, ovens, stoves, stove pipes, hearths, boilers, ash houses"*
— apparatus, never supply. T-0333 read the stove-pipe clause out of that code; the rest of it
survives in this repository only as raw OCR (`chicago_democrat_1835_08_12`, `_08_26`), and a
line-by-line reading of it is a separate ticket, not this one. Nothing legible in either
printing mentions a well, a cistern, a pump or a reservoir.

**Andreas answers, and §1 is his answer.** Read at the offsets recorded in
`town_findings_andreas_v1.json`, against the same 5 MB OCR the file's sha256 pins. One nearby
item is worth keeping beside it: the town borrowed **$2,000 in June 1835 to improve the
sanitary condition of the place** against a threatened cholera invasion (`#c011`). No source
in this repository says what that money bought, and it must not be assumed to have bought
wells — a town whose settlers were already driving to the lake for water was buying drainage.
The precedent is `#c009`: the town's first debt at all, sixty dollars in October 1834, went
to *drain State Street and redeem a large slough*.

**The corpus's own water trade begins after 1835.** The first men in this project's records
who make or sell the apparatus of a well appear in Fergus's 1843 directory and Norris's 1844
— Samuel Mars, *pump pedler*; Christopher Seebor, *agent Kelley's patent pump*; Noah
Scranton, *block, pump, and spar maker*. Nine years of newspapers before them name no such
trade in Chicago. That is an argument from silence and is weighted as one, but the silence is
nine years deep.

**One false positive, recorded so it is not chased again.** The Chicago Democrat of 22 July
1835 prints a paragraph in which *"a Town-Pump was sunk into the source of the ancient
spring."* It is Nathaniel Hawthorne's sketch *A Rill from the Town-Pump*, reprinted — the
pump speaks in the first person, offers the reader its *"stone goblet"*, and stands among
*"brick buildings"* over the burial place of a spring the *"red Sagamore"* drank from. It is
not Chicago and it is not evidence of anything in Chicago.

## 4 · The ruling

**No well class is placed, and the refusal is on an argument rather than on an absence.**

1. **There is no rule to place them by.** A class in this dataset needs an aggregate argument
   — the kind the 665-roof programme makes from a population count. Wells have none: no
   ordinance requires one, no assessment lists them, no plat draws them, and the one public
   well the town paid for is located to an addition. There is no density to deal from, and a
   density invented for the purpose would be conjectural geometry standing in for the answer
   rather than an answer.
2. **The town's documented answer to water was not a well anyway.** It was a hogshead on two
   wheels, filled by pail at the foot of Randolph Street. Filling the town with well-heads
   would misdescribe July 1835 more badly than leaving it with none.
3. **Lot 7's well therefore stays documented and absent** — but for this reason, which is a
   finding, and no longer for *"the town has no well"*, which was a description of the model.
   `data/research/newspapers/lot_addresses.json` and the record the address seats both say so
   now, and both point here.
4. **The fort's well is a different question and is not settled by this refusal.** It was one
   of six things the Harrison plan names on the fort's ground that this model had never drawn
   — *Well, Wash house, Big Barn with Cupola, Shop, Out Buildings, Fort Cemetery* — while
   eleven records inside the pickets and the garrison garden outside them come off that very
   sheet. The omission is the outer enclosure's, not the well's, and it was filed as its own
   ticket. **Four of the six now stand**: the barn, the wash house and the shop (T-0883,
   2026-09-06) and the two blocks the plate draws under *Out Buildings* (T-0887, the same day).
   **THE WELL IS NOW T-0888, AND IT IS NOT WAITING ON EVIDENCE.** Its pixel has been measured
   off the same leaf — the ring's ink runs x 1440-1453, y 880-894, centre (1446.5, 887.0),
   which is 0.50 m east and 81.5 m south of the committed fort centre, UTM 448225.20 E,
   4637535.32 N — and the ring is about 4.9 m across, which is a map symbol's size and not a
   well's. What it waits on is a CLASS: `generators/archetypes/` holds nine and every one of
   them is a building, a wall or a crib. That is the whole of what stands between this reading
   and a well on the ground, and the ticket says so.
5. **The water carts are the visible thing this reading found, and they are not taken here.**
   A cart, a hogshead, a barrel at a door and a watering place at the foot of Randolph Street
   are a documented piece of this town that the model does not have. They rest on one
   retrospective sentence written in 1884 about a trade nobody minuted, so anything drawn from
   them is `inferred` at best — and that is a demonstration of its own, with its own liberty.
   It is filed as its own ticket.

**Nothing is drawn by this ticket, no confidence moves, and no geometry is baked.** What
changes is that the question is answered on the record, with citations, where the next run
will find it.
