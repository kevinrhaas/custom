Read this first

Four things about this site decide everything else, and three of them are invisible in a catalog. None of the six plans will save you if you get these wrong, and any of the six will work if you get them right.

How I read your sketch — correct me if this is wrong

Everything downstream is built on this, so it's worth thirty seconds:

  • 95 ft overall, front (street) to back (yard).
  • 0–82 ft: the main bed, 3 ft wide against the house. 246 sq ft.
  • 82–89 ft: a 7-ft run where the bed pinches to 2 ft.
  • 89–95 ft: the 6×3 ft firewood pad at the back corner, by the chain link.

If the 7-ft and 6-ft segments are the other way round — pad first, then the pinch — say so and I'll flip them. Nothing else in the plans changes; the pad and the last three plants just swap places.

1. A gangway is not one light condition. It's four.

Twenty-two-foot masonry walls, ten feet apart, running the length of the lot. The middle of that slot is the darkest ground you own. The two ends are not — the street end catches raking sun, and the yard corner is bright enough that your roses are blooming in it.

Gold needs sun to be gold. August Moon in the slot turns a muddy olive by July. It isn't sick — there's just no light to make the pigment.
Blue needs shade to be blue. The blue is a wax bloom sitting on top of a green leaf. Direct sun melts it, and so does an overhead sprinkler. The slot is the single best blue-hosta site on your property — better than anything you could build in the yard.
White is the only colour that reads at sixty feet in low light. Blue goes to black in shade. Green disappears. A white margin is what pulls the eye down the run.
White centres want more light, not less. Fire & Ice, Whirlwind and Pandora's Box have less chlorophyll than a green leaf and less engine to run on. They belong at the ends. This trips up more people than anything else on this list.

2. The three-foot rule disqualifies half the catalog

Your bed is three feet wide against a wall. That is the whole budget. Roughly half the seventy-four varieties on the reference sheet mature wider than that — Blue Angel↗, Sum & Substance↗, Empress Wu↗, Victory↗, Atlantis↗ all want five to six feet. They don't politely stay small. They grow into the walk, get stepped on and torn, and the leaves pressed against sun-warmed brick scorch on the wall side.

Every plant in all six plans matures at 3.5 ft or less — with one deliberate exception.

The vase trick. Krossa Regal↗ and Regal Splendor↗ are listed at 3.5–4 ft wide, but that width is measured at the top of the vase, thirty inches in the air, out over the walk where nothing is in the way. At soil level the base is a fist. You get the mass of a giant on the footprint of a medium. This is the single most useful thing to know about planting a narrow bed, and it's why two of the six plans are built around it.

3. The soil is the actual project

Your photos show limestone screenings, brick rubble and what looks like coal cinder banked against the foundation. That's not neglect — that's what's under most Chicago bungalow gangways. The trench was backfilled with whatever came out of the excavation in 1925, plus a century of ash. The existing hostas along the walk are surviving on the few inches of leaf litter that have accumulated on top of it.

Hostas are shallow — most of the root mass sits in the top ten inches. That's the good news, because it means you have a choice:

Full trench — the right way

Excavate 12″ deep the length of the run. That's about 9 cubic yards out and 9 back in as a 50/50 topsoil–compost blend. Roughly $450–600 in material plus disposal, and a hard weekend with a mattock and a rented dumpster bag.

Pocket method — what I'd do

Dig a 24″×12″ pocket at each plant, amend that, then topdress the whole run with 2–3″ of compost and shredded leaves. About a fifth of the work and a quarter of the cost. The hostas will not know the difference for five years, and by then the worms will have done the rest of the trench for you.

Check the downspout first. There's a leader running along the wall in your photo. Hostas shrug off wet feet in July, but a crown sitting in ice water in March rots, and you'll blame the plant. Make sure that water is leaving the bed before you put $600 of plants in it.

4. It is a slug hotel, and you can design your way out of it

Cool, damp, shaded, walls to hide under, no wind, no ground beetles. If you were designing a slug habitat you'd build a gangway. The good news is that slug resistance is a physical property, not a spray: thick-substanced leaves — which in practice means the blues, because the wax bloom that makes them blue is also what makes them hard to chew — get left alone. Thin-leaved varieties get shredded by June.

Every plan here leans thick. Blue Ridge (plan 5) is the extreme case and would be my pick if slugs are already a problem. One round of iron-phosphate bait at pip emergence in April, and cutting the foliage to the ground after the first hard frost — that's where the eggs overwinter — is the whole programme.

Also worth knowing: a gangway is a rabbit highway, and they'll take new hostas in the first two seasons before the plants get tough. And if the walk gets salted, the first six inches of bed is a splash zone — that's why the mouse ears in these plans sit back from the curb, not on it.

The wood pile and the corner

This is the part of the drawing with the scribbled arrows, so here's what I'd actually do with it. The detail below is the same whichever of the six plans you pick — only the hostas leading up to it change.

Looking back from about the 68-foot mark: the last of the bed, the pinch to two feet, the 6×3 paver pad, the rack holding the stack off the ground and off the brick, and the climbing hydrangea taking the chain link above the woodline. Hostas shown are plan 5.

The pad

Six feet by three, eighteen square feet, at the very back against the wall. Build it like a real paver pad or it will heave by the third winter:

  1. Excavate 7″ and remove the spoil — don't try to compact cinder and rubble.
  2. 4″ of CA-6 compacted in two lifts with a hand tamper. This is the pad; the pavers are just the shoes.
  3. 1″ of HPB or coarse sand, screeded, not tamped.
  4. Pavers, running bond, with a hidden edge restraint on the walk side.
  5. Slope it 1/8″ per foot toward the walk so meltwater leaves rather than sitting against the foundation.

Reclaimed Chicago common brick laid flat is the right material here and often free off the alley. It matches the wall it's sitting against, which a concrete paver never will.

The stack

Do not stack wood against the brick. A woodpile touching a foundation is a bridge — carpenter ants and termites use it, and you won't find out for two years. Keep the stack 4″ off the wall and 6″ off the pavers on a steel rack.

Logs laid across the bed with the ends facing down the gangway, roughly 3½ ft tall. That's the look in the renderings, and it's also structurally the most stable way to stack in a narrow space.

Cover the top only, never the sides. A tarped stack traps its own moisture and composts instead of seasoning. A scrap of steel roofing or a board over the top is all it wants.
Buy it local. Illinois is under emerald ash borer and now spotted lanternfly pressure. Moving firewood is how both of them travel. Source within ten miles and you're fine.

A 6×3 pad, stacked 3½ ft, holds a bit over a third of a cord — comfortable for a fire pit season.

Screening the fence — the corner plant

Here's the honest version. The wood stack is your screen for the bottom three and a half feet of that fence panel; you don't need a plant to do a job the firewood is already doing. What needs covering is the run above the stack and the corner post itself, and the only place you can plant it is the last couple of feet of bed. That corner has four-plus hours of sun — the roses in your photo prove it — so you have real options that the rest of the run doesn't.

Climbing hydrangea ★ pick

Hydrangea anomala ssp. petiolaris. Clings by its own rootlets, so chain link needs nothing done to it. Happy in part shade, white lacecap flowers in June, and cinnamon-coloured peeling bark that is the best thing in the January garden. The catch: sleep, creep, leap — two years of nothing, then it goes. ~$55 in a #2 pot.

Clematis viticella

Chain link is a perfect clematis trellis — it's exactly what the tendrils want. A viticella type or 'Jackmanii' cut hard to 12″ every March means no training, no tangle, and no wilt. Faster than the hydrangea and it needs the sun that corner has. ~$25–35.

Year one, while you wait

Hyacinth bean or cardinal climber, direct-sown in May, $4 a packet. Covers the whole panel by August and dies at frost, which is exactly what you want while the permanent vine is still asleep. Pull it, plant it again next year, stop when the hydrangea takes over.

Not Virginia creeper, not Boston ivy, not wintercreeper. All three will cover that fence beautifully and then get into your mortar joints, under the neighbour's siding, and across the yard. On a hundred-year-old brick wall that's a repointing bill, not a garden.

Building it — sequence, timing and the rest of the money

Do it in this order

  1. Fix the water first. Confirm the downspout discharges past the bed, not into it.
  2. Kill the weeds. There's mature stuff in those photos with real roots. Get it out before you import good soil, or you're planting a nursery bed for it.
  3. Build the pad while the bed is still empty and you can wheelbarrow through it.
  4. Soil. Pockets or trench. Let it settle a week and water it in.
  5. Extend the drip. You already have the mainline and a valve on the wall — that's the expensive part done. Run 1/4″ line down the bed with a 1 gph emitter per plant.
  6. Plant, then mulch 2–3″ of shredded hardwood or leaf mould, pulled back an inch from every crown.
  7. Vine last, so you're not stepping over it all season.

When — and it isn't now

July (now)Build. Weeds, pad, soil, drip. Don't plant — a July hosta in a gangway wants water every single day for a month, and most of what you pour will run down the brick.
Sept 1 – Oct 10Plant. Warm soil, cool air, and roots that keep running until the ground hits about 40°F. It's the best perennial window in Chicago by a wide margin, and it's when the nurseries mark everything down.
Late NovAfter the ground freezes, mulch the first-year plants. Not before — early mulch is a vole invitation.
AprilIron-phosphate bait as the pips break. Rabbit repellent on the new stuff.
After first hard frostCut all foliage to the ground and bin it. That's where the slug eggs are.

Render kit — turning these plans into accurate images

The side elevations are demoted on purpose: they're rough massing sketches. The photoreal renders are what sell a design — so the workflow is to hand a stronger image model (GPT / DALL·E) the measured top-down plan and the exact shopping list for a design, generate a render, then drop it into that design's render slot. This kit is the prompt and the rules that make the result match the plan.

What went wrong in the first pass. The earlier renders were close, but the borders came out far too dense — plants packed shoulder-to-shoulder into a solid wall, with no soil, no mulch, and far more plants than the plan calls for. Hostas in a new planting sit apart, each as a distinct clump with dark mulch between them; they only touch years later at mature spread. The prompt below fixes that explicitly.

Master prompt (paste, then append the design's data)

Photorealistic photograph of a narrow shade garden border planted along a Chicago
"gangway" — the 10-foot-wide grass-and-path corridor between two tall brick bungalows.
Overcast, soft even daylight. Camera at standing eye height (~5.5 ft), looking down the
length of the corridor so the bed recedes toward a vanishing point.

THE SITE (fixed for every render):
- Two 22-ft red-brick bungalow walls, one on each side, in deep shade. A simple path
  or lawn strip runs down the middle.
- The planting bed is ONE continuous strip, only 3 FEET DEEP, running ~95 feet along
  ONE wall. It is NOT a wide mass planting.
- Dark hardwood-mulch soil is clearly visible BETWEEN plants.

PLANTING RULES (this is where past renders failed — follow exactly):
- Plants are spaced at their mature spread and are NOT yet touching: each hosta is a
  SEPARATE, distinct clump with a visible ring of dark mulch around it. Sparse, freshly
  planted, breathing room — NOT a dense continuous hedge or wall of foliage.
- Match the COUNTS and VARIETIES in the list below. Do not invent extra plants or fill
  every gap. If in doubt, use FEWER plants, more mulch.
- The front edge is a single thin ribbon of small mouse-ear hostas — one plant deep,
  low and neat, with mulch showing between them. Not a solid border.
- Keep leaf colors true to the varieties named (blue = dusty blue-grey; gold = soft
  chartreuse; white-margined = green leaf with a clean creamy-white edge).
- Realistic nursery-size plants (1-gallon), not giant mature specimens.

STYLE: natural documentary garden photography, shallow-to-medium depth of field, no
people, no text, no labels, true-to-life proportions for a 3-ft-deep bed.

— THEN PASTE, from the design you're rendering —
DESIGN NAME + the SHOPPING LIST table (variety, mature spread, quantity), read
street-end (left, a little more light) to the deep shade slot. Tell it the dominant
color story in one line (e.g. "blue anchors, crisp white margins, blue mouse-ear edge").

How to use it

1. Pick a design (or a variation) above. Each has a top-down plan and a shopping list — those are the source of truth.

2. Copy the master prompt, then paste that design's shopping list and its one-line color story underneath.

3. Generate. Ask for a couple of variations. When one matches the plan, save it and drop it into that design's render slot.

If the borders still look too dense, add: "Reduce the number of plants by a third, double the visible mulch between clumps, and make sure no two plants touch."

Feeding it the data

The base plant data (spread, colour, light, price, stock) lives in the Plant finder — you can export it as JSON or hand a fresh re-pull to a model with the Lurvey data spec. Everything the renderer needs is in each design's shopping list.
