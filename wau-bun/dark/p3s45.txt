At last, about the beginning of November, two months after the time appointed, Governor Porter came in with the annuity money, and Major Forsyth and Mr. Kercheval with him. The Indians were assembled again, the payment was made, and they laid in a larger quantity of ammunition than usual — they understood well enough that only a good hunt would make up what was already lost — and then they set off for their wintering grounds.

We were changing our own quarters about then, to our no small satisfaction. Through all the disturbances of the summer the new Agency House had been going quietly up — permission to build it had come from the Government at last, after a great deal of delay — and soon after the Governor and his party left, we moved into it.

We had been settled a few weeks when Lieutenant Davies appeared one morning, just as we were sitting down to breakfast, with consternation all over his face. The Indian prisoners had escaped from the black-hole. The commanding officer, Colonel Cutler, wanted Mr. Kinzie to come over to the Fort and counsel with him about what was to be done.

They had probably begun work very soon after they were put down there — the black-hole is a dungeon in the basement of the guard-house. What they noticed first was the regularity of it: meals brought three times a day, and in between, no one at all. So they went at the wall with their knives, and the earth they drew out they scattered thin across the floor of the prison. A blanket went over the opening, and one of the company always sat on the blanket, in place well before the hour when the soldier who had charge of them was due. Every time he came the Indians were sitting quietly, smoking, in perfect order. There was never anything about them to excite suspicion.

They had never read the memoirs of Baron Trenck. What they had done was watch the proceedings of the badgers. Profiting by that example, they shaped the opening as they went, working it in a spiral, and in about six weeks the end of it came up into the open air beyond the walls of the Fort.

They left their blankets behind so as to be as little encumbered as possible in flight. It was bitter December weather. They went out into the woods and prairies in nothing but their calico shirts and leggings, and I can readily believe that hope and exultation kept them comfortably warm until they reached shelter among their friends.

It would compromise our own reputation as loyal and patriotic citizens to say how much this news privately delighted us.

The question was how to get the fugitives back. The Agent could promise no more than to communicate with the chiefs and lay before them the wish of the officers — that the young men should give themselves up a second time, and so clear the men who had had charge of them of the imputation of carelessness the Government would otherwise be very likely to fasten upon them.

On New-Year's Day many of the chiefs came in to the Agency, as their custom was, and their Father put the matter to them.

They answered that if they saw the young men they would tell them what the officers would like them to do. Beyond that they could do nothing. They had kept their engagement; they had brought them in once and put them into the officers' hands. The Government had had them in its power and could not keep them. It must now go and catch them itself.

The Government, having acquired a certain amount of experience the past summer in the business of catching Indians, wisely concluded to let the matter drop.
