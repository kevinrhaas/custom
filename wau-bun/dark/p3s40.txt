We had been quietly at home only a very short time when a summons came for my husband to collect the principal chiefs of the Winnebagoes and meet General Scott and Governor Reynolds at Rock Island, where it was proposed to hold a treaty for the purchase of all the lands east and south of the Wisconsin. Messengers went out to gather the principal men, and, accompanied by as many of them as chose to report themselves, he set off on his trip.

He had been gone about two weeks, and I was beginning to count the days that must pass before I could reasonably look for him back, when one afternoon I walked over to pay a visit to my sister at the Fort. As I came into the large hall of one range of quarters, Lieutenant Lacy came in suddenly from the opposite direction, and, hardly stopping, called out, "Bad news, madam! Have you heard it?"

"No. What is it?"

"The cholera has broken out at Rock Island, and they are dying by five hundred a day. Dr. Finley has just arrived with the news." And he was gone, without waiting to answer a question.

The cholera at Rock Island. And my husband there. I flew to the other door of the hall, the one that looks out on the parade-ground. A sentinel was walking near it. "Soldier," I cried, "will you run to the young officers' quarters and ask Dr. Finley to come here for a moment?"

The man shook his head. He was not allowed to leave his post.

Then Mrs. Lacy's servant-girl came out of a door under the steps. She was a worthless creature, but where help was so scarce, ladies could not afford to keep a scrupulous tariff of moral qualification.

"Oh! Catharine," I said, "will you run over and ask Dr. Finley to come here a moment? I must hear what news he has brought from Rock Island." She put on a modest look and said, "I don't like to go to the young officers' quarters."

I was indignant at her hypocrisy. I was also wild with impatience — and then, to my great joy, Dr. Finley himself appeared.

"Where is my husband?" I cried.

"On his way home, madam, safe and sound. He will probably be here tomorrow." He then gave me an account of the ravages the cholera was making among the troops. They were severe, though less so than rumor had first proclaimed them.

For all the doctor's assurance of his safety, my husband was seized with cholera on the trip. By the kind care of Paquette, and the plentiful use of chicken-broth administered to him by the poor woman at whose cabin he had stopped, he recovered, and reached his home in safety, having taken Prairie du Chien on his route and brought his mother back with him again to her home.

The Indians had consented to the sale of their beautiful domain. Indeed, in such cases there is no alternative. If they persist in keeping their lands, and become surrounded and hemmed in by the white settlers, their situation is more deplorable than if they had surrendered their homes altogether. They are aware of this, and so, as a general thing, they give up their lands when the Government proposes it, and only take care to make the best bargain for themselves that they can. In this instance they were to receive as an equivalent a tract of land[57] running into the interior of Iowa, and an additional sum of ten thousand dollars a year.

One of the stipulations of the treaty was that the Winnebagoes should surrender certain people of their tribe who were accused of having taken part with the Sauks in some of the murders on the frontier, so that they might be tried by our laws, and acquitted or punished as the case might be.
