We had hardly settled back into the quiet of our own house before a summons overtook my husband. He was to gather the principal chiefs of the Winnebagoes and bring them down to Rock Island, to meet General Scott and Governor Reynolds. A treaty was to be laid before them there, for the purchase of every acre the tribe held east and south of the Wisconsin. Runners went out to the principal men. Whoever chose to present himself came, and with that company at his back my husband started.

Two weeks passed. I had begun counting off the days that still had to go by before it was reasonable to look for him, and one afternoon I walked over to the Fort to sit with my sister. I had just stepped into the long hall of one range of quarters when Lieutenant Lacy came through from the far end, moving fast, and barely checked his stride enough to speak. "Bad news, madam! Have you heard it?"

"No. What is it?"

"The cholera has broken out at Rock Island, and they are dying by five hundred a day. Dr. Finley has just arrived with the news." Then he was gone, before I could get a single question out of him.

Cholera at Rock Island. My husband at Rock Island. I ran the length of the hall to the far door, the one that opens on the parade-ground. A sentinel was pacing not far off. "Soldier," I called to him, "will you run to the young officers' quarters and ask Dr. Finley to come here for a moment?"

He shook his head at me. He was not permitted to leave his post.

A minute later Mrs. Lacy's servant-girl came out of a door under the steps. She was a worthless creature; but help was so hard to come by in that country that no lady could afford to keep a scrupulous tariff of moral qualification.

"Oh! Catharine," I said, "will you run over and ask Dr. Finley to come here a moment? I must hear what news he has brought from Rock Island." She composed her face into something modest and answered, "I don't like to go to the young officers' quarters."

The hypocrisy of it made me furious, and the waiting was worse than the fury, and then, to my great joy, Dr. Finley appeared.

"Where is my husband?" cried I.

"On his way home, madam, safe and sound. He will probably be here tomorrow." Then he told me what the disease was doing among the troops. It was bad — severe enough by any measure — but not the wholesale dying that the first report had made of it.

The doctor was right that he was coming and wrong that he was out of danger. My husband was seized with cholera on the road. Paquette nursed him, and the poor woman at whose cabin he had stopped kept the chicken-broth going into him, and between the two of them he was soon over it. He reached home whole. He had gone round by Prairie du Chien on his way, and he brought his mother back with him again to her own home.

The Indians had consented to sell their beautiful country. In such a case there is never really a choice to make. If they hold on to the land, the white settlements close in around them and hem them in, and they are worse off than if they had given it all up at the outset. They know this as well as anyone. So as a rule they let the land go when the Government asks for it, and spend what strength they have on getting the best bargain they can out of the asking. Here the equivalent was a tract of land[57] running back into the interior of Iowa, and ten thousand dollars a year besides.

One article of the treaty asked something further of them. The Winnebagoes were to give up certain of their own people, men accused of having taken part with the Sauks in some of the murders along the frontier, so that our laws could try them, and acquit them or punish them as the case might be.
