Hard rowing brought us before long to the place where the river runs out into that beautiful sheet of water, Winnebago Lake. There was hardly any wind when we came onto it, but the Frenchmen hoisted their sail anyway, in hope of being spared the labor of rowing across. Then they whistled for a wind. They whistled with the whole force of their lungs and nothing came of it. Then they appealed to La Vierge, in that comical mixture of joking and real reverence they keep for such occasions, and nothing came of that either. At last somebody proposed the only remaining course: the goddess of the winds must be propitiated with an offering of some cast-off garment.

Guardapié, who spoke for the crew in all matters, made the application all round. Not one of the poor voyageurs owned a spare article of any kind. A few old rags were finally dug out of the little hold in the bow where they keep their food and their clothing and their dirt in one heap, and these were thrown into the waves. For a moment everybody believed it had worked. The sail stirred, filled a little — and then flapped down idly against the mast. The party were in despair. Then Julian and Edwin put their heads together, whispered, and stepped forward as messengers of mercy. In a trice they had their jackets and vests off and were offering the crew the next garment in, for the raising of the wind.

The boatmen hesitated. It seemed to them too magnificent a gift to accept. But a preparatory shake or two revealed that the contribution was worth rather less than they had supposed, and with a great shout of laughter they gave it to the waves. To the enormous delight and astonishment of the boys, a breeze got up at that exact moment and carried the little vessel beautifully along, about half of the way to Garlic Island. There the charm ran out. It could not be renewed. Further offerings of the same kind produced nothing at all, and every other expedient failed too, and the Frenchmen went back to their oars with some distinctly disrespectful remarks about the lady whose help they had asked for.

Two or three canoes of Winnebagoes came in at the island at the same time we did, and their owners came straight up with an offering of sturgeon they had taken in the lake. As this promised a pleasant change for the noon meal — for the Frenchmen at least — we decided to stop and kindle a fire and cook it. We used the interval to send the boys off to the far side of the island, where the water is clear and shallow over a pebbly beach and altogether too tempting to pass up. Harry, the black boy, went along to watch them bathe, and we amused ourselves picking the fine red raspberries that grow all over that island.

Our enjoyment was cut short by a discovery. The whole place — the vines, the shrubs, and by every appearance the ground itself — was crawling with wood-ticks. The wood-tick fastens on the skin and then works its way into the flesh, and what it leaves behind is a swelling and an irritation that is exceedingly painful and can be dangerous. We raised the alarm and got the boys back at speed to the open, well-trodden part of the island. But we had not left our tormentors behind us. We were conscious of them all that day. They were not done with us for several days after, and the ones that had managed to bury themselves had to be taken out with the point of a penknife or a large needle. When dinner was over we boarded the boat, and the wind having risen in earnest at last, it carried us down to the far verge of the lake and into the mouth of the river beyond — the Winnebago Rapids, they call that stretch.

On the point of land to the right stood a cluster of neat bark wigwams. That was Four-Legs' village.
