The rain had gone down into the ground and the trees and the brush and stayed there, and there was no camping on any of it, so we made our arrangements to pass the night in our little saloon instead. Getting a fire to take was another matter. But in a wild place that no one has picked over there is always something dry — a broken branch back under the thicket, a tuft of grass the rain never reached — and with the spunk, or light-wood, that no traveler in that country goes without, we had a good blaze going at last, and it was a comfort to us.

After a day of cold and tedium, it was a fine thing to close in around that fire, to sit on the ends of the burning logs, and watch the Frenchmen build a supper out of it. The kettle went down into a hollow of red coals. Slices of ham went brown and crisping on the forked sticks — broches — that the voyageurs cut and planted around the brands with a few quick strokes of the knife. The pan of pork and onions hissed, and there is no dish a hungry Frenchman would rather be promised. You want a wet, cold day behind you, taken nearly without food, as ours had been, before you know what that one sociable meal, a supper, is really worth.

The sun went down into great banked masses of cloud, of the kind you see only in our Western sky, and it promised us fair weather for the morning. On that assurance we were glad enough to say goodnight to it, and then, very soon after, to one another.

We had barely got up next morning at the bourgeois's call when we found that the company had grown in the night. A tall young mitiff — a half-breed — stood there, well made and good to look at, with two or three Indians behind him. The Indians shouted their greetings to their "father" and to their new "mother," loud and delighted. They were the first Winnebagoes I had ever seen, and they were, I have to say, by no means the finest specimens of their tribe. The mitiff was one branch of the wide-spreading Grignons, and he had come with an invitation. Judge Law was camped a few miles above us with a friend or two from Green Bay, and would we come and breakfast in his tent? We had never imagined white neighbors so far in. But there is no keeping your whereabouts to yourself while there is one Indian in the country. We waited only for the soldiers to finish eating, and then pushed on for the Butte des Morts — Betty More's, as Mrs. A insisted on calling it to the last.

The Judge's tent stood white in the morning sun on the little rise we were coming up to. The river under it was crowded with canoes, and it was the squaws doing most of the paddling. Indians stood along the banks with their guns and all their hunting gear, because the whole air overhead was moving with teal, which at that season are everywhere and are the best eating a man can ask. The enormous beds of wild rice here, and in the little lake below, are what bring them in the autumn. That feed fattens them until the flesh comes out white and tender past believing, and it is the reason a young sportsman is warned to fire only at the birds flying low: one killed high up is bruised to pieces by its own fall and is worth nothing on a plate.

The canoes closed in until we couldn't move, the women all chattering and laughing at once and holding out their little wooden bowls of fresh cranberries to me, an offering to welcome me in.

I passed the time throwing crackers across to them. Some reached the canoes; some went into the river, and those set off a riot — hands and paddles in the water everywhere at once, and a shout of triumph from whichever of them got to a floating one first.

Shaw-nee-aw-kee was shaking hands all round with men who plainly counted him an old friend. A good many of them were fine, straight, well-built figures, and you could see in every one a frame made to carry fatigue and the hard usage their manner of life demands. One of them was describing, with a great deal of arm and hand, how thick the game lay just then, and when he was given a quantity of ammunition he was hugely pleased and went off that instant to make his boast good.

A short walk brought us to the tent, and there I was presented to Judge Law and to a pleasant little gray-haired French gentleman by the name of Porlier. Voyageurs and half-breeds stood about nearby, the voyageurs hard at work, the half-breeds lounging for the most part and doing whatever they did with a sort of listless indifference.

One class all alive, the other apathetic. Set down side by side that way, the difference between them was striking.

The party talked a while, breakfast was announced, and we went in and took our seats on the ground around the Indian mat that served for a table.

The head of it, the post of honor, was given to me, as of course it would be, which let me look round the whole circle of the company and, past them, straight out through the open door of the tent, so that nothing at the side-table outside got by me. Judge Doty sat facing me with his back to the opening, the other gentlemen to the right and left of him. Our waiter was the tall mitiff who had carried the morning's message. He was dressed as he had been then: calico shirt, a bright-colored scarf knotted at the waist, and a straw hat with a band of black ostrich feathers around the crown, which is the usual dress of his class.

The tin cups came round for our coffee, then breakfast-plates of the same metal, knives and forks after them, and then the food, and the most conspicuous item of it was a large tin pan of boiled ducks.

The Judge meant to show, I suppose, that a man may sit in the middle of a vast wilderness and keep his standards about him. He picked up the plate in front of him, saw something adhering to it that did not please him, and handed it back over his shoulder to Grignon, asking him to wipe it carefully. Grignon drew out of his bosom a black silk barcelona handkerchief, snugly tucked there against whatever occasion the day might bring, gave the tin a furious polishing with it, and handed it back. The Judge looked it over, smiled his approbation, and glanced round the table as much as to say, "You see how I choose to have things done," and applied himself to his breakfast.

In those days the trail for Fort Winnebago began on the shore opposite Butte des Morts and ran through the Ma-zhee-gaw-gaw swamp and on past Green Lake. It was fortunate for the Judge that his horses stood waiting for him to mount and away as early as could be managed after breakfast, or I am afraid the story I was tempted to tell would have gone with him and made the whole of that day's ride an uncomfortable one.

We had hardly finished eating when our hunter came back with the ammunition well accounted for: about fifty fine ducks, shot in little more than an hour. From that morning to the end of our trip we never once wanted for those delicate birds.
