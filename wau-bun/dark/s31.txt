About this time a letter reached Mr. Kinzie from Colonel Richard M. Johnson, of Kentucky. The Colonel had taken a great interest in a school established in that State for the education of Indian youths and children, and he wanted something from the Agent. He wanted him to press the Winnebagoes to send their children to the institution to be educated, and — this was the heavier part of the request — to set aside a portion of their annuity-money to help keep the place running.

The holidays had brought a number of the chiefs into the neighborhood of the Portage. A messenger went out to gather them all at the house of Paquette, the interpreter, so that their Father might hold a talk with them.

On the appointed day they came. The letter was laid before them, and the whole case for civilization and education was set out at length — what it would mean for their nation if even a small number of the younger ones could be well taught by the whites, and then come home to their tribe to teach them in turn the learning, the arts, the manufactures, and the habits of civilized life. At the end of every paragraph, as it was rendered to them, they answered with one voice: "Humph!" Good.

When their Father had finished, Day-kau-ray, the oldest and most venerable of the chiefs, stood up. This is what he said.

"Father, — The Great Spirit made the white man and the Indian. He didn't make them alike. He gave the white man a heart to love peace, and the arts of a quiet life. He taught him to live in towns, to build houses, to make books, to learn all things that would make him happy and prosperous in the way of life appointed him. To the Native man the Great Spirit gave a different character. He gave him a love of the woods, of a free life, of hunting and fishing, of making war with his enemies and taking scalps. The white man doesn't live like the Indian — it isn't his nature. Neither does the Indian love to live like the white man — the Great Spirit didn't make him so.

"Father, — We don't wish to do anything contrary to the will of the Great Spirit. If he had made us with white skins, and characters like the white men, then we would send our children to this school to be taught like the white children.

"Father, — We think that if the Great Spirit had wished us to be like the whites, he would have made us so. As he hasn't seen fit to do so, we believe he would be displeased with us, to try and make ourselves different from what he thought good.

"Father, — I have nothing more to say. This is what we think. If we change our minds, we will let you know."

You can see from what Day-kau-ray said that the Indians hold a settled conviction on the matter: that it is the Great Spirit himself who teaches the white man his arts and his sciences, and that since he has given the Native man no instruction in those branches, it would be unbecoming in him to go and acquire them in an irregular way.
