The plan had Judge Doty in the boat with us as far as the Butte des Morts, where an attendant of his would be standing by with horses to take him along to Mineral Point. He had a court to hold there.

We left his pleasant house on a bright and beautiful morning to begin the passage up the Fox River. Captain Harney meant to stay on at "the Bay" a few days yet, but he came by to walk us down and see us installed in whatever comforts the boat had to offer.

The boat lay down at the river-bank, and everything between us and it was ploughed ground or worse, so he gave me his arm and talked as we went about Mrs. Twiggs, the commanding officer's wife. Fort Winnebago had held no other white lady since the spring, and she was waiting on the arrival of a companion and a friend with no patience left at all. We had met in New York not long after her marriage, so we would not be strangers to one another. I had the whole of it settled in my mind already. Two of our sex instead of one, my piano brought up safe, the Post Library we had bought unpacked at last, everything arranged and in its place, and then, far off in the wilderness as we were, we would make the happiest little circle anyone could imagine. Nothing I looked forward to in that country was anything but bright.

The boat waiting for us was a Mackinac boat of moderate size, some thirty feet of dark-looking hull, crewed by soldiers with our own three voyageurs added to them. Amidships stood a frame of light posts holding up a canvas roof, and canvas curtains hung from it that could be dropped along the sides and the ends, the way they are on a country stage-coach, or rolled up again to let in light and air.

Into the middle of this little cabin, or saloon, went the box that held my piano, and onto the box went a mattress, to serve as a divan through the day and as a place to sleep at night if the weather ever turned too wet or too disagreeable for camping. Beside it were stowed the boxes of silver: the annuities my husband would count out to his red children, owed them by the stipulation of the treaty. The mess-basket sat within easy reach. We had bought, besides, two large square covered baskets from the Waubanakees, the New York Indians, to hold our outer clothing and bedding, and those did double duty as very convenient little work-tables and dinner-tables.

Being a true daughter of New England, I need hardly say that embroidery and knitting-work came along with me. Pencils and books needed no saying at all.

The greater part of our furniture, and all the housekeeping goods we had laid in at New York and Detroit, were to come up behind us in another boat, in the charge of men whose professed trade was the bringing of cargo safely up the rapids as far as Fort Winnebago. It is an undertaking of some three weeks and an immense amount of labor, and the owner of the goods may think himself a lucky man to receive them at the end of it at all, in whatever wet, broken and dilapidated state they reach him. That was why the things we valued most travelled with us, and why we were willing to be a little crowded for the sake of it.

Until that morning I had never in my life seen a gentleman in a shirt of any color at all. A white collar and bosom, spotless, belonged among the "notions" which "Boston," and New England "folks" after it, entertained on the subject of a gentleman's toilette. Mrs. Cass had warned me, laughing, that a traveling equipment wanted calico shirts every bit as much as it wanted patch-work pillow-cases; and sure enough, some tidy little Frenchwoman had been at our divan-pillows with taste and skill both, and turned every one of them out in garb of the brightest and most variegated kind.

The Judge and my husband went gay in the deepest blue and the deepest pink. Each had by him besides a cap of bright red, the voyageurs' bonnet rouge, or tuque, and in deference to the lady aboard it was to come out only when a nap in the afternoon became unavoidable — after a hearty dinner, or a dull book, or too long a stretch without exercise.

The Judge was an admirable man to travel with. He had spent years in that country; he had gone with General Cass on the expedition that reached the head-waters of the Mississippi; and about the early times, the customs and the people he carried a vast fund of anecdote.

Two of his accounts of the way justice used to be administered in those days have stayed with me.

At the Bay there had been an old Frenchman named Réaume, excessively ignorant and grasping with it, though otherwise tolerably good-natured. This was the man they made justice of the peace. Two men came before him once, the one complaining, the other defending himself. He heard the complaint out with patience, and he heard the defense out, and then he stood up with great dignity and delivered himself of a judgment.

"You are both wrong. You, Bois-vert," to the plaintiff, "you bring me one load of hay, and you, Crély," to the defendant, "you bring me one load of wood, and now the matter is settled." So far as anyone ever heard, no exception was taken to that verdict.

The one story brought on another, and the second was set on the Mississippi, at Prairie du Chien.

A Frenchman there, another justice of the peace, went by the name of "Old Boilvin" with everybody. His office stood just beyond the fort walls, and the officers made quite a fashion of lounging in there in the morning, to get some sport out of an idle hour and to drink with the old gentleman a glass of brandy-and-water, which he called "taking a little quelque-chose."

A soldier named Fry stood accused of stealing a calf belonging to M. Rolette and killing it, and the constable — a bricklayer by the name of Bell — had been sent to arrest him and bring him in for trial.

The gentlemen were paying their customary morning visit to the justice when a noise started up in the entry, and then somebody knocked.

"Come in," cried Old Boilvin, getting up and crossing to the door himself.

Bell. - "Here, sir, I have brought Fry to you, as you ordered."

Justice. - "Fry, you great rascal! What for you kill M. Rolette's calf?"

Fry. - "I didn't kill M. Rolette's calf."

Justice, shaking his fist. - "You lie, you great - - rascal! Bell, take him to jail. Come, gentlemen, come, let us take a leetle quelque-chose."

The Canadian boatmen sing the whole time they are rowing or paddling, and nothing heartens them like having the "bourgeois" lead off the singing himself. Should the passengers join in on the refrain, and the ladies above all, the compliment counts for more still.

Their songs run light and cheerful. Most of them carry some small satire or witticism in them, built to bring on a spirited chorus, and now and then an uproarious one.

Verse and refrain go somewhat like this:

BOURGEOIS. - Par-derrière chez ma tante, Par-derrière chez ma tante.

CHORUS. - Par-derrière chez ma tante, Par-derrière chez ma tante.

BOURGEOIS. - Il y a un coq qui chante, Des pommes, des poires, des raves, des choux, Des figues nouvelles, des raisins doux.

CHORUS. - Des pommes, des poires, des raves, des choux, Des figues nouvelles, des raisins doux.

BOURGEOIS. - Il y a un coq qui chante, Il y a un coq qui chante.

CHORUS. - Il y a un coq qui chante, etc.

BOURGEOIS. - Demande une femme à prendre, Des pommes, des poires, des raves, des choux, etc.

CHORUS. - Des pommes, dos poires, etc.

BOURGEOIS. - Demande une femme à prendre, Demande une femme à, etc.

It runs on in that fashion, the counsel handed down one piece at a time — "Ne prenez pas une noire, Car elles aiment trop à boire," "Ne prenez pas une rousse, Car elles sont trop jalouses."

And when every last qualification has been named and objected to, drawn out beyond all reckoning by the endless repetition of the chorus, the bourgeois lifts his voice and calls out - "Whoop la! à terre, à terre - pour la pipe!"

The voyageurs stop every five or six miles without fail, to rest and to smoke, and that used to be the measure of distance in the country — not so many miles, but so many pipes.

Some of the Canadian melodies are very beautiful, and I can imagine no more exhilarating way of travelling than over those waters, in the midst of all that wild magnificence, with the measured stroke of the oars keeping time to "Le Rosier Blanc," "En roulant ma Boule," or "Lève ton pied, ma jolie Bergère."

The height of the fun, though, was a comic piece which they sang over and over and which somehow never went stale. It went about like this:

BOURGEOIS. - Michaud est monté dans un prunier, Pour treiller des prunes.

La branche a cassé - CHORUS. - Michaud a tombé?

BOURGEOIS. - Ou est-ce qu'il est?

CHORUS. - Il est en bas.

BOURGEOIS. - Oh! reveille, reveille, reveille, Oh! reveille, Michaud est en haut! Etiquette required that everyone look astonished at Michaud's luck in keeping his place in the tree after the branch had broken under him, and then the joke had to be worked through every variety of fruit-tree that Michaud might be supposed capable of climbing.
