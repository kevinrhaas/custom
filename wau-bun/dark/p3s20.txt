There was one among them that year I had never laid eyes on before — the mother of the elder Day-kau-ray. Nobody could tell you her age. Everyone agreed she had seen upwards of a hundred winters. Her eyes had gone dim and nearly white. Her face was dark and shrunken in on itself like a baked apple. Her voice shook and had almost nothing left in it, except when she raised it in fury at her graceless grandsons, who liked to play every kind of trick on her. All of it said the same thing about how long she had been alive.

She went about on all fours, having no strength left to hold herself upright. On the day of the payment, once she had her portion and had hidden it away in a corner of her blanket, she came crawling along and settled herself on the door-step to count what she had.

My sister and I were watching her from the open window.

She had just spread the silver out in front of her, unobserved, as she believed, when two of her descendants came down on her out of nowhere. At first they seemed to be begging a share of it. She drove them off with angry gestures. Then one of them made a fast swoop with his hand and came away with a fistful.

She tried to get up and go after him and could do nothing but clutch what was left and let out screams of rage that did not sound like anything human. Just then the boys happened to look up and see us watching them. They burst out laughing, and with a sort of mocking flourish they threw the half-dollars back to her and ran off to the pay-ground.

For all their tormenting she was extremely fond of them, and she never came without begging something of her Father that she could turn around and give to them.

She crept into the parlor one morning, then hauled herself straight up by the frame of the door and cried, in the most pitiful voice, "Shaw-nee-aw-kee! Wau-tshob-ee-rah Thsoonsh-koo-nee-noh!" (Silver-man, I have no looking-glass.) My husband, smiling, picked up the same small tone and called back, "Do you wish to look at yourself, mother?"

She found this so irresistibly funny that she laughed until she had to sit herself down on the floor and give herself over to it. Then she admitted the mirror was for one of the boys. When her Father had handed it over, she discovered that she had no comb. Then that she had no knife. Then that she had no calico shawl — until it finished the way it always finished, with Shaw-nee-aw-kee paying pretty dearly for his joke.
