Every tie poor Agathe had was broken but the one that held her to her infant. She never went back to her father's lodge. She had been deserted, and she felt that being deserted she was dishonored. All the ambition she had left was to bring the child up as the whites brought up theirs. She dressed it in the costume of the French children, a frock of bright calico and a cap of the same stuff trimmed with narrow black lace. It was a fine child. The only time I ever saw a smile cross that woman's face was when somebody in our family praised it or took it up.

Even that, the one source of happiness she had, Agathe was required to give up. While we were away at Green Bay, with the Sauks in the neighborhood, the child was taken violently ill. Paquette's house, which was the mother's home, was full of Indians coming and going, and the noise and disturbance in it were constant. My husband had a place made ready for her under our own roof where it would be quiet and where the post physician could attend her. It was all no use. Nothing could save the little creature's life. They told me afterward about the mother's agony as she hung over the only thing she had on the earth, and I am told there was no bearing to look at it. When they took the child from her at last, it seemed almost more than nature could carry. There were friends about her, not of her nation or her color, who tried to comfort her. Did the father ever send so much as a thought or an inquiry after what became of his child, or after the young creature whose life he had made dark and desolate? We will hope that he did. That he repented, and asked pardon from above for the evil he had worked.

Agathe had been baptized by M. Mazzuchelli. It may be that she had gathered some religious knowledge out of it that could console her in her sorrow, and make up to her for hopes and joys blasted so early.

Some months after the child's death she came, with several of the half-breed women of the neighborhood, to pay me a visit of respect and congratulation on the arrival of the young Shaw-nee-aw-kee. She looked at her "little brother," as they were calling him. She took his soft, tiny hand inside her own. The tears stood in her eyes, and she said a few small words of tenderness that showed how full her heart was. I could hardly keep from mingling my tears with hers, thinking on the whole sum of sorrow and desolation that one man's selfishness had brought about.
