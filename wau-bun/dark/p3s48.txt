Everything that had held poor Agathe to the world had been cut away, except the baby. She would not go back to her father's lodge. She had been abandoned, and to her mind that made her a dishonored woman. What was left of her was poured into one purpose: the child would be raised as a white child. She dressed it as the French children were dressed — a frock of bright calico and a cap made of the same stuff, edged with a narrow black lace. It was a beautiful baby. In all the time I knew her, the only smile I ever saw come to Agathe's face came when somebody in our family took the child up and praised it.

That was taken from her too. We were away at Green Bay, and the Sauks were in the country then, and the child fell violently ill. Her home was the house at Paquette's, and the place was full of Indians coming and going, noise at all hours, no rest to be had in it. My husband had a room made ready for her in our own house, where it was quiet and where the post physician could attend her. None of it made any difference. The little creature could not be saved. Those who were there told me afterward what the mother was like, bent over the one thing on earth that belonged to her, and they said it was past bearing to watch. When they had to take the child out of her arms, it seemed more than a human being could survive. There were friends around her then, none of them her nation or her color, doing what they could. And the father — did he ever once spare a thought, or send so much as a question, about what became of his child, or about the girl whose whole life he had turned dark and empty? Let us hope that he did. Let us hope he repented, and asked pardon from above for the evil he had done.

M. Mazzuchelli had baptized her. It may be that something of what she learned then stayed with her, and gave her some comfort in her grief, and stood in some small part for everything that had been blighted in her so early.

Months after the child died she came to see me, with several of the half-breed women of the neighborhood, to pay her respects and to offer her congratulations on the arrival of the young Shaw-nee-aw-kee. She looked down at her "little brother," as they all called him, and she took his soft, tiny hand into her own, and her eyes filled, and she said a few small tender words to him that told me plainly how full her heart was. I very nearly wept along with her — thinking of all that ruin and grief, and that it was the work of one man's selfishness.
