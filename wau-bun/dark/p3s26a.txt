We crossed the river at the place that is now Depere and rode into the wild, unsettled country on the west bank, and there we found a succession of wooded hills divided from one another by ravines so narrow and so steep that it seemed impossible any animal but a mule or a goat could make its way among them.

Wish-tay-yun took the lead. The horse he rode knew the country and was well trained to that style of road. As for Charlie, he was perfectly admirable. When he came to a precipitous descent he would set his forefeet out ahead of him and slide down on his haunches in the most scientific manner, while my only mode of preserving my balance was to hold fast by the bridle and lay myself braced almost flat along his back. Then our position would change all at once and we would be scaling the opposite bank, at the imminent risk of falling backward into the ravine below.

It was amusing to watch Wish-tay-yun as he scrambled on ahead, turning half round now and then to see how I was faring. And when at last I came up over the summit, panting and laughing, he would throw up both hands and shout, with the utmost glee, "Mamma Manitou!" My mother is a spirit.

Our old acquaintances the Grignons seemed much surprised that I should have ventured on such a trip. They had never undertaken it themselves, although they had lived so long at the Kakalin; but then there was no reason why they should have. They could always command a canoe or a boat whenever they wished to visit the Bay.

As we had anticipated, our gentlemen joined us at supper. They had delayed to take dinner with Colonel Stambaugh. They had had a delightful gallop up from the Bay. They had seen no ravines, nor anything but fine smooth roads. They might have been asleep, but if so they were not conscious of it. That was the account they gave of themselves, to our no small amusement.
