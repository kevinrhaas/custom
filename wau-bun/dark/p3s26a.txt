We crossed the river where Depere stands now and rode into the wild country on the west side, where nobody had settled anything. What we found was hill after wooded hill, and between each of them a ravine so narrow and so steep that I could not see how any creature except a mule or a goat would ever get through.

Wish-tay-yun went first. His horse knew this country and had been schooled for roads of exactly this kind. Charlie, I have to say, was admirable. Whenever the ground dropped away in front of him he would plant his forefeet out ahead and go down on his haunches, sliding, entirely scientific about it, while I kept my seat by the only method available to me: a hard grip on the bridle and my whole body laid back and braced nearly flat along his spine. Then, with no warning, everything reversed. We would be climbing the far wall instead, and the danger now was going over backward into the ravine underneath us.

There was comedy in it. Wish-tay-yun would scramble on up ahead and keep turning half around to see how I was faring behind him. And when I came over the top at last, out of breath and laughing, he would throw both hands into the air and shout, in tremendous glee, "Mamma Manitou!" (My mother is a spirit.)

The Grignons, whom we had known a long while, could hardly believe I had attempted such a trip. They had never once attempted it themselves in all their years at the Kakalin — though there had never been any call for them to. If they wanted to see "the Bay," they had only to take a canoe or a boat.

Our gentlemen turned up at supper, exactly as we had expected. "They had delayed to take dinner with Colonel Stambaugh - had had a delightful gallop up from: the Bay - had seen no ravines, nor anything but fine smooth roads - might have been asleep, but, if so, weren't conscious of it." That was their whole account of themselves, and it entertained us a good deal.
