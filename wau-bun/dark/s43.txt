Our Indian guide came to us early and took us carefully out of the timber. As we went he pointed out bee-trees, one after another; that grove was famous for them, he said. Then he set off at a long trot and held it, and a little after nine he brought us out at Piché's — a log cabin on a rise, its face turned east over the broad prairie. We had counted on food and drink there. Some of the party knew Piché of old. The door opened on a room full of people, and not one of them was him. Travelers, a few. Indians, a great many.

Nothing there tempted us to stay except the fire, a bright one burning in the clay chimney, and the chance to warm ourselves at it. A man in Quaker dress came forward to answer our questions. He was bound for Chicago himself, he said, and would take us there. So we thanked our Indian friend and paid him well for all the trouble he had so kindly taken on our account, and he went back the way he had come.

From Piché's the prairie ran unbroken to the Du Page. Between the two forks of that river, our new acquaintance — Mr. Dogherty — told us we would find the house of a Mr. Hawley, and at it a comfortable dinner.

The cold that day was past anything I have words for. The wind came over that open ground with nothing standing in its way, and it went through to the heart of every one of us. When my feet went numb I beat them against the saddle to bring the blood back, and I kept beating until they were too bruised to beat any longer. Nothing offered itself. Not a house, not a wigwam, not so much as a clump of trees to put at our backs, mile after weary mile. Then the west fork of the Du Page, frozen — but not hard enough to bear the horses. There was nothing for it but to cut them a road. It took time. The ice had thickened to several inches in that one bitter night. Plante went ahead with the axe and cut as far as his arms would reach, then mounted one of the hardy little ponies and broke the ice in front of him until he had opened a passage clear to the opposite shore.

The horses shook as they were reined in among the floating pieces, and it was a pitiful thing to watch. Those of us waiting on the bank in that wind were in no position to pity anybody. Brunet must have arrived at the same conclusion, because he did what Brunet always did and went in directly behind Plante, out of his turn, and stood quaking at his heels, turning his head now and again to look back at the rest of us, as much as to say, I've got ahead of you, this time. We were all over at last. We put the spurs to them and did not slow until we came up to Hawley's — a large, roomy house near the east fork of the river.

The good woman of the place took us in and soon had us warm and comfortable, and it was like stepping back into a settled country. She began on dinner at once. We watched every motion of it, eager-eyed. She reached a huge ham down from the rafters and cut innumerable slices off it; she broke a dozen and more fine fresh eggs into a pan, ready for frying; she mixed a johnny-cake and propped it against a board in front of the fire to bake. With that bright fire going the business still took an unconscionable while. It was done at last, and she might well stare at the travelers' appetites we had carried in with us. She had no way of knowing what short commons we had been on for two days.

We asked, and learned that if we pushed on we could make Lawton's, on the Aux Plaines, that same night — and that would put us within twelve miles of Chicago. We did not waste a minute we did not have to waste. We were in the saddle again as soon after dinner as it could be managed.

The east fork was worse than the west had been. Something had already broken the ice — the current, or riders ahead of us who had cut their own way through — and it was coming down in large cakes. The horses had to make a rush of it. The water was high and running in a torrent, and if I had not been up on Jerry, the tallest horse in the cavalcade, I would have come out of it splashed terribly.

As it was I was well frightened, and I took hold of bridle and mane together and did not let go of either. After that we traveled as fast as the ground allowed, because we meant to reach our destination before dark.

Mr. Dogherty, a tall man, bolt upright, half Quaker and half Methodist, spent those miles doing his best to entertain me. He laid out a thorough schedule of his religious opinions, together with the Scripture on which each of them rested. He was a good deal of a perfectionist, and it was plain that he regarded himself with no small satisfaction as a living illustration of his favorite doctrine.

"St. John says," — this was the style of it — "St. John says, 'He that is born of God, doth not commit sin.' Now, if I am born of God, I don't commit sin."

I was too cold and too weary to take up the point, so I let him have the whole of it his own way. I believe he must have thought me rather a dull companion. He gave me the credit, at least, of being a good listener.
