Detroit had surrendered the day after the massacre at Chicago, and one of General Hull's stipulations in the surrender was that the inhabitants should be left undisturbed in their homes. So the family of Mr. Kinzie took up their quarters with friends in the old mansion — the one many people will still remember standing on the northwest corner of Jefferson Avenue and Wayne Street.

They had a window on the winter that followed, and it worked on them. Almost every day there was something new to watch: American prisoners brought in by their Indian captors and used for the amusement of the town. Men who could barely drag their wounded, bleeding feet over the frozen ground were made to dance. Some of these exhibitions took place directly in front of the Government House, where Colonel McKee lived. British officers watched from their windows while it went on. For the honor of humanity we will hope that such instances were rare.

Everything that could be turned into money or trade among the citizens' effects was offered, to buy their countrymen out of those hands. Then came the prisoners from the River Raisin — the men who, after surrendering to General Proctor, were handed to his savage allies to be tortured and murdered by inches — and the whole community moved at once. Private houses became hospitals. Everybody wanted to get hold of as many of the survivors as they could. The ladies of Detroit stood at their doors and windows watching the miserable creatures carried past for sale, and bartered away the very clothes they owned to get them.

In Mr. Kinzie's house one large room was given over to the sufferers. Few of them lived. The two people spoken of afterward with the deepest feeling were young gentlemen from Kentucky, brothers, both badly wounded, both wounds made mortal by the treatment and hardship that followed. Each of them was frightened only for the other. What they showed of that tender fraternal affection, in a hundred small ways, left an impression on that house that nobody in it ever lost.

The last bargain of the winter was made by black Jim and one of the children, who were given leave to redeem a negro servant of the gallant Colonel Allen. The price was an old white horse. It was the only article of any value left in their possession.

A brother of Colonel Allen came to Detroit afterward, and the negro chose to go back into servitude rather than stay on as a stranger in a strange land.
