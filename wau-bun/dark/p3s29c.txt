"Tshah-ko-zhah?" — What is it?

It was Hoo-wau-ne-kah, the Little Elk. He spoke fast, and there was great agitation in it. I could not follow him. I lay there shaking, waiting for somebody to turn it into English for me, and dreading it. Every so often a word came through that I did know. Sau-kee. Shoonk-hat-tay-rah, horse. Neither of them was any comfort.

The trouble, as I learned soon enough, was this. A fresh trail had been found near the Petit Rocher, on the Wisconsin, and the people at the villages on the Barribault were badly frightened, afraid it might be the Sauks. By the look of it something like a hundred horses or more had gone by that way. Hoo-wau-ne-kah had been sent off at once to tell their Father and ask what he made of it.

His Father heard him out and then told him the trail was almost certainly General Henry's troops, who were said to have come north hunting for the enemy. The marks of the hoofs, by his own account of them, showed the horses had been shod. That settled it. No Sauk trail would look like that. The people at the villages had nothing to be uneasy about.

"Very well, Father," said Hoo-wau-ne-kah. "I will go back and tell my people what you say. They will believe you, for you always tell them the truth. You aren't like us Indians, who sometimes deceive each other." And he went back to his friends much easier in his mind than he had come.

The picketing and the other defences were finished about then, and a detachment of troops came up from Fort Howard under Lieutenant Hunter, which between them made the fort the safest place on the Portage. So every evening, just before dusk, we formed up and marched across to the other side of the river, to the quarters that had been given us inside the garrison, and left our own house and everything in it to the care of the Frenchmen and our friends the Winnebagoes.
