It was after that last piece of news that the appointed council with the Winnebagoes came due, to be held at the Four Lakes, thirty-five miles from Fort Winnebago.

We pleaded with him. We argued. None of it moved him an inch. "It was his duty to assemble his people and talk to them," my husband said, "and he must run the risk, if there were any. He had perfect confidence in the Winnebagoes. The enemy, by all he could learn, were now far distant from the Four Lakes — probably at Kosh-ko-nong. He would set off early in the morning with Paquette, hold his council, and return to us the same evening."

I will not try to describe that day. It was long and it was gray and there was nothing in it to do but wait through it. By nightfall we had come to the state where a drunken Indian calling out somewhere in the dark, or even a dog barking, went through us like a blade.

We sat up late at the open window, listening to everything. And at last, out of all that listening, came the sound of horses. We knew the tread of Griffin and Jerry coming up the hill, and then a cheerful shout came up ahead of them, which meant that all was well. He and his interpreter had ridden seventy miles that day, and held a long talk with the Indians in the middle of it.

The Winnebagoes in council had promised to do everything in their power to keep peace and good order among their young men. They told their Father that the bands on the Rock River, Win-no-sheek's excepted, were all determined to stay friendly and keep clear of the Sauks — and that to prove it they were giving up their villages and their corn-fields and moving north, so that their Great Father the President should have no cause to be dissatisfied with them. About Win-no-sheek and his people they said they could not answer.

Time went on and brought more of the same. The murders of Auberry, Green, and Force at Blue Mound. The attack on Apple Fort. That last came to us from old Crély, the father of Mrs. Paquette, who rode express from Galena, and who swore he had passed within arm's reach of a bush with Sauks lying behind it — but his horse caught the smell of the sweet-scented grass they always wear on a war-party, and set off at a gallop that did not stop until it reached the Portage.

Another man who brought us news was a young gentleman named Follett. He had spent so many days watching for the enemy that his eyes had come out and set, and it was a long while after he reached safety before they went back into their usual place and expression.
