Somewhere in the middle of that spring the Rev. Mr. Kent and Mrs. Kent came up from Galena to see us. I mark the visit because of what it was: the first time the gospel was preached at Fort Winnebago according to the Protestant faith. They cleared the large parlor of the hospital and made it fit for a service, and we said it to one another with real gladness — "Let us go to the house of the Lord!"

We had been living in that place close on three years with no public service of praise and thanksgiving to go to. This looked to us like the beginning of better times, and our little "sewing-society" bent to its work harder than before, building up a fund that might one day be enough to keep a missionary here for good.

Not long afterward, on a fine spring morning, we were sitting at breakfast when a party of Indians came into the parlor and up to the door of the room where we were. Two of them walked on through and out onto the small portico. The third did not follow them. He stopped in the doorway where he had first shown himself, and stayed there. He was standing nearly opposite me. I lifted my eyes to him, and the clothes were not the clothes I had seen before, and the paint was laid over his face and body, and I knew him anyway, at once.

I went on pouring the coffee, and while I poured I said to my husband, "The one behind you, with whom you are speaking, is one of the escaped prisoners."

Mr. Kinzie did not turn his head. He went on taking in every direction they had to give him about the guns and traps and the rest of it that they meant to leave with the blacksmith for mending. Then, while they were still talking, he turned toward the parlor door as though it meant nothing at all and answered the man who was speaking to him. When he spoke to me again, this is what he said. "You are right, but it is no affair of ours. We are none of us to look so as to give him notice that we suspect anything. They are undoubtedly innocent, and have suffered enough already."

Their Father did what he never did: he asked none of them their names. He wrote out their directions, tied each paper to the implement it belonged with, and told them to carry them to M. Morrin and deliver them themselves.

The rest of our circle were delighted by the young fellow's audacity, and we very much wanted to tell the officers that if we had chosen to, we could have handed them one of their fugitives.
