Among so many painful events there were now and then some that came very near the ludicrous.

One evening, sitting at tea, we heard guns firing off in the direction of the Wisconsin. Everyone was on their feet at once, making instinctively for the garrison. We came out of the house into a bluff and a meadow already in full commotion — Indians running toward the sound with their guns and spears laid across their shoulders, squaws and children standing out in front of their lodges and staring anxiously toward the noise that nobody could account for, and knots of French and half-breeds doing exactly what we were doing, running for the bridge to get themselves inside the pickets that had been put up so lately.

One company of Indians came past us at speed, and a weapon carried carelessly caught one of our party on the side of the head. "Oh!" she shrieked, "I am killed! an Indian has tomahawked me!" She was reassured only by discovering that she could still run as fast as the best of us.

When we were inside the Fort and standing on the parade-ground we could not help laughing at the figures we cut. Some of us had come without hats or shawls. Others had bundles of valuables snatched up in the moment of leaving. One was still holding her piece of bread-and-butter, not having had the presence of mind to put it down before she took to flight.

The alarm turned out to have come from a party of Winnebagoes out of one of the Barribault villages. They were about to leave their home for a time, and were going through the ceremony of burying the scalps that they and their fathers had taken.

Like the military funerals of civilized nations, their solemnities closed with the discharge of several volleys over the grave of their trophies.
