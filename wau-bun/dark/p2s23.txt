Mrs. Burns and her infant became the prisoners of a chief who carried them to his village and treated them with great kindness. His wife did not. She saw the favor shown to "the white woman" and her child, and she hated them for it, and she made it plain every day. Once she swung a tomahawk at the baby. She missed — barely — of putting an end to it altogether. After that demonstration they were not left long in the old woman's power; at the first opportunity they were carried away to a place of safety.

The Lees had lived in a house on the lake shore, not far from the fort. Mr. Lee owned Lee's Place and farmed it. It was his son who ran down to the fort with the discharged soldier on the afternoon of the 7th of April, shouting "Indians." On the 15th of August the father, the son, and every other member of that family were killed, except Mrs. Lee and her young infant. Black Partridge claimed those two and took them to his village on the Au Sable. There had been another child, a girl of about twelve, whom he had been particularly fond of. She had been put on horseback for the march, and because she was not used to riding they had tied her fast to the saddle, so that she could not slip off or be thrown.

She was inside the range of the balls when the firing started, and she was badly hit. The horse bolted. The fall half threw her, but the bands held, and she hung down along its side, swinging, while the animal ran in circles about the field. That was how Black Partridge found her. He caught the horse and got her loose from the saddle. She was hurt past any recovering, and she was in great agony, and he finished it there with his tomahawk. He said afterward that it was the hardest thing he ever tried to do. He said he did it because he could not stand to watch her suffer.

He took the mother and the baby home to his village, and he came to care for her a great deal — enough that he wanted to marry her. She naturally objected. He did not press it. He treated her with the greatest respect and consideration instead, and he was in no hurry at all to release her, because he still hoped she would change her mind. That winter the child fell ill. Nothing they had for it did any good. So Black Partridge proposed to carry the little one down to Chicago, where a French trader was now living in Mr. Kinzie's house, and get some medical help from him. He wrapped his charge up with the greatest care and set out.

He reached M. Du Pin's, went into the room where the trader was, and laid his bundle down on the floor, carefully.

"What have you there?" asked M. Du Pin.

"A young raccoon, which I have brought you as a present," he said, and opened the pack, and there was the sick infant.

The trader prescribed for the child's complaint. Then, as Black Partridge was preparing to start home, the chief told him about the offer he had made to Mrs. Lee, and how she had answered it.

M. Du Pin had a few private doubts as to how long a man's honorable resolution to leave the question with the lady herself would hold. He opened a negotiation for her ransom on the spot, and worked so effectually on the good feelings of Black Partridge that the chief agreed to bring his fair prisoner to Chicago at once, so that she could be restored to her friends.

Whether the kind trader had anything in his mind at the outset beyond sympathy and brotherly kindness, we cannot say. We only know that in the course of time Mrs. Lee became Madame Du Pin, and that the worthy couple lived together in great happiness for many years afterward.

The fate of Nau-non-gee, one of the chiefs of the Calumet village, mentioned earlier in this narrative, deserves to be recorded.

During the battle of the 15th of August the man he went after, above all others, was one Sergeant Hays — a man from whom he had received many acts of kindness.

Hays had already taken a ball through the body when the chief ran up to tomahawk him. The sergeant gathered what strength he had left and put his bayonet through him. They went down together. Other Indians came up and soon finished Hays, and only then was his bayonet drawn out of the body of his adversary.

The wounded chief was carried after the battle to his village on the Calumet, and he lasted several days there. When he felt the end coming he called his young men together and charged them, in the most solemn terms he had, to see to the safety of the prisoners after his death and to take no life among them out of respect for his memory — since he had deserved what he got, at the hands of people whose kindness he had repaid so badly.
