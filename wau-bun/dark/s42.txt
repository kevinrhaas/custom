We followed the old squaw to her lodge, which stood at no great distance in the woods. I had never before been inside an Indian lodge, though I had sometimes peeped into one of the many that were always clustered round the house of the Interpreter at the Portage.

This one was very nicely arranged. Four sticks of wood laid in a square in the center answered the purpose of a hearth, and the fire was built inside them, with the smoke escaping through an opening in the top. The mats the lodge was made of were neat and new. Against the sides, hanging from the poles of the frame-work, were various bags of Indian manufacture holding their dried food and their other household treasures. Sundry ladles, small kettles and wooden bowls hung from the cross-poles, and dangling in the center, from an iron chain, was a large kettle in which some dark, suspicious-looking substance was seething over the scanty fire. On the floor, between the fire and the outer wall, mats were spread, and there my husband invited me to sit down and make myself comfortable.

The first demand of an Indian on meeting a white man is for bread, of which they are exceedingly fond, and I knew enough of the Pottowattamie language to understand the timid "pe-qua-zhe-gun choh-kay-go" — I have no bread — with which the squaw began our talk after my husband had left the lodge.

I shook my head and tried to convey to her that, so far from being able to give her any, I had had no breakfast myself. She understood me. She produced a bowl at once, ladled into it a quantity of the Indian potatoes from the kettle over the fire, and set it in front of me. I was too hungry to be fastidious, and, owing in part, no doubt, to the sharpness of my appetite, I found them really delicious.

Two little girls who lived in the lodge sat gazing at me with evident admiration and astonishment, which increased when I took my little Prayer book out of my pocket and began to read. They had undoubtedly never seen a book before. It amused me to watch the care with which they looked away from me while they questioned their mother about my strange employment and listened to her replies.

While I was so occupied, I was startled by a sudden "hogh!" The mat over the entrance was raised, and an Indian came in with that graceful bound peculiar to themselves. It was the master of the lodge, back from shooting ducks. He was a tall, finely-formed man with a cheerful, open face, and he listened to what his wife told him in a quiet tone while he divested himself of his accoutrements, in the most unembarrassed, well-bred manner imaginable.

Soon my husband joined us. He had been seeing to the comfort of his horses and helping his men make their fire and pitch the tent, which the rising storm was making a matter of some difficulty.

From the Indian he learned that we were in what was called the Big Woods, or Piché's Grove, after a Frenchman of that name who lived not far from the spot; that the river we had crossed was the Fox River; and that he could guide us to Piché's, from which the road was perfectly plain, or take us all the way into Chicago if we preferred. But we had better stay encamped for that day, he said, because there was a storm coming on. In the mean time he would go and shoot some ducks for our dinner and our supper. He was furnished accordingly with powder and shot, and set off again for game without delay.

I had put a roll of scarlet ribbon in my pocket when we left home, in case a stout string should be wanted, and I drew it out now and, with the knife that hung round my neck, cut off a couple of yards for each of the little girls. They received it with great delight. Their mother divided each portion in two and tied a piece to each of the little clubs into which their hair was knotted at the temples. They laughed and exclaimed "Saum!" as they looked at each other, and their mother joined in their mirth, though a little unwilling, I thought, to display her maternal exultation before a stranger.

The tent being all in order, my husband came for me, and we took leave of our friends in the wigwam with grateful hearts.

The storm was raging outside. The trees were bending and cracking around us, and the air was completely filled with wild-fowl, screaming and quacking as they drove south before the wind. Our tent stood among the trees not far from the river. My husband took me to the bank to look for a moment at what we had escaped. The wind was coming down from the north in a perfect hurricane. The water was filled with masses of snow and ice dancing along on the torrent, and over the torrent hurried thousands of wild-fowl, making the woods resound with their deafening clamor.

Had we been one hour later, we could not possibly have crossed that stream, and there would have been nothing for us but to stay on the other bank and starve in the wilderness. Could we be sufficiently grateful to the kind Providence that had brought us safely through such dangers?

The men had cut down an immense tree and built the fire against it, but the wind shifted so constantly that every five minutes the tent filled completely with smoke and I was driven into the open air to breathe. Then I would seat myself on one end of the huge log, as near the fire as I could get, because it was dismally cold. The wind seemed to be actuated by a kind of caprice: in whatever direction I took my seat, just that way came the smoke and the hot ashes, puffing into my face until I was nearly blinded. Neither veil nor silk handkerchief afforded any effectual protection. I was glad when our huntsmen arrived with a quantity of ducks and gave me an opportunity of diverting my thoughts from my own sufferings by helping the men pick them and get them ready for our meal.

We borrowed a kettle from our Indian friends. It was not remarkably clean, so we heated a little water in it and prairie-hayed it out before consigning our birds to it, and with a bowl of Indian potatoes, a present from our kind neighbors, we soon had an excellent soup.

What with the cold, the smoke, and the driving ashes and cinders, that was the most uncomfortable afternoon I had yet passed, and I was glad when night came and I could creep into the tent and cover myself up in the blankets, out of the way of all three of these evils.

The storm raged with tenfold violence during the night. We were startled again and again by the crash of falling trees around us, and who could tell but that the next one would come down on us? In spite of our fatigue, we passed an almost sleepless night. When we got up in the morning we were made fully alive to the perils we had been lying in the middle of. At least fifty trees, the giants of the forest, lay prostrate within view of the tent.

When we had taken our scanty breakfast and were mounted and ready for departure, we could hardly thread our way out, so completely was it obstructed by the fallen trunks.
