The old squaw led and we went after her, and her lodge stood only a little way back among the trees. I had never once been inside an Indian lodge. I had looked into them — there are always a cluster of them standing around the Interpreter's house at the Portage, and I had peeped in at those — but I had never gone through a door.

Inside, everything had its place. Four sticks of wood laid in a square in the middle of the floor served for a hearth, and the fire burned inside that square, and the smoke went up and out through the opening at the top. The mats that made the walls were new and neatly done. Bags of their own manufacture hung against the sides from the poles of the frame, holding dried food and whatever else the family counted as treasure, and from the cross-poles hung ladles and small kettles and wooden bowls. In the center, swinging from an iron chain, a large kettle hung over a thin fire, and in it something dark was seething that I did not care to look at twice. Mats were spread on the floor between the fire and the outer wall, and my husband told me to sit down there and make myself comfortable.

When an Indian meets a white person the first thing asked for is bread. They are exceedingly fond of it. So I was not surprised, after my husband had gone out of the lodge, when the woman opened our talk with a timid "pe-qua-zhe-gun choh-kay-go" — I have no bread.

I shook my head. I tried to make her understand that giving was beyond me, that I had not so much as had my own breakfast. She got my meaning at once. She took down a bowl and ladled it full of Indian potatoes out of the kettle on the fire and put it in front of me. I was in no condition to be fastidious. My appetite had a good deal to do with it, I am sure — but I thought them delicious.

Two little girls belonged to the lodge, and they sat and stared at me with an admiration and an astonishment that only grew when I took my little Prayer book out of my pocket and began to read. They had certainly never seen a book in their lives. What amused me was their manners: they kept their faces carefully turned away from me while they asked their mother what I could possibly be doing, and listened to what she answered.

I was reading when a sound came from the doorway — a sudden "hogh!" — and the mat that hung across the entrance lifted and a man came in with that springing, graceful bound that belongs only to them. The master of the lodge. He had been out after ducks and was just back. He was tall and finely made, with an open, cheerful face, and while his wife told him quietly who I was he was taking off his accoutrements, as unembarrassed and as well-bred as any gentleman I ever watched receive news of a guest.

My husband came in not long after. He had been seeing to the horses, and helping the men get their fire going and their tent up, which the rising wind was making no easy work.

From the Indian he had learned where we were. This was the Big Woods, or Piché's Grove, called so for a Frenchman of that name who lived not far off. The river we had crossed was the Fox River. He would guide us to Piché's, and from there the road ran perfectly plain, or he would take us the whole way into Chicago if we liked that better — but his advice was that we stay camped where we were for the day, because there was a storm coming. In the meantime he would go and shoot ducks for our dinner and our supper. We gave him powder and shot, and he went straight back out after the game.

I had a roll of scarlet ribbon in my pocket. I had put it there when we left home, thinking a stout string might be wanted for something. I took it out now and cut two yards for each of the little girls with the knife that hung at my neck. They were delighted. Their mother took each length and divided it in half and tied a piece to each of the small clubs their hair was knotted into at the temples. Then the two of them looked at each other and laughed and cried "Saum!" and their mother laughed with them — though she seemed to me to be holding some of it back, not quite willing to show a stranger how proud she was of them.

When the tent was in order my husband came to fetch me, and we said our thanks to our friends in the wigwam and went out.

The storm had arrived. The trees around us were bending and cracking, and the whole air was full of wild-fowl going south ahead of the blast, screaming and quacking as they came. Our tent stood among the trees a short way from the water. My husband walked me down to the bank so that I could see, for one minute, what we had gotten out of. The wind came down out of the north in a perfect hurricane. The river ran loaded with masses of snow and ice, dancing along on the torrent, and above the torrent went thousands of birds, and the woods rang with the noise of them until you could hardly think.

One hour later and the crossing would have been impossible. There would have been nothing left for us to do but stop on the far bank and starve there in the wilderness. I do not know how any of us could be grateful enough to the kind Providence that had carried us through such dangers.

The men had felled an immense tree and built the fire up against the trunk of it. But the wind would not settle. Every five minutes it swung, and the tent filled with smoke, and I had to get out into the open just to breathe. Then I would go and sit on one end of that huge log, as close to the flames as I could manage, because the cold was dismal — and the wind seemed to me to have a will of its own and a spiteful one, since whichever side I chose was the side the smoke and the hot ashes came at, puffing straight into my face until I was half blind. My veil was no defense. Neither was a silk handkerchief. I was thankful when our huntsmen came back with a great many ducks, because plucking them and getting them ready with the men gave me something to think about besides myself.

We borrowed a kettle from our Indian friends. It was not remarkably clean, so we warmed a little water in it and scoured it out with prairie hay before the birds went in. With that and a bowl of Indian potatoes, which our kind neighbors made us a present of, we had an excellent soup before long.

Between the cold and the smoke and the ashes and cinders driving at me all afternoon, it was the most uncomfortable stretch of the journey so far, and I was glad when the dark came and I could crawl into the tent and pull the blankets over me and be out of reach of all three at once.

In the night the storm came on ten times worse. Trees were going down all around the camp, and every crash brought us up out of whatever sleep we had, because there was no telling which one was coming down on us. Tired as we were, we hardly slept at all. It was the morning that showed us properly what had been happening in the dark. Within sight of the tent, fifty trees at least — giants, every one of them — lay flat on the ground.

We ate our scanty breakfast and got mounted, and then had to pick our way out foot by foot, the road so blocked with fallen trunks that there was barely a line through them.
