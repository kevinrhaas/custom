What the Americans did, it appears, after that first attack, was to charge the men who had hidden themselves in a kind of ravine that ran between the sand-banks and the prairie. The Indians drew together into a body, and there was hard fighting, and by the end of it the whites were down to twenty-eight men. Those twenty-eight broke through and gained a rising ground not far from the Oak Woods. Further contest was hopeless now and everyone on the hill knew it. Lieutenant Helm sent Peresh Leclerc, a half-breed boy in the service of Mr. Kinzie who had come out with the detachment and fought manfully on their side, to propose terms of capitulation. It was stipulated that the lives of all the survivors would be spared, and a ransom permitted as soon as it could be arranged.

But something else had happened while that was going on. A young savage climbed up into the baggage-wagon where the children of the white families had been put, twelve of them, and tomahawked the whole group. This was during the engagement near the sand-hills. Captain Wells was fighting close by and saw it, and cried out, "Is that their game, butchering the women and children? Then I will kill, too!"

He turned his horse's head and rode for the Indian camp near the fort, where their own squaws and children had been left.

Several of them went after him. He lay flat along his horse's neck and loaded and fired from that position, turning now and then on his pursuers. In the end their balls found him. They killed his horse and wounded him badly. Winnemeg and Wau-ban-see reached him there and tried to save him from the men who had caught up. They got him clear of the horse and were holding him up between them when another Indian, Pee-so-tum, came in behind and stabbed him in the back, and that was his death-blow.
