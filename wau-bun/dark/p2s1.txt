It was the evening of the 7th of April, 1812. Mr. Kinzie's children were dancing in front of the fire while their father played the violin for them. The tea-table was spread. They were waiting on their mother, who had gone up the river a quarter of a mile to sit with a sick neighbor.

Then the dancing stopped. The door was thrown open and Mrs. Kinzie was in the room, white with terror, hardly able to get the words out of her mouth. "The Indians! the Indians!"

"The Indians? What? Where?" they all demanded at once.

"Up at Lee's Place, killing and scalping!"

It took her some time to compose herself enough to tell it. While she was up at Burns's, a man and a boy had been seen running down the opposite side of the river at all the speed they had in them. They had called across the water to give notice to the Burns family to save themselves — the Indians were at Lee's Place, and they had only just made their escape from it. Having given that terrifying news, they had gone on at all speed for the fort, which stood on the same side of the river they were already on.

After that it was all consternation and dismay. The family were hurried into two old pirogues moored near the house and paddled across the river with every ounce of haste that could be got out of the paddles, to take refuge in the fort.

Everything the man and the boy had been able to tell was known before long. To make their story intelligible, though, the ground it happened on has first to be described.

Lee's Place, known since by the name of Hardscrabble, was a farm cut through by the Chicago River about four miles up from its mouth. The farm-house stood on the western bank of the south branch of that river. On the north side of the main stream, quite near its junction with Lake Michigan, stood the home-house and trading-establishment of Mr. Kinzie, as has already been described.

The fort was situated on the southern bank, directly opposite that mansion. The river, and a few rods of sloping green turf on either side of it, were all that lay between the two.

The fort was not constructed like the one erected on the same site in 1816. It had two block-houses on the southern side, and on the northern a sally-port — a subterranean passage running from the parade-ground down to the river. It was designed either to facilitate escape in an emergency, or as a means of supplying the garrison with water during a siege.
