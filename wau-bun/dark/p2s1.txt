The evening of the 7th of April, 1812. Mr. Kinzie had his violin out and his children were dancing to it in front of the fire. Tea was laid. They were only waiting on their mother, who had walked a quarter of a mile up the river to sit with a neighbor who was ill.

Then the door came open and she was in the room, pale, barely able to get the words out. "The Indians! the Indians!"

They all spoke at once. "The Indians? What? Where?"

"Up at Lee's Place, killing and scalping!"

It took her a moment to steady herself enough to tell it. She had been up at Burns's when a man and a boy were spotted on the far bank, running downstream as hard as they could go. They shouted across the water for the Burns family to save themselves — the Indians were at Lee's Place, and the two of them had only just got out of it. Then they kept on for the fort, which stood on their own side of the water.

After that there was nothing in the house but alarm and dismay. The family were bundled into two old pirogues that lay moored near the door, and paddled over as fast as the paddles would take them, to shelter inside the fort.

Everything the man and the boy had been able to tell was soon known. To follow any of it, though, you have to know the ground.

Lee's Place — Hardscrabble, in later years — was a farm with the Chicago River running through it, some four miles above the mouth. The farm-house stood on the west bank of the south branch. Down on the north side of the main stream, close to where it meets Lake Michigan, was the Kinzie home-house and trading establishment, described already.

The fort faced it across the water, on the southern bank: the river, with a few rods of green turf sloping to it on each side, was the whole of what lay between the two.

It was not the fort that went up on the same ground in 1816. This one had two block-houses on its southern side, and on its northern a sally-port — a passage running underground from the parade to the river. The point of it was that people could get out that way in an emergency, or that the garrison could be kept in water through a siege.
