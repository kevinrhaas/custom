We said our goodbyes, and on the morning of the eighth of March we were mounted and ready. It was a fine day. The streams had already gone green along their edges and were throwing the sun back at us, and everything about that morning promised a kind season, and an early one. At the ferry below the hill the fort stood on, Major Twiggs tried once more, and then again, to talk me out of going. He told me plainly that the journey would be dangerous past anything I was capable of imagining. I would not be moved.

Every young officer in the garrison had decided he would ride out with us as far as Duck Creek, four miles off, and none of them would hear of being refused. One or two, I think, would gladly have come the whole way, if only to break the long sameness of a life in which nothing whatever occurs. Behind us came an ox cart with a canoe riding on top of it. The canoe was to carry us over the creek, and over a wide marsh beyond it that at this time of year always stood deep in water. We laughed most of the way at the cart, which pitched and banged along a road that had never before in its life been asked to consider a wheeled carriage.

Our Canadian — a short, shock-headed, sunburnt, thick-lipped man who by some miracle happened to be the husband of my pretty servant, Mrs. Pillon — kept up a running roar at the oxen whenever they slackened or knocked the cart against a stump. "Marchez, don-g," "regardez," "prenez garde," and we found it endlessly funny. My own spirits were high. I could see no hardship anywhere ahead of me and had somehow got it into my head that I was setting out on a pleasure excursion across the prairies. That a straw bonnet and kid gloves might be the wrong things to wear had not once occurred to me. I had never travelled in so raw a season and knew nothing at all about preparing for one; when my husband suggested blanket socks and a woollen capuchon for my head and shoulders, I laughed at him and went without. So when the wind got under my head-gear and shoved it about with a rude puff, and when my hands began to swell and stiffen with the cold, I told myself these were trifles and that I would soon be used to them. I was far too pleased with the novelty of my outfit to mind — the hunting-knife in its gay scabbard hanging at my neck, the tin cup at my saddle-bow.

At Duck Creek we parted from the young men, who stayed on the bank long enough to watch us over: ourselves in the canoe, the poor horses swimming, the water carrying cakes of ice.

The far bank rose, and past the rise lay the marsh, perhaps three hundred yards of it. The men hauled the canoe across to its edge. The water there was not deep, so our people only lifted the pack off Brunet and my side-saddle off Le Gris, so that nothing would be lost if anything went wrong, and then got on their own horses and led the two spare ones. My husband set the pack-horse's load and my saddle in the middle of the canoe and took up the paddle.

"Now, wifie," he said, "jump in, and seat yourself flat in the bottom of the canoe."

"Oh, no," I said, "I will sit on the little trunk in the center. I shall be so much more comfortable, and I can balance the canoe exactly."

"As you please, but I think you will find it isn't the best way."

He shoved off hard and we slid a few feet clear of the bank. The two greyhounds we had brought with us had been standing there whining, refusing the water they had been ordered into. Now they made up their minds. They jumped together and came down square on top of me. For a moment the canoe held. Then it gave, and quick as that, dogs and baggage and lady went into the deepest water there was.

My husband had been on the point of stepping in himself when the dogs so unceremoniously went ahead of him. He was beside me before I had time to be afraid, and he had me by the collar of my cloak, begging me not to be frightened. I was not, in the least. I was laughing while he lifted me out and set me back on the bank.

The saddle and the little trunk were fished out after me, thoroughly soaked. And then our amusement was topped altogether by the maladroit Pillon, who was making a hopeless business of riding my spirited Jerry across the marsh. He had both arms around the animal's neck and a face wrung out of shape with terror, and he was shouting every French objurgation he owned. Jerry pranced and curveted, and then pitched his rider — his burden, rather — headforemost, several feet out into the water.

A general howl of mirth went up at the unfortunate Frenchman, and doubled when he came up out of his watery bed puffing and snorting and waddled back to the place he had started from. The horse, meanwhile, showed the better sense of the two and made his way off to join his companions, who had already reached the farther bank.

"Well, wifie," said Mr. Kinzie, "I can't trust you in the canoe again. There is no way but to carry you across the marsh like a pappoose. Will you take a ride on my shoulders?"

"With all my heart, if you will promise to take me safely." And I was soon mounted.

I must confess that the gentleman staggered once or twice under me, for I was no light load, and more than once I was quite sure I was about to go the way of old Pillon. But we reached the other side in safety.

There he made me change into dry shoes and stockings and — must I admit it? — drink a little brandy against the effects of my icy bath. He wanted to stop altogether, kindle a fire and dry my clothing and my wardrobe properly, and I would not hear of it. I argued that standing still would chill me worse than riding on in a wet habit and cloak, and that may well have been true. But there was something else mixed in with my convictions, which was that I could not bear to give our friends at the fort the pleasure — and they would certainly have taken it — of laughing at so inauspicious a commencement.

The horses were soon put in order and we started again. It was a fine day for the season. The wet clothes never troubled me at all; the exercise of riding took the chill straight out. The whole manner of travelling was new to me, and I liked it the better for having been shut up more than five months inside the walls of the fort, where the only variation on any day was an occasional walk of half a mile into the surrounding woods.

We lost a little more time on the road when we met Lapierre, the blacksmith from Sugar Creek, who was going up to the Portage for supplies with one of his associates. So we had come no more than twenty-three miles when we reached the ground we meant to camp on. It lay along a beautiful stream that feeds one of the Four Lakes — that chain whose banks have no equal anywhere for romantic loveliness.

I could only admire the sagacity of the horses. They seemed to know, the way a person knows, that they were coming to the place where the day's toil would end. Long before we were near the point of woods that meant a halt, their ears went up and their pace quickened, and they came into the spot at a full gallop.

We got down in an open space just inside the verge of the wood — the timber, as travelers in the West call it. My husband told me to walk about and keep moving until there was a fire, and there was one soon enough; our woodsmen were active and practised, and a large tree came down under them in a very few minutes. The dry grass all around made excellent tinder, and the sparks off the flint took it — there were no loco-focos in those days — and with broken branches and bits of light-wood fed in, we had a cheering flame. Meanwhile the bourgeois put up the tent, facing the fire, but set so that the wind would carry the smoke and flame away from the opening instead of into it. Inside, on the ground, went a bear-skin, then two or three blankets — each rider carried two, one under the saddle and one above it — and once the rest of the luggage had been brought in I could get out of every wet thing I had on and into dry ones. If you want to know what the thermometer was doing, consider my riding-habit. It was laid over the end of the huge log our fire was built against, and within a very few minutes it had frozen so stiff that it stood upright on its own, a dress out of which a lady had vanished by some means nobody could account for.

To describe the ham broiled on the "broches," the toasted bread, the steaming coffee and the primitive table-furniture would only be to tell the Fox River over again. There is this difference. On horseback we carry a coffee-pot, a tea-kettle, and for each rider a tin cup and a hunting-knife, and nothing else. Ceremony at table does not exist. A man draws his knife out of the scabbard, and if he happens to think of it he gives it a wipe on the napkin. Its first duty is to stir the cup of coffee. Its second is to divide the piece of ham lying on the half of a traveling biscuit held in the left hand, the biscuit doing the office of a plate. To cut the meat cleanly without also destroying the dish beneath it is an art that comes only with long practice.

We take our places around the mat and eat, and after a ride like that day's we find it delicious. The Frenchmen sit a little distance off with their own supplies of coffee and meat and bread, and now and then pass a joke with the bourgeois, who is a demi-god to them; nothing he does, however affable, however condescending, ever costs him an ounce of their respect or their devotion.

When the meal is finished, the table-furniture is rinsed out in hot water and set aside until morning. A wisp of dry prairie-grass is generally judged enough to make a knife fit to go back into its scabbard. At this season there is no amusement to be had but watching the spancelled horses shuffle awkwardly from one spot to the next in search of pasturage, so it is not long before we arrange our blankets and lie down to rest.

At break of day the bourgeois shouts us awake — "How! how! how!"

Everyone is up at once. The fire, fed once or twice through the night, is brought back to a flame. The horses are caught and saddled while a breakfast, the same food as the evening before, is got ready; the tent comes down; the pack-horse is loaded; "tout démanché," as the Canadian says. We eat, rinse the kettles and the cups and tie them to our saddle-bows, and then we are mounted and away, leaving our fire, or rather our smoke, to tell of our visit.
