We took a tender leave of our friends, and on the morning of the 8th of March we were mounted and equipped and ready to go. The weather was fine. The streams were already fringed with green and sparkling in the sun, and everything promised an early, genial season. At the ferry, at the foot of the hill the fort stood on, Major Twiggs tried once more to talk me out of it. He told me the trip would be perilous beyond anything I was able to anticipate. He said it more than once. I was resolute.

Our party was swelled by an escort of all the young officers, who insisted politely on riding with us as far as Duck Creek, four miles off. There were some among them who would gladly have gone the whole way and got free of the monotony of their solitary, uneventful lives. Behind us came an ox cart with a canoe perched on top of it, meant to carry us over the creek and over an extensive marsh beyond it that was flooded to a considerable depth every year at that season. We got a good deal of amusement out of the progress of this vehicle as it bumped and thumped along a road that had never before known the dignity of a wheeled carriage.

Our little shock-headed, sunburnt, thick-lipped Canadian — who happened, most miraculously, to be the husband of my pretty servant, Mrs. Pillon — shouted at the oxen whenever they lagged or jolted against a stump. "Marchez, don-g!" "Regardez!" "Prenez garde!" It kept us laughing. I was in high spirits. I foresaw no hardship and no danger. I had somehow arranged it in my mind that I was setting out on a pleasure excursion across the prairies, and it had not once occurred to me that a straw bonnet and kid gloves were no equipment for such an expedition. I had never traveled at so hard a season, so I was heedlessly ignorant of how one prepares for it, and I had resisted, or laughed at, my husband's suggestion that I provide myself with blanket socks and a woollen capuchon for my head and shoulders. Now the wind lifted my head-gear with a rude puff now and then, and before long my hands were swollen and stiff with cold, and I persuaded myself that these were trifles I would soon grow used to. I was too well pleased with the novelty of my outfit — my hunting-knife in its gay scabbard hanging at my neck, my tin cup at my saddle-bow — to think much about minor inconveniences.

At Duck Creek we took leave of our young friends, who stayed standing on the bank long enough to watch us make the passage across: ourselves in the canoe, and the poor horses swimming the stream beside us, which was running full of cakes of floating ice.

Past the rising ground that made the far bank lay a marsh about three hundred yards across. The men carried the canoe over to it. The water there was not deep, so our attendants only took the pack off Brunet and my side-saddle off Le Gris, to avoid accidents, and then mounted their own animals and led the two spare ones. My husband put the pack-horse's furniture and my saddle in the center of the canoe. He would paddle us over.

"Now, wifie," he said, "jump in, and seat yourself flat in the bottom of the canoe."

"Oh, no," I said. "I will sit on the little trunk in the center. I shall be so much more comfortable, and I can balance the canoe exactly."

"As you please, but I think you will find it isn't the best way."

A vigorous push sent us a few feet off the bank. The two favorite greyhounds we had brought with us had been standing at the water's edge whining, unwilling to go in as they had been ordered. Now they gathered themselves and jumped, and came down full on top of me. The canoe held its balance for a moment. Then it gave. Dogs, furniture and lady went into the deepest part of the water together.

My husband had been just about to spring into the canoe when the dogs took precedence of him so unceremoniously. He was beside me in a moment. He caught me by the collar of my cloak and begged me not to be frightened. I was not frightened in the least, and I only laughed while he lifted me out and set me on the bank.

The saddle and the little trunk were fished up after me, both of them thoroughly soaked by then. What was left of our gravity went when we looked out at the marsh and saw the maladroit Pillon trying to ride my spirited Jerry across it. He had both arms around the animal's neck. His face was distorted with terror and he was shouting every French objurgation he knew. Jerry pranced, curveted, and then shot his rider — his burden, rather — headforemost several feet out into the water.

A general outcry of mirth greeted the unfortunate Frenchman. It doubled when he came up puffing and snorting out of his watery bed and waddled back to where he had started. The horse, meanwhile, very sensibly made his own way over to join his companions, who had reached the farther bank some time before.

"Well, wifie," said Mr. Kinzie, "I can't trust you in the canoe again. There is no way but to carry you across the marsh like a pappoose. Will you take a ride on my shoulders?"

"With all my heart, if you will promise to take me safely." And I was soon mounted.

I must confess that the gentleman staggered now and then under his burden, which was no slight one, and more than once on the way over I was sadly afraid that I was about to meet a fate very similar to old Pillon's. But happily we came to the other side in safety.

There my husband insisted on dry shoes and stockings, and — must I admit it? — a little brandy, to work off the effects of my icy bath. He would gladly have halted to build a fire and dry my clothes and my wardrobe properly. I would not hear of it. I argued that the delay would expose me to more cold than riding on in my wet habit and cloak, and that may well have been true, but mixed in with my convictions on the subject was a spice of reluctance to give our friends at the fort the chance, which they certainly would have taken, to laugh at such an inauspicious commencement.

The horses were soon put in order and we took up our march again. It was a fine day for the season. My wet garments gave me no trouble at all, since the exercise of riding took away any feeling of chill. It was a new way of traveling to me, and I enjoyed it the more for having been shut up more than five months inside the walls of the fort, with hardly any change in the tenor of our lives beyond a walk of half a mile now and then into the surrounding woods.

We were held up once more on the road by meeting Lapierre, the blacksmith from Sugar Creek, who was going up to the Portage for supplies with one of his associates, so that we had not made more than twenty-three miles when we came to the ground we meant to camp on. It stood on a beautiful stream, a tributary of one of the Four Lakes — that chain whose banks are unrivalled for romantic loveliness.

I could not help admiring the sagacity of the horses, who seemed to know with something like human intelligence that we were coming to the place where their work would end. While we were still well short of the point of woods that meant a halt, their ears went up, their pace quickened, and they came into the spot at a full gallop.

We got down at an open space just inside the verge of the wood — the timber, as Western travelers call it. My husband told me to walk about until a fire could be made, which our active and experienced woodsmen managed quickly, felling a large tree in a very few minutes. The dry grass around us made excellent tinder. Sparks off the flint took it — there were no loco-focos in those days — and with broken branches and bits of light-wood it came up into a cheering flame. The bourgeois, meanwhile, was setting up the tent, taking care to place it opposite the fire but at such an angle that the wind would carry the smoke and flame away from the opening. Inside, on the ground, went first a bear-skin, then two or three blankets, of which every rider had carried two, one under the saddle and one above it. Then the rest of the luggage was brought in, and I could at last strip off everything wet and put on dry. You may judge the state of the thermometer from this: my riding-habit, laid over the end of the huge log our fire was built against, froze stiff in a very few minutes and stood upright by itself, like a dress a lady had vanished out of in some way nobody could account for.

It would only be a repetition of our experience on the Fox River to describe the ham broiled on the broches, the toasted bread, the steaming coffee, the primitive table-furniture. There is this difference, though: traveling on horseback we carry only a coffee-pot, a tea-kettle, and for each rider his tin cup and his hunting-knife. The deportment at table is marked by an absence of ceremony. The knife comes out of the scabbard, and those who remember to do it give it a wipe on the napkin. Its first office is to stir the cup of coffee. Its next is to divide the piece of ham, which is laid on the half of a traveling biscuit held in the left hand to serve as a plate. Cutting the meat so skilfully that you do not at the same time destroy the dish is an art that comes only with long practice.

We take our places around the mat, and after a fatiguing ride we find the food delicious. The Frenchmen sit a little apart, taking their own supplies of coffee, meat and bread, and now and then passing a joke with the bourgeois, who is their demi-god, and for whom their respect and devotion are never lessened by his affability or condescension.

The meal over, the table-furniture is rinsed in hot water and set aside until morning. A wisp of dry prairie-grass is considered in most cases to render the knife fit to go back in its scabbard, and at this season of the year there is no amusement left but watching the awkward movements of the spancelled horses as they work their way from spot to spot in search of pasturage, so we are usually soon content to arrange our blankets and lie down.

At break of day we are woken by the shout of the bourgeois — "How! how! how!"

Everyone comes up out of sleep at once. The fire, which has sometimes been replenished in the night, is soon blown into flame. The horses are caught and saddled while a breakfast like the last evening's meal is got ready. The tent comes down. The pack-horse is loaded — tout démanché, as the Canadian says. Breakfast finished, we rinse our kettles and cups, tie them to our saddle-bows, and mount and go, leaving our fire, or rather our smoke, to say that we were there.
