Morning came up clear and bright. Sleep had put us back together, and we looked forward to taking up the journey again with real pleasure, certain that the rest of it would give us no more trouble. Mr. Hamilton offered to ride with us as far as his next neighbor's — twenty-five miles, a distance he waved off as trifling. Beyond that, from Kellogg's on to Ogie's Ferry on the Rock River, the track was traveled enough that we could not lose it. That was his assurance.

One of the miners came to see us off — the one who owned the wife and the baby, and who was, for that reason, a shade more human than the men he worked beside. His farewell was that he wished us well out of the country, and that we might never have occasion to return to it.

"I pity a body," he said, "when I see them making such an awful mistake as to come out this way, for comfort never touched this Western country."

Mr. Hamilton was as good a companion that day as he had been the day before, and a perfectly desperate rider. He set a pace that would have left me somewhere behind if I had still been on my pony. I had the fine, noble Jerry under me now, and I needed him.

We were all well mounted and he still nearly rode away from us. The prairie had given out. We were among the branches of the Pickatonick, in country that had gone rough and broken, and we went down into ravines and up out of them and through passes so narrow that the branches met overhead. They were interwoven and they projected, and as I came through behind the others I paid them my veil in pieces. Once they caught my hat, and I would have hung there like Absalom if my husband had not sprung to me — Jerry being far too keen on holding his place in the race to do anything but what the mule did under the unfortunate prince.

Nobody called a halt. We rode at that same rate until three o'clock in the afternoon, so that when we came in at last to Kellogg's there was no arguing the distance: we had made thirty miles at least. What wore on me hardest through all of it was not the pace but the little beast Brunet. He had been a saddle-horse until now, and he had been used to a place well up in the file, near the guide, near the front. Being demoted to pack-horse and put at the back did not suit him. Every time we came down to a stream the file would break apart, each rider choosing his own place to ford, and every time — every single time — the moment I turned Jerry's head into the water, Brunet came past me at a rush and flung himself into the exact spot where we meant to go. He plunged and snorted and threw the water over me, and more than once he startled Jerry sideways and very nearly put me into the stream. I learned after the first two crossings to hang back and let him have it his own way. That changed nothing. What he wanted was not to go first; it was to go in at the same instant Jerry did. In the end I struck a bargain with young Roy, who got down at every stream and held him until I was safely on the other bank.

Kellogg's was a comfortable mansion set just inside the verge of a pleasant grove of timber, which is what a Western traveler calls a small forest. Mrs. Kellogg proved to be a very respectable-looking matron, and she let us know early that she came from the city of New York. Mr. Hamilton's presence at her table plainly gratified her, and she found occasion to tell us why: in earlier days she had done needle-work for his family.

She gave us an excellent dinner. Afterward she settled me into a rocking-chair beside a large fire and put the "Life of Mrs. Fletcher" into my hands to keep me occupied, while the gentlemen went out to look over the place, and Mr. Kellogg's stock, and then, more carefully, their own animals. We had meant to push on to Dixon's before dark. The snow started again, and that settled it.

When it came out that we were bound for Chicago, Mr. Kellogg made up his mind on the spot to come with us; he had business to do there, he said. Mrs. Kellogg set about getting him ready to leave in the morning. I argued against it. The next day was Sunday, and I do not travel on Sunday, and I pleaded for one more day where we were. I lost. There was the necessity of the case, and the weather that could not be relied on, and the plain importance of getting through this wild country as fast as we were able. All I carried was a delay in the hour of starting — late enough in the afternoon to leave us just time for the sixteen miles to Dixon's before the sun went.

Mr. Kellogg needed almost no time to prepare. He would take two days' provisions and no more. At his brother-in-law Dixon's we would have our supper and our breakfast, and from Dixon's to Chicago was a day and a half; he knew that road well.

By that arithmetic what remained in our own packs was enough to see us to the end of the journey. My husband asked Mrs. Kellogg to bake us another bag of biscuits anyway, against accidents, and he suggested to Mr. Kellogg that he might do well to carry something beyond his limited allowance. The good man would not have it. He was unwilling to burden his horse any further than was absolutely necessary, and at this season, he pointed out, the animals had to carry their own fodder on top of everything else. It will be seen that we had reason to rejoice in our own foresight.

After the night before, my standards had come down considerably. So when I was shown our sleeping-apartment and understood that we would be sharing it with six men, travelers like ourselves, the only thing I felt was gratitude that every bed in the room had a full suit of blue checked curtains, which made a very tolerable substitute for a dressing-room.
