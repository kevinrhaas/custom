The commandant at Fort Pitt took up Mr. Lytle's business as if it were his own. He gave him a detachment of soldiers to go with him and his friends after the marauders, and they rode out. Certain circumstances had already turned suspicion toward the Senecas, and it was among the villages of that tribe that the search settled.

They asked their questions in a dozen directions and asked them carefully, every time, because all the tribes of the Iroquois — the Five Nations, as they grandly called themselves — were allies of Great Britain and carried a settled hatred of the Americans. A wrong word in the wrong lodge would have ended the search and the searchers together. So it was a long while before the father and his party stood at last in the village of the Big White Man.

A treaty for the ransom of the captives was opened at once, and for Mrs. Lytle and the younger child it went easily; a price was named and the price was met. For the little Eleanor, the adopted child of the tribe, there was no price. Nothing moved him — not offers, not entreaties, not promises. "No," the chief said, "she was his sister, he had taken her to supply the place of his brother who was killed by the enemy — she was dear to him, and he wouldn't part with her."

Everything the father tried came back off that answer like a stone off a wall. In the end he had to go, and take with him only such of his beloved ones as he had had the good fortune to recover.

We will not try to set down what it is to be a parent walking out of a village with one child's hand still in it. There was no alternative. They commended her to the care of their heavenly Father, and they took some comfort — real comfort — from the plain tenderness with which she had been treated so far, and on that they started the melancholy trip home, telling themselves that the next attempt would be the one that brought her back.

He put his family somewhere safe at Pittsburg first. Then, still backed by the commandant and the Indian Agent, he set out for the frontier and the house of the British Agent, Colonel Johnson. He laid the whole case out, and it went straight to that benevolent officer's heart. Johnson promised to spare no exertion on his behalf, and he kept the promise to the letter. As soon as the spring opened the country he went in person to the village of the Big White Man and offered him splendid presents, guns and horses both. The chief was inexorable.
