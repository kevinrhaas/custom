When he reached Fort Winnebago the first thing we did was fit him out with a whole wardrobe of his own. It went into a box in the room where he slept, and the box was his to keep. I have no words for what that did to him. Every minute he could spare he spent looking at what was in it. Now and then Tomah would simply be gone, and he was never anywhere else: sitting on the floor by his little trunk, folding and refolding the clothes, laying them out lengthwise, then crosswise, the happiest creature alive.

The next thing was to make him useful. We began with small jobs, the sort of thing we supposed he had done before, and it did not take us long to notice a pattern: the moment there was anything resembling work in the house, Tomah went to bed. He would go without his supper to do it. One evening, right at dark, he was handed some fish to scale, and Tom, true to himself, retired at once.

The cook came in to ask what she was supposed to do about it. There was nothing for it but to call my husband in to interpret. He sent for Tomah, and when the boy came into the parlor Mr. Kinzie said to him, in Pottowattamie, "There are some fish, Tomah, in the kitchen, and we want you to scale them."

"Now?" said Tom, in complete astonishment. "It is very late."

Miss Rolette, a young lady who was staying with us and who had the language, could not hold it in and laughed outright at the plainness of the thing, and we all laughed with her, which made Tom look a little indignant. But once it was explained to him that scaling fish at night, and putting salt and pepper on them, is simply a white custom, he made his peace with the duty and went and did it.

His next office was laying the table. We had a best service of china that only came out for company, and a best set of teaspoons that lived in a bureau drawer in my own room upstairs. I kept that drawer locked and the key under a little clock on the mantel-piece. The first time I showed Tomah how things were to be arranged for visitors, I carried the silver down and laid it out on the table with my own hands.

Not long after, we were expecting company to tea again, and I told Tomah that the best china was to be used. So imagine what I felt when I passed through the dining-room a little later and found on the table not only the new china but the company silver as well. I asked our mother, who could talk with him, to look into it.

Tomah answered her perfectly coolly. "He got the silver where it was kept."

"Did he find the drawer open?"

"No - he opened it with a key."

"Was the key in the drawer?"

"No - it was under that thing on the shelf."

"How did he know it was kept there?"

That was the one question Mr. Tomah would not answer. We never did learn whether he had watched me at some point and taken note. Nobody had ever laid eyes on him in that part of the house. All the same, there was hardly an article under our roof whose whereabouts Tomah did not know, and when anybody was hunting for something the answer in our house became, "Ask Tomah - he will tell you." He always could.

The young officers found him an endless entertainment. We were giving a tea one evening for all the families and the young officers from the Fort, and to make Tomah look as professional as we could we made him a white apron with long sleeves to wear while he helped Mary and Josette carry the tea round — for I must own that Tomah's clothes were never kept in the order out of the trunk that they were kept in while they were in it.

Tom was delighted with the costume and just as delighted with the work. He did it faultlessly, too; he never had the least trouble copying anything he had watched somebody else do. After tea there was music. I was standing beside the piano, where one of the ladies had sat down to play, when Lieutenant Vancleve said quietly at my elbow, "Look behind you a moment."

I turned round. There was Tom, seated between two of the company, as stately as a judge, his white apron smoothed flat over his knees and his hands clasped in front of him, listening to the music and plainly on the best possible terms with himself and with everyone in the room. Julian and Edwin could barely hold their faces straight, but neither of them dared say a word or make a move for fear of shifting him before the company had got the full good of the sight. It was carried unanimously that Tomah should be allowed to stay and taste the pleasures of society for one evening. Then the music stopped, and with that restlessness of his he was bored on the instant, and took his leave of the company without ceremony.
