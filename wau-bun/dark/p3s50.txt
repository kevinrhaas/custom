The first thing we did when he reached Fort Winnebago was to furnish him with a complete wardrobe, which we put in a box in his sleeping-apartment and placed under his own charge. There are no words for what that did to him. The valuable possessions were his, and every spare minute he had went to the contemplation of them. Now and then Tomah would go missing. He was always found in the same place, seated beside his little trunk, folding and refolding the clothes, laying them in lengthwise, then taking them out and laying them crosswise, the happiest of mortals.

Our next step was to teach him to be useful. The little offices given him at first were chosen as things not altogether strange to him, but we soon noticed a pattern: whenever there was anything in the shape of work in prospect, Tomah slipped off to bed, sometimes before he had even had his supper. One evening some fish were given him to scale. It was just at dark, and Tom, according to custom, retired at once to bed.

The cook came in to ask what was to be done about it. I had to call in my husband as interpreter. He sent for Tomah, and when the boy came into the parlor Mr. Kinzie said to him, in Pottowattamie, "There are some fish, Tomah, in the kitchen, and we want you to scale them."

"Now?" said Tom, with an expression of pure amazement. "It is very late."

A young lady who was visiting us, Miss Rolette, understood the language, and she could not help bursting out laughing at the simplicity of it, and we joined her out of sympathy. Tom looked a little indignant at that. But when it was explained to him that it was the white custom to scale fish at night, and to put salt and pepper on them, he was soon reconciled to doing his duty in the matter.

His next office was to lay the table. There was a best service of china that only came out when we had company, and a best set of teaspoons that I kept in the drawer of a bureau in my own room, above-stairs. I was in the habit of keeping that drawer locked and putting the key under a small clock on the mantel-piece. The first time I showed Tomah how to arrange things for visitors, I brought the silver down and put it on the table myself.

Soon after, we were to have company to tea again, and I explained to Tomah that the best china must be used. Imagine my surprise, going through the dining-room a little while later, at finding not only the new china laid out but the company silver beside it. I asked our mother, who could speak with him, to inquire into the matter.

Tomah said, very coolly, "He got the silver where it was kept."

"Did he find the drawer open?"

"No — he opened it with a key."

"Was the key in the drawer?"

"No — it was under that thing on the shelf."

"How did he know it was kept there?"

That was the one thing Mr. Tomah declined to tell. We never did find out whether he had watched my movements at some time or other. Nobody had ever seen him in that part of the house. And yet there was scarcely an article in it whose whereabouts Tomah did not know. When anybody was at a loss to find something, it was always the same answer — ask Tomah, he will tell you. And in fact he always did.

He was a subject of much amusement to the young officers. We were to have a tea-party one evening, all the families and the young officers from the Fort. To make Tomah's appearance as professional as possible we made him a white apron with long sleeves to wear while he helped Mary and Josette carry the tea round, for I have to admit that Tomah's clothes were not kept in such nice order out of the trunk as in it.

Tom was delighted with the new costume and with the new employment. He acquitted himself to perfection; he never had any difficulty imitating what he had seen another person do. After tea we had some music. I was standing by the piano, where one of the ladies was seated, when Lieutenant Vancleve said to me in a low voice, "Look behind you a moment."

I turned. There sat Tom, between two of the company, as stately as could be, his white apron smoothed down over his knees and his hands clasped in front of him, listening to the music, on the best possible terms with himself and with everyone in the room. Julian and Edwin were hardly able to hold their merriment in, but they were afraid to do or say anything that might make him move before the company had had the full enjoyment of the scene. It was voted unanimously that Tomah should be allowed to stay and enjoy the pleasures of society for one evening. But he had his own restlessness in him, and the moment the music stopped he was tired of it, and took his leave of the company without ceremony.
