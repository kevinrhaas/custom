On the forenoon of the second day word reached us that we were coming up on that place. I have to own it: we held our breath for awe.

The three Winnebagoes were up in the bow. Old Smoker, the chief, sat squatted on his feet on the bench of the foremost rowers. We looked at him. He was staring hard at the wooded point ahead of us, and we followed the line of his eyes, and while we were looking three Indians stepped forward and stood on the bank. We said it to each other under our breath: if they are Sauks, we are lost, because the whole body of them will be in that thicket. The boat went on. Nobody spoke. There was the dip of the paddle, and there was, I think, the sound of some hearts, and nothing else at all. We looked back at the chief. His nostrils were flared open and his eyes were almost glaring.

Then he came off that bench in a single bound and let out his long, shrill whoop.

"Hoh! hoh! hoh! Neechee — friend — Muh-no-mo-nee!"

After that it was all gladness. Every one of us was pressing forward to shake hands with the strangers the moment we could reach them, purely out of joy that they were Menomonees and not the others. They could tell us nothing whatever about the Sauks.

By noon that day we counted ourselves clear of the dangerous country. We were still careful. When the boat was pushed ashore for the mid-day pipe, under a fine overhanging bank with thick woods on top of it, the watchfulness slackened a little, and the young people were let out to wander a short way from the boat with Arthur and Mâtâ minding them.

They were back before long. The woods, they said, were alive with pigeons — you could nearly knock them down with a stick — and would we please, please let them shoot just enough for supper. No. The enemy might be closer than we thought, and one shot fired would tell them exactly where we were, and the wise thing was to give notice to nobody, friend or foe. They went back to the boat without their game, and not happily.

The next morning brought us to Powell's, at the Butte des Morts. There was not a loaf of bread in the place, and the faces of the poor Frenchmen went sad at the news of it. Our own store was finished by then too. All that could be got as a substitute was a bag of dark, bitter-looking flour. That was the provision for our whole party, and we had to be content with it, and we left the Hillock of the Dead feeling that it had been the grave of our hopes as well.
