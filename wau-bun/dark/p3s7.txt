The country changed under us now, more varied than anything we had crossed so far — hills swelling up gently, lovely valleys between them, streams running bright at the bottom. What it did not have was living things. Now and then a shout from whoever was leading (we traveled Indian file, as the custom is) would turn all our heads toward a herd of deer loping, as the Westerners say it, off through the trees; or somebody would put a spur in at the sight of a small dark shape far ahead of us on the trail. It never came to anything. The game always contrived to be gone before we could get near it, and to leave the beaten track for a proper hunt was out of the question.

A little after midday we came down a long sloping knoll, and at a sudden turn the whole lake opened in front of us — Gros-pied to the French, Maunk-suck to the people who lived on it, and Big-foot to us, after the chief whose village stood above its water. Bold hills pushed forward into the clear blue of it, or drew back a little to leave a green level nook where a man might set down a home. Along the nearer shore ran a bright gravelly beach, and here and there a small pure stream came coursing across it to join the larger water.

On rising ground at the foot of one of the bluffs in the middle distance stood a cluster of neat wigwams, with gardens around them. They did not spoil the picture. They completed it.

The whole party shouted out loud without meaning to. It was like the Hudson, only less bold — no, it was like the lake of the Forest Cantons in the picture of the Chapel of William Tell. What could be imagined more enchanting? Oh, if our friends at the East could only be here to enjoy it with us.

We stayed a long while looking at it, and then put the spurs in and skirted the head of the lake, and before long we were climbing the broad platform where the village of Maunk-suck, or Big-foot, stood.

They had watched us coming from a long way off. Every one of them was assembled in front of the wigwams by the time we arrived, ready to greet us if we were friends, and to do whatever the occasion should call for if we were not. Nothing like the sight of us had ever come up that rise before. Their welcome was every bit as warm as we had expected. "Shaw-nee-aw-kee" and his mother, who was known through the whole tribe by that touching name, "Our friend's wife," were received with real kindness, and an animated talk started up that I could follow only as far as the gestures carried it. So I gave myself over to studying everything in front of me.

The chief was a large man, raw-boned and ugly, his face swollen out of shape by drink, with something sinister and unpleasant in his expression. He had a gay-colored handkerchief tied over his head and was otherwise dressed in his best, out of compliment to the strangers.

This was the chief to whom Chambly — Shaw-bee-nay, they call him now — and Billy Caldwell and Robinson were sent by Dr. Wolcott, their Agent, during the Winnebago war in 1821, to do everything in their power to keep him and his band from joining the hostile Indians. It took some doing, but they managed it, and by managing it they almost certainly saved the life of every settler then living exposed on the frontier.

Of all the groups of his people standing around us, none held my eye like one young man. His face was handsome and his figure was striking even in a crowd where every figure was fine and well made. He too had a gay handkerchief on his head, and a shirt of the brightest lemon-colored calico, and silver ornaments in quantity, and — this was what gave the whole costume its strangeness — one legging of blue and the other of bright scarlet. I knew what that particular detail meant. It is the sign of a heart suffering under the tender passion. The flute in his hand said the same thing over again, and the joyous, animated look on his face said, just as plainly, that he was not a lover in any danger of despair.

It was easy to picture him lately back from the chase, loaded with game, walking into the lodge of the fair one as the custom is and throwing his burden down at her parents' feet with a superb, indifferent air, as much as to say: here is some meat, it is a mere trifle, but it will show you what you might expect with me for a son-in-law. And I had no doubt at all that the girl had stepped forward and gathered it up, which is how she accepts the offering and the man who brought it. There was nothing in the look or the manner of any of the young women around us to show which of them was the happy one, and though I searched every face I could not find so much as a conscious blush anywhere. So I picked out the youngest and the prettiest of them for myself and went on weaving my romance to my own satisfaction.

The village sat inside a ring of hills so steep, cut by gorges so narrow, that climbing out seemed barely possible on horseback. How we were to get a four-wheeled carriage up was the question now being argued between my husband and the Pottowattamies. There was no choice but to try it, by whichever pass the inhabitants judged the most practicable. Petaille went first and I came after him on my favorite Jerry. It was a scramble of a kind one does not often take — very nearly straight up, through what looked like the dry bed of a torrent, filled now with loose stones and hardly offering one secure foothold from the bottom to the top. I hung on to the mane, and part of the way I had both arms around Jerry's neck outright, and with the shouts and cheers of the people below coming up after us we got there — safe, and very nearly out of breath — and sat on the pinnacle looking down to see how the next party would fare.

The horses had been taken out of the carriage. Its luggage went up on the shoulders of the young Indians, to be toted to the top. Then ropes were made fast to its sides and a whole crowd of our red friends took hold of them, with our two Frenchmen at the head, and two or three more got in behind to hold it and keep it from running backward. Then the work started. Such pulling. Such shouting. Such clapping of hands from the spectators of both sexes, and such stentorian orders and encouragement bawled out by the bourgeois. Every so often there would be a check, a wavering, the whole thing seeming about to go over backward into the plain below — and then no, they would have it again, and after efforts I would not have believed possible the carriage came safely up onto the table-land with the rest of us. In time everything and everyone was landed there. We paid our friends to their satisfaction, gathered up the goods and chattels, repacked the wagon, and set off for our encampment at Turtle Creek.

Between the labor of that climb, the excitement of it, and a sun that kept getting hotter, that afternoon's ride was more uncomfortable than anything we had felt on the whole journey. We were truly glad when our guide's whoop went up and a few scattered lodges came into view and we knew we had reached our ground. We picked a beautiful sequestered spot beside a clear, sparkling stream, and when we had dismounted and seen the horses made comfortable, my husband gave his men their directions and then took me off to a private place where I could put aside my hat and my mask and bathe my flushed face and aching head in the cool water. I have never felt anything so grateful, so delicious. I sat down and leaned my head back against one of the tall trees overshadowing the place, and I was nearly dreaming when they called me in to the evening meal.

The Indians had made us a present of some fine brook trout, and our Frenchmen had cooked them in the most tempting way, and before the bright moon came up and we were ready for our rest, the headache and the fatigue had both gone out of me.

One of the finest things about traveling this way is the joyous, vocal life of the forest at first light, when every feathered creature in it comes out to pay its cheerful salutation to the opening day.
