Wau-kaun-kah, the Little Snake, offered himself as a hostage until the suspected people should be delivered up. He was received as such by the Agent, and marched over and put in confinement at the Fort, to stay there until the seven accused men came in to redeem him.

It took the nation some little time to persuade those men to place themselves in the hands of the whites, so that they might receive justice according to the laws of the whites. The trial of Red Bird, and his slow death in prison afterward, were still fresh in their memories. It took a good deal of resolution, and a strong conviction of their own innocence, to brace them up to such a step.

It had to be brought about by argument and by persuasion. The nation would never have resorted to force to compel the fulfilment of their own stipulation.

In the meantime a solemn talk was held with the principal chiefs, assembled at the Agency. A great part of the nation was in the immediate neighborhood, in obedience to a notice sent out by Governor Porter, who, by virtue of his office as Governor of Michigan Territory, was also Superintendent of the Northwest Division of the Indians. Instead of calling on the Agent to take charge of the annuity money, as had been the custom until then, the Governor had announced that he would bring it himself to Fort Winnebago and be present at the payment. The appointed time had now come, and with it the main body of the Winnebagoes.

Those of the Indians who had not attended the treaty at Rock Island, and had no hand in the ceding of their country, were loud in their condemnation of the step and in their lamentations over it. Foremost among them was Wild-Cat, the Falstaff of Garlic Island and its vicinity. It was little wonder that he should shed bitter tears, as he did, over the loss of his beautiful home on the blue waters of Winnebago Lake.

If he had not been accidentally stopped on his way to the treaty, he said, and detained until it was too late, he would never, never have permitted the bargain.

His Father, who knew that the real cause of his failing to accompany his countrymen to Rock Island was a desperate frolic he had been enticed into along the way, replied gravely that he had heard of the chief's misfortune on this occasion. How, in ascending the Fox River, a couple of kegs of whiskey had come floating down the stream, and had run foul of his canoe with such force that the canoe was injured, and he had been obliged to stop several days at the Mee-kan to repair damages.

The shouts of laughter that greeted this explanation were so contagious that poor Wild-Cat was compelled to join in and treat his misfortune as a joke.

The suspected Indians engaged the services of Judge Doty to defend them at their future trial, and in time notice was given that on a certain day they would be brought to the Portage and surrendered to their Father, who would transfer them to the keeping of the military officer appointed to receive them.

It was joyful news to poor Wau-kaun-kah that the day of his release was at hand. Every time we had been inside the walls of the Fort, we had been saluted by a call from him, from his station at the guard-room window.

"Do you hear anything of those Indians? When are they coming, that I may be let out?"

We had tried to lighten his confinement by seeing that he was well supplied with food, and his Father and Paquette had paid him visits from time to time. But in spite of these attentions, and in spite of the kindness he received at the Fort, his confinement was irksome beyond anything he could say.
