Wau-kaun-kah — the Little Snake — offered himself. No one asked it of him. He would stand as hostage until the suspected men were handed over, and on those terms the Agent accepted him, and he was walked across to the Fort and shut up there to wait until the seven accused came to buy him back.

Getting those seven to give themselves up was not quick work, and the nation had to do it themselves. What was being asked of them was that they walk into the hands of the whites and take whatever the white law decided to call justice. They remembered Red Bird. They remembered what his trial had been, and how he had wasted away and died in confinement. A man needs a good deal of nerve to step into that, and he needs to be very certain in himself that he has done nothing.

So it was done with talk, and with more talk. The nation would not compel anyone. They had given their word, but they would not use force on their own people to make it good.

While that went on, a formal council was held with the head chiefs at the Agency. Most of the nation was camped close by, having come in on word from Governor Porter, who was Governor of Michigan Territory and by that office also Superintendent of the Northwest Division of the Indians. Custom had always been for the Agent to receive the annuity money and hand it out. This year the Governor had let it be known that he would carry the money to Fort Winnebago himself and stand there while it was paid. The date he had named had now come round, and the main body of the Winnebagoes had come with it.

Those who had not been at Rock Island, who had had no hand in signing their country away, said what they thought of it loudly, and grieved over it loudly. Loudest of all was Wild-Cat, the Falstaff of Garlic Island and the country round about. And who could blame him for the tears he shed. He had lost his home on the blue water of Winnebago Lake, and it had been a beautiful one.

"If he hadn't been accidentally stopped," he said, "on his way to the treaty, and detained until it was too late, he would never, never have permitted the bargain."

His Father knew exactly what had stopped him, which was a spectacular carouse somebody had lured him into along the road, and he answered with a perfectly straight face, "That he had heard of the chief's misfortune on this occasion. How that, in ascending the Fox River, a couple of kegs of whiskey had come floating down the stream, which, running foul of his canoe with great force, had injured it to such a degree that he had been obliged to stop several days at the Mee-kan, to repair damages."

The council came apart laughing, and it was so contagious a noise that Wild-Cat had no choice but to laugh along with the rest and let his misfortune stand as a joke.

The accused men had by then retained Judge Doty to defend them when they came to trial, and at last word arrived: on a named day they would be brought to the Portage and given up to their Father, who would pass them on to the military officer appointed to take them.

For poor Wau-kaun-kah that was the best news in the world, because it meant the door would open. We never once crossed inside the walls of the Fort without being hailed from the guard-room window, where he kept his post:

"Do you hear anything of those Indians? When are they coming, that I may be let out?"

We did what could be done. We saw that he had plenty to eat, and his Father and Paquette came to sit with him now and then. The Fort itself treated him well enough. None of it made any difference. Being shut in was a misery past any describing.
