It did not take us long to find out that Green Bay had worked itself into a panic over the Sauks. The town had settled on a conviction and would not be talked out of it: the enemy was coming here, on their way to Canada, to put themselves under the protection of the British Government. By what route they were to manage the last leg of it, nobody could say. Whether they meant to halt on the shore and build themselves bark canoes for the crossing, or whether they intended to charter one of Mr. Newbery's schooners, the good people had not quite settled in their minds. What was beyond dispute was that a share of the citizens had frightened themselves nearly to death, and had decided there was no safety anywhere on earth except behind the walls of the old fort — a fort falling apart at the seams, and emptied besides, since nearly every soldier in it had been pulled out and marched off to Fort Winnebago some time before.

Then a traveler came through with a story, and the story did the rest. He had, he said, slept one night on the exact ground where the Sauks took their breakfast the next morning. Everyone knew the Sauks were down to very short commons by then. It followed, to the town's satisfaction, that half an hour's delay on his part would have seen him eaten, and the man was accordingly credited with a wonderful escape.

Our own friends and acquaintances took no part in any of it. Anyone willing to think for a moment about the country such a march would have to cross, and about the numerous and well-armed whites waiting at the far end of it, could see the thing was impossible. Still, there were people no argument could reach, who believed safety lay in flight and nowhere else, and as the anxiety wound tighter the eyes of the town kept turning to the water, watching for the "Mariner."
