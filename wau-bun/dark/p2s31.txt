The kindness lasted exactly as long as it took a second party of the enemy to come up. They had the mother of the little prisoners with them, and in her arms was the youngest of the family, an infant three months old.

Their father had gone off early that day with the serving-men to a raising a few miles away, and the house was left without a defender in it. Nobody had thought twice about that. The country had been quiet for a long stretch of years — nothing to molest anyone, nothing to raise an alarm — and quiet gets into a household and softens it. Lately they had set aside some of the caution they had once thought necessary.

The party had lain in wait for the favorable moment, and when it came they took the defenceless family prisoner. Afterward Mrs. Lytle studied the paint on their faces, and the other marks by which the early settlers learned to tell one tribe from another, and concluded that she and her children had fallen into the hands of Senecas. She was not mistaken. They had come down from their village meaning to fall on some isolated band of Delawares, who were their enemies; they had failed to find them; and they had made up the loss by carrying off a few white settlers instead.

That the tribe was, on the whole, a mild one, and that the chief with this party was a man of some magnanimity — those two facts, and nothing else, are why the Lytles did not end as most Americans ended who fell into the hands of the Iroquois.

From their mother the children learned what she did not know. She had no word at all of the other two, the boy of six and the little girl of four. The servant-girl had disappeared at the very beginning as well, and the mother let herself hope that the three of them had got away together.

They halted a few hours, long enough for the prisoners' exhausted bodies to recover a little, and then the march began again. One of the older men offered to take the infant off her hands; she had carried it in her arms until then. The kindness was more than she had looked for, and she gave the child up to him.

They went on. The man carrying the infant let himself fall back a little from the rest. When he came to a spot that suited his purpose he took the child by the feet and swung it once, the whole way round, to put his weight behind the blow, and broke its head against a tree. He left the body where it lay and rejoined the party.

The mother suspected nothing of what had happened. She watched him come up again with his arms empty, and looked at him hard, and then looked wildly round at the rest of the group. Her beloved little one was not there. The absence said what had become of it. She had the shriek in her and she held it down, because she knew that the lives of the ones still with her depended on her firmness in that hour, and she drew them closer against her and went on along that melancholy road without a word spoken or a question asked.

Out of the depths of her heart she cried to Him who is able to save, and He comforted her with hope of deliverance for the ones who were left; because she could see that if blood had been the whole object of these men, her scalp and her children's would have been taken in the yard where they were seized.

And there was the one who was plainly in command of them. In his eyes she read something more merciful than she had dared to hope for. She had particularly noticed how soothing his manner was, and how openly he favored her eldest — the little girl of whom we have spoken — and on those slender foundations she built one bright hope after another of escape, or of ransom.
