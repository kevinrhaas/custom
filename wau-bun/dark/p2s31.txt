The kindness lasted as long as it took another party to come up out of the trees. In the middle of them walked the children's mother, upright, saying nothing, and in her arms was the youngest of the family, a baby three months old. That was how the two little prisoners learned they had not been the only ones taken.

It had been an ordinary morning. Their father had gone off early with the serving-men to a raising a few miles down the valley, and the house was left without a defender in it. Nobody had thought twice about that. The country had been quiet for years — no alarms, no runners with news — and quiet gets into a household and softens it. The old precautions had been set aside one at a time until there were none left.

The men in the trees had been watching all day, and when it would cost them nothing they took the family the way you take fruit off a low branch. Later, Mrs. Lytle studied the paint on their faces and the other marks settlers had learned to read the way other people read a signature, and decided they were Senecas. She was not mistaken. They had come down from their village to fall on some isolated band of Delawares, their enemies, and had failed to find them, and made up the loss out of whatever the valley left unguarded.

That the tribe was, on the whole, a mild one, and that the chief with this party was a man of some magnanimity — those two facts, and nothing else, are why the Lytles did not end as most Americans ended who fell into the hands of the Iroquois.

From their mother the children learned what she did not know. She had no word of the other two, the boy of six and the little girl of four. She had not seen them taken. The servant-girl had gone missing at the first alarm, and the mother let herself hope that the three of them had gone together.

They halted a few hours, long enough for the prisoners' legs to come back under them, and then the march started again. One of the older men gestured toward the baby and offered, pleasantly enough, to carry it for her. She had carried it since the dooryard. The kindness was more than she expected, and she gave the child up to him.

They went on. The man carrying the infant let himself fall back from the rest, the way a man does when he is in no hurry. When he came to a spot that suited his purpose he took the child by the feet and swung it once, the whole way around, to put his weight behind the blow, and broke its head against a tree. He left the body where it lay. Then he lengthened his stride and rejoined the party.

The mother had not looked back. She saw him come up level with the others with his arms empty, and she looked at him a long moment, and then wildly around at the whole group, at every pair of arms in it. Her beloved little one was not there. The absence said it. She understood in the same breath that the cry she wanted to make would be paid for with the lives of the three still walking beside her, and she did not make it. She drew them closer against her and went on along that melancholy road, without a word spoken or a question asked.

Inside, where none of them could see it, she cried out to Him who is able to save, and something came back to her that was near enough to hope. If blood had been all these men wanted, her scalp and her children's would have been lifted in the yard where they were caught. Blood was not all they wanted. That meant there was something still to be bargained over.

And there was the one who plainly commanded them. She had been watching his eyes, because a captive learns very quickly to watch the man who decides things, and what she found in them was more merciful than she had dared hope for. He was gentle with her eldest — the little girl of whom we have spoken — and he made no secret of the preference. On that thin footing she built escape. She built ransom.
