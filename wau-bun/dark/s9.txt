Morning, and rain again. Rain, and rain, and nothing in the world but rain. It made no difference to the gentlemen. They meant to take a small boat out to the steamer and bring the luggage up whatever the weather, and among that luggage — the part of it no one could afford to lose — were several boxes of silver, the annuity money for the Winnebagoes at the Portage.

I went out onto the piazza with some of the others to watch them go. Somebody pointed across the water at Fort Howard, set on a jutting point of the far shore about three-quarters of a mile off: the old barracks, the picketed inclosure, the walls, all of it odd to my eye, and looking ancient and venerable although in fact it had been thrown up only lately. Then we all turned and found the boat again. It had the middle of the river by then. One of the passengers was standing upright in the stern, giving directions, as far as we could tell.

"That is rather a venturesome fellow," said one, "if he isn't careful he will lose his balance." The words were barely out before the man went over backward — a clean somersault — and was gone into the water.

"Oh!" cried I, "he will be drowned!"

The gentlemen laughed. "No, there he is, they are helping him in again."

The boat came about at once and brought the party back to shore. I watched them land, and it was not until one of them stepped out and came toward me streaming wet and laughing that I understood I was looking at my own peculiar property. He chose to treat the whole business as a joke. I thought it a rather sad beginning to our Western experience.

He let himself be talked into leaving his effects in the care of his friends. He changed his clothes and was settling in to stay quietly with me when a vehicle drew up at the door, and out of it came a face we both knew and were glad of — our old friend, Judge Doty.

Word of our arrival had reached him, and he had come to carry us off at once to his hospitable mansion. We wanted nothing better. We gathered up our bags and our traveling-baskets and went with him without any further ceremony.

The drive took us first along the edge of Navarino and then through Shanty-town — of the two names, only the second has been honestly earned — through mud and mire, over roads that were barely roads, up and down pitches steep enough to break a neck, until we came to our friends' little brick house. Mrs. Doty took us in with a kindness that was plain sisterly truth, and there was so much welcome in the place that we were at home in it almost at once.

They had been expecting us, and the invitations were already written out. The whole circle of Green Bay society was to be assembled to meet us at an evening party. In a new country that is simply the established way of doing honor to a guest or a stranger.

We asked after Captain Harney, who had kindly offered to bring a boat and a crew of soldiers down from Fort Winnebago to carry us up to that place, our destined home. He had not yet arrived. That left us free, and we made our arrangements for a few days of social enjoyment at "the Bay."

People shut away as these were from the world at large were glad of everything we had brought with us: the news, the particulars of how life was being lived and how people were behaving, the thousand small items no newspaper of that day dreamed of furnishing, the fashions, and the general gossip that a lady is erroneously thought to be more au fait of than a gentleman.

I remember that day chiefly as one nearly unbroken stream of talk, given and taken, with hardly a pause anywhere in it. Every one of the party except me had already made the trip — the voyage, rather — up the Fox River and down the Wisconsin to the Mississippi.

The anecdotes came mostly out of one journey in particular, made by the three of them in company with a French trader and his two sisters, who were then making their debut as Western travelers. There was Mademoiselle Julie, who would help herself, without asking anybody's leave, to a fine damask napkin or two for wiping out the ducks before they went to the fire. There was the impossibility of persuading either sister that the table apparatus ought to be washed and rinsed nicely before it was packed away in the mess-basket, so that at every meal another good napkin had to be whisked out to wipe the dishes with. And there was the young gentleman's answer to it all, which was to hunt his misappropriated articles out of every nook and corner of the boat, tie them up with a cord, and hang them over the stern to make their own way down the Wisconsin to Prairie du Chien.

Best of the lot was the story of M. Rolette himself. Somewhere on the route — crossing Winnebago Lake, I believe it was — the travelers fell in with one of the Company's boats headed down to Green Bay for supplies. M. Rolette was one of the Company's agents and the men in that boat were his employés, and he had been some weeks away from home. To meet like that on those lonely waters and trade the news was an event, and both parties felt it as one.

The boats were stopped. Greetings, in earnest. Then the questions, each one crowding the last.

" Eh bien - have they finished the new house?"

" Oui, Monsieur."

" Et la cheminée, fume-t-elle? " (Does the chimney smoke?)

" Non, Monsieur."

"And the harvest - how is that?"

"Very fine, indeed."

"Is the mill at work?"

"Yes, plenty of water."

"How is Whip?" (his favorite horse.)

"Oh! Whip is first-rate."

Store, farm, the business in every branch of it — the whole of it gone through and settled to his satisfaction, with nothing left to ask. No reason to hold the boats any longer. Time to be off.

" Eh bien - adieu! bon voyage! "

" Arrachez, mes gens! " (Go ahead, men!)

Then suddenly - " Arrêtez! arrêtez! " (Stop, stop!)

" Comment se portent Madame Rolette et les enfans? " (How are Mrs. Rolette and the children?)

The day wore itself out at last, excitement and all, and we went to our rest — thankful above everything else that General Root and his secretary were not lying close by our bed's head with their budget of political news.
