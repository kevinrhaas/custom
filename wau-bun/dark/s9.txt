The next morning it was still rain. Rain, rain, nothing in the world but rain. In spite of it the gentlemen would go out anyway, taking a small boat to row out to the steamer and bring up the luggage — and not the least important part of what belonged to us was sundry boxes of silver, for paying the annuities to the Winnebagoes at the Portage.

I went out onto the piazza with some others of the company to watch them go. A gentleman pointed out Fort Howard to me, on a projecting point of the opposite shore about three-quarters of a mile off: the old barracks, the picketed inclosure, the walls, all of it looking quaint and, considering how recently it had been put up, really ancient and venerable. Then we turned our attention back to the boat, which by that time had gained the middle of the river. One of the passengers was standing up in the stern, apparently giving some directions.

"That is rather a venturesome fellow," one of them said. "If he isn't careful he will lose his balance."

And at that moment we watched him turn a somersault backward and disappear into the water.

"Oh!" I cried. "He will be drowned!"

The gentlemen laughed. "No, there he is, they are helping him in again."

The boat's course was changed right away and the party came back to the shore. Not until one of them disembarked and came toward me, dripping and laughing, did I recognize him as my own peculiar property. He was pleased to treat the whole thing as a joke. For my part I thought it rather a sad beginning of Western experience.

He let himself be persuaded to intrust the care of his effects to his friends, and having changed his dress he prepared to stay quietly with me. Just at that moment a vehicle drove up to the door, and we recognized the pleasant, familiar face of our old friend Judge Doty.

He had heard of our arrival and had come to carry us off at once to his hospitable house. We were only too happy to gather up our bags and traveling-baskets and go with him without any further ceremony.

The drive took us first along the edge of Navarino, then through Shanty-town — the second name a far more appropriate one than the first — amid mud and mire, over bad roads, up and down hilly, break-neck places, until we reached the little brick home of our friends. Mrs. Doty received us with such true, sisterly kindness, and everything about the place was so full of welcome, that we soon felt ourselves at home.

We found that, our arrival being expected, invitations had already been prepared to assemble the whole circle of Green Bay society to meet us at an evening party. In a new country this is the established way of doing honor to guests or strangers.

We learned, on inquiry, that Captain Harney — who had kindly offered to come with a boat and a crew of soldiers from Fort Winnebago to convey us to that place, our destined home — had not arrived yet, so we felt at liberty to arrange a few days of social enjoyment at the Bay.

It was pleasant to people so cut off from the world at large to hear all the news we had brought: all the particulars of life and manners, the thousand little items the newspapers of that day never dreamed of furnishing, the fashions, and that general gossip which a lady is erroneously thought to be more au fait of than a gentleman.

I remember well that, in giving information and receiving it, the day passed in a pretty uninterrupted stream of talk. Everyone in the party but me had made the trip — the voyage, rather — up the Fox River and down the Wisconsin to the Mississippi.

There were plenty of anecdotes of one particular trip the three of them had made in company with a French trader and his two sisters, then making their debut as Western travelers. How Mademoiselle Julie would borrow, without asking, a fine damask napkin or two to wipe out the ducks before cooking them. How impossible it was to persuade either sister of the propriety of washing and rinsing the table apparatus nicely before packing it away in the mess-basket, the consequence being that another good napkin had to be whisked out stealthily to wipe the dishes when the hour for meals came. And the fun of the young gentleman hunting up his stray articles, so misappropriated, from the nooks and corners of the boat, tying them together with a cord, and hanging them over the stern to make their own way down the Wisconsin to Prairie du Chien.

Then there was a capital story of M. Rolette himself. At one point on the route — I think it was in crossing Winnebago Lake — the travelers met one of the Company's boats going down to Green Bay for supplies. M. Rolette was one of the agents of the Company, and the people in that boat were his own employés. After an absence of some weeks from home, a meeting on those lonely waters and the exchanging of news was, of course, an occasion of great excitement on both sides.

The boats were stopped. Earnest greetings were interchanged. Question followed question.

"Eh bien — have they finished the new house?"

"Oui, Monsieur."

"Et la cheminée, fume-t-elle?"

"Non, Monsieur."

"And the harvest — how is that?"

"Very fine, indeed."

"Is the mill at work?"

"Yes, plenty of water."

"How is Whip?"

"Oh! Whip is first-rate."

Everything, in short, about the store, the farm, the business of various descriptions, having been satisfactorily gone over, there was no occasion for further delay. It was time to proceed.

"Eh bien — adieu! bon voyage!"

"Arrachez, mes gens!"

Then, suddenly — "Arrêtez! arrêtez!"

"Comment se portent Madame Rolette et les enfans?"

That day and all its excitement was over at last, and we went to our rest, thankful that we did not have General Root and his secretary at our bed's head with their budget of political news.
