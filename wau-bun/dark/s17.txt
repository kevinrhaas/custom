The camp we made that night was the finest of the whole journey. The men had been worked hard before noon, so we took only a short stage after it — seven or eight miles of pulling, no more — and put in a little below the "little Butte," where the timber opened into a clearing that might have been cut for us.

The soldiers went at encamping now like men with a trade. They felled a big tree for the fire and set about cooking food enough for the next day's run, which was to be a long one: twenty-one miles, straight across Winnebago Lake. Our Frenchmen did the same. Then the fire found the light dry grass we were sitting in the middle of, and in a moment the ground all around us was lit and crackling.

The wind was steady enough to carry the flames one way only, and there was too little to burn for anybody to be frightened by it. We climbed onto the fallen logs and gave that small blaze the grand title of "a prairie on fire." The real loss was that it took every blade of dry grass with it. The night before I had pushed armfuls of that grass under the bear-skin in my tent, and it had made all the difference to my rest.

Supper was a happy business that evening, eaten at the sunset hour under the high trees, with the water going by and murmuring to itself. Later the moon came up broad and white and poured its light over the whole of it, a country wild and empty and beautiful in its emptiness, and it seemed to me that the friends I had left behind had better reason to envy me than to pity me.

But nothing keeps, not even that, and at last my raptures over the scenery ran out with everything else. I shut my tent, unwillingly, and lay down close against the fall of the canvas, where I could lift it a little and look out at the night whenever I chose.

After a time the camp went quiet. The men had stopped talking and seemed to have gone to their blankets, and we were on the very edge of sleep when a heavy step came up outside, and then a blunt voice with it.

"Mr. Kinzie! Mr. Kinzie!"

"Who is there? What is it?"

"I'm Krissman, didn't you mean, sir, that the men should have any liquor tonight?"

"Of course I did. Hasn't Kilgour given out your rations?"

"No: he says you didn't say anything particular about it, and he wasn't coming to ask you if you forgot it, but I thought I wouldn't be bashful - I'd just come and ask."

"That is right. Tell Kilgour I should like to have him serve out a ration apiece."

"Thank you, sir," in the cheerfullest voice imaginable, "I'll tell him."

Krissman was becoming a character among us.

A few miles of rowing the next morning brought us to Four-Legs' village, at the mouth of Winnebago Lake — a handsome scatter of Indian lodges standing about on a green glade under tall, fine trees.

We were properly in the Winnebago country now. The odd name of the place, I soon learned, came from the head chief of the nation, whose home it was. Nobody was there; they had gone off, in all likelihood, to their wintering grounds. Here we parted with our friend Wish-tay-yun, whose own country ended where this one began.

"Bon-jour, Chon!" (John:) "bon-jour, maman." A hard shake of the hand and that was his goodbye. We pushed off into the lake and left him smoking his kin-nee-kin-nick, waiting for the spirit to move him before he set out at his long Indian trot for home in the Menomonee country.

Our good weather seemed to leave with him. The sky, clear and mild for days, closed over, and instead of the pleasant crossing we had promised ourselves on that silver water we stayed shut up under canvas, putting our heads out now and then to catch what we could of the country through the pouring rain.

It was no more than an October day owed us, but we were unreasonable enough to find it tedious. To pass the hours, and to make my disappointment a little smaller, my husband told me something of his early life — a story that belonged, as it happened, to the name Four-Legs.

He was living then at Prairie du Chien, in the service of the American Fur Company. The chiefs and their people came down from the Upper Mississippi to sell their furs and peltries and to buy what they wanted — merchandise, ammunition, trinkets, and the rest.

Like everyone not yet seasoned to that country, he took the chills and fever. One day, in a fit of the fever worse than any before it, a chief of the Four-Legs family — brother to the one whose village we had just passed — came into the Company's warehouse to trade. There is no ceremony among the Indians, and no restraint either. Hearing that Shaw-nee-aw-kee was sick, Four-Legs went straight to him to say how sorry he was and to tell him what he ought to be taking for it.

Anyone who has had the ague, and the fever that comes up behind it, knows what it is to be talked at by a man who cannot see that he should stop. The longer the old chief went on, the higher the fever climbed, and the worse the ache grew in his head and his back and his limbs.

At last the old man arrived at his point. It was not good, he said, for a young man to be sick and far from his family with no home and no wife. He had a daughter at home, a fine one — handsome, healthy, a capital nurse, and the best hand in the whole tribe at trapping beaver and musk-rats. He would be coming down again in the spring, and he would bring her with him, and then Shaw-nee-aw-kee could see for himself that nothing had been overstated. Should he go now, and bring his daughter next time?

Beaten flat by that babble, wanting nothing on earth but quiet, poor Shaw-nee-aw-kee agreed to all of it eagerly, and the chief went away.

The fever ran so close to delirium that the whole conversation went out of his head and stayed out for months. It came back to him on the day Four-Legs walked in with a woman well past her youth and, for ugliness, a perfect Hecate. She had an enormous pack of furs across her shoulders, and she came at him with a lurching, criss-cross walk and dropped the pack at his feet — which, by Indian custom, announced exactly what she understood herself to be to him.

The talk with her father came back to him whole, and with it the understanding that he was going to need some skill to get himself out of where he now stood.

He told one of the young clerks to take up the pack and carry it into the magazine where the furs were stored. Then he went on talking with the chief about matters of no consequence, quite coolly.

Miss Four-Legs sat a while with a sulky, ill-used look on her, and at last it came out of her. "Humph! he seems to take no more notice of me than if I was nobody!"

He turned to the clerk again. "Give her a calico shirt and half a dozen bread-tickets."

Her face did not clear. Seeing that he would have to open the subject himself, the father said, "Well, I have brought you my daughter, according to our agreement. How do you like her?"

"Ah, yes - she is a very nice young woman, and would make a first-rate wife, I am sure. But do you know a very strange thing has happened since you were here? Our father, Governor Cass, has sent for me to come to Detroit, that he may send me among the Wyandottes and other nations to learn their customs and manners. Now, if I go, as I shall be obliged to do, I shall be absent two or three years, - maybe four. What then? Why, the people will say, Shaw-nee-aw-kee has married Four-Legs' daughter, and then has hated her and run away from her, and so everybody will laugh at her, and she will be ashamed. It will be better to take some good, valuable presents, blankets, guns, etc., and to marry her to one of her own people, who will always stay by her and take care of her."

The old man was shrewd, and shrewd enough to see that what remained to him was the best bargain he could strike. It cost a round sum, I have no doubt, to settle the thing to the satisfaction of the injured damsel, though I have never been able to learn how much. What I do know is that the young gentleman took care never again to close a bargain in a fit of the ague. And the lady up on the Mississippi is called by his name to this day, in derision.

Halfway down the lake we passed Garlic Island, a lovely place saddled with an unlovely name. It belonged, with the village on the shore opposite, to "Wild Cat" — a fat, jolly, good-natured man, nothing whatever like the formidable animal he was named for.

He and his band were away, like their neighbors at Four-Legs' village, so there was nothing to break the sameness of the sail. It was too wet for singing. The men sat in their overcoats looking like drowned chickens, and had to work their oars unusually hard simply to keep warm, which likely spared them some of the dead weight of that cold, raining October day. We, who had nothing to do, felt it more.

Toward evening the sun came out. We had passed into the Fox River and were coming into that pretty little widening called Butte des Morts Lake, at the far end of which we were to camp for the night.

The shallows along the shore were green with wild rice, and the gathering of it, just at that season, is one of the great labors of the Indian women. They push their canoes into the thick of the stand, bend the heads down over the side with their paddles, and beat the ripe husks off the stalks into a cloth laid ready in the bottom. Afterward the grain is rubbed to free it from the husk, fanned in the open air, put into cordage bags and packed away for the winter. It is longer and more slender than Carolina rice, greenish-olive in color, and though it makes a pleasant enough dish it is far from nutritive. They are fond of it as a soup, with birds or venison added.
