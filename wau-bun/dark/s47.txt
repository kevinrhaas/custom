Where the name Chicago came from is still argued over. Some of the Indians derive it from the fitch, or polecat; others from the wild onion that once grew thick in these woods. But all of them agree on the one point: the place took its name from an old chief who was drowned in the stream in former times. Carefully as the tradition has been kept, that drowning must belong to a very remote period, and there is an old French manuscript, brought over from France by General Cass, that makes it evident.

The paper purports to be a letter from M. de Ligney, at Green Bay, to M. de Siette, among the Illinois, and it is dated as early as 1726. In it the place is designated Chica-goux. The same orthography turns up in old family letters from the beginning of the present century.

When the Indians give the early history of Chicago, they say it with great simplicity: the first white man who settled here was a negro.

They meant Jean Baptiste Point-au-Sable, a native of St. Domingo, who about the year 1796 found his way into this remote region and began a life among the Indians. There is usually a strong affection between the two races, and Jean Baptiste imposed upon his new friends by making them believe he had been a great chief among the whites. Perhaps he was disgusted at not being elected to a similar dignity by the Pottowattamies. At any rate he left this vicinity, and ended his days at Peoria under the roof of his friend Glamorgan, another St. Domingo negro, who had got large Spanish grants in St. Louis and the country around it, and who at one time was in the enjoyment of an extensive landed estate.

Point-au-Sable had made some improvements at Chicago. A Frenchman named Le Mai took possession of them and began trading with the Indians. After a few years Le Mai's establishment was purchased by John Kinzie, Esq., who was living at that time at Bertrand — Parc aux Vaches, as they called it then — near Niles, in Michigan. For nearly twenty years, the military excepted, this gentleman was the only white inhabitant of Northern Illinois, and some particulars of his early life may not be uninteresting.
