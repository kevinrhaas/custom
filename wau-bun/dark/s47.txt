Where the name Chicago came from is still argued over. Some of the Indians trace it to the fitch, the polecat; others to the wild onion that once grew thick all through these woods. On one point nobody disagrees: the place took its name from an old chief who drowned in the stream in former times. Tradition has kept that death carefully, but it must belong to a very remote period, and there is a paper to prove it — an old French manuscript that General Cass carried back from France.

It offers itself as a letter. M. de Ligney writes from Green Bay; M. de Siette, off among the Illinois, is the man he writes to; and the date on it is as early as 1726. The place is set down there as "Chica-goux." That same spelling turns up again in family letters from the opening years of the present century.

When the Indians give the early history of Chicago, they give it with great simplicity: "the first white man who settled here was a negro."

His name was Jean Baptiste Point-au-Sable. St. Domingo was where he came from, and about the year 1796 he made his way into this remote region and began a life among the Indians. Between those two races there is usually a strong affection. Jean Baptiste imposed on his new friends by letting them believe that among the whites he had been a "great chief." Perhaps it galled him that the Pottowattamies never raised him to any such dignity; at any rate he left the vicinity, and he ended his days in Peoria, sheltered by a friend named Glamorgan — another man out of St. Domingo, of his own color, who had come into large Spanish grants at St. Louis and in the country round it, and who for a time had an extensive landed estate to his name.

Point-au-Sable had made some improvements at Chicago, and those a Frenchman named Le Mai took possession of, and began trading with the Indians there. The place did not stay his either. A few years on, John Kinzie, Esq., bought Le Mai's establishment out. Mr. Kinzie was then living at Bertrand — Parc aux Vaches, they called it in those days — near Niles, in Michigan. Except for the military, that gentleman was the only white inhabitant of Northern Illinois for nearly twenty years, so a few particulars of his early years may be worth having.
