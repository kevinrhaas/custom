In 1816 Mr. Kinzie brought his family back to Chicago once more. A new fort went up on the old ground, somewhat larger than the one it replaced. The bones of the Americans massacred four years earlier were not gathered up and buried until after the troops came back.

An Indian Agency was established there under the charge of Charles Jewett, Esq., of Kentucky. In 1820 Dr. Alexander Wolcott, of Connecticut, took his place, and he held the office until he died in 1830.

The garrison was emptied of troops in 1823 and filled again in 1828, after the Winnebago war. That trouble had broken out between the Winnebagoes and the white settlers on and near the Mississippi. Murders were done, and the young chief Red Bird was taken and held at Prairie du Chien to wait for his trial, and there he killed himself — the chagrin of it, and the confinement, which he could not bear. There was real fear that the Pottowattamies would throw in with the Winnebagoes and set the whole frontier alight. What most likely stopped it was the work of Billy Caldwell, Robinson, and Shaw-bee-nay, who went out among the Rock River bands and reasoned with them, band by band, until they agreed to stay quiet.

Chicago in those years held very few citizens, and their days went by much alike, one after another. Game was everywhere for the taking and the ground they broke was so rich that garden, corn-field, and dairy gave them more of every good thing than they could use. A friend back in the East countrie once put the obvious question to them.

"How do you dispose of all the good things you raise? You have no market?" "No." "And you can't consume them all yourselves?" "No." "What then do you do with them?"

"Why, we manage, when a vessel arrives, to persuade the captain to accept a few kegs of butter, and stores of corn and vegetables, as a present, and that helps us to get rid of some of our overplus."

Mail came in, as anyone would guess, only at long and uncertain intervals. Sometimes it came up from Fort Clark, at Peoria; more often from Fort Wayne, or else across the peninsula of Michigan, which was in those days a wilderness with savages living in it. The hardy man who carried it had more than once to do as the birds of heaven do and lodge among the branches, that being the only way to keep himself and his charge safe through the night.

Visitors were scarce. Now and then a friend would come and stay for months at a stretch and take up the life of the woods along with them. Now and then a traveler on his way to or from parts unknown would turn up at the door, and a traveler could count on a warm and open-handed welcome.

One such gentleman, coming north from the southern settlements, reached Wolf Point late in the evening. There was a small trading-establishment there then, kept by George Hunt and a Mr. Wallace. He stopped and asked whether they could put him and his horse up for the night. They told him they were poorly fitted to entertain a stranger — the house was small, and they were keeping bachelor's hall.

"Is there no place," asked the traveler, "where I can obtain a lodging?"

"Oh, yes - you will find a very comfortable house, Mr. Kinzie's, about half a mile below, near the mouth of the river."

He pulled his horse's head around and rode the way they had pointed.

At the house, the first thing he asked was, "Is this the residence of Mr. Kinzie?"

"Yes, sir."

"I should be glad to get accommodation for myself and horse."

"Certainly, sir - walk in."

Someone led the horse off to the stable and the gentleman was shown into a parlor where two ladies were sitting. They went through the usual opening questions and answers, people in a new country getting acquainted quickly, and before very long he was sitting down to a hot supper, and we may safely call it a fine one, the table in that house having always had a certain reputation.

He appeared to think well of it, because he settled in thoroughly. After tea he called for a boot-jack and pulled off his boots. The ladies were somewhat taken aback. But they had been living a long while outside the world, and they could not be sure what etiquette had been doing in their absence.

Before he went up for the night he let them know what he would like for his breakfast, and his breakfast was made accordingly. The next day came on stormy. He was content where he was, and having first satisfied himself that his horse wanted for nothing and was in no way neglected, he passed the day quite comfortably.

When he tired of his book he would talk a while with the family. He showed no inclination to keep himself apart. He unbent, in fact, into a decent sociability, considering they were all of them out there in the woods together.

On the second day it cleared. The traveler announced that he would be going. He ordered his horse brought around to the door, and then he asked for his bill.

"My house isn't a tavern, sir," was the astounding reply.

"Not a tavern! Good heavens! have I been making myself at home in this manner in a private family?"

He apologized at length and there was no need of a word of it. The family had understood his mistake from the moment he walked in, and had spent the whole of his visit privately enjoying the thought of his face when he finally found out.

It was in that same year, 1816 — the year the fort was raised again after the Indians destroyed it — that the Pottowattamies ceded to the United States the tract of land Chicago stands on, and the country around it. For twenty years more they went on living there peaceably. Not until 1836 did the Government remove them to the lands set aside for their use on the Upper Missouri.

In 1830 Commissioners appointed by the State came and laid the town of Chicago out into lots. A lot cost, in those days, somewhere between ten and sixty dollars.

Mr. Kinzie had always said what this place would become. He knew where it sat on the map and he knew the richness of the country behind it, and he never doubted that prosperity and importance were coming to it. He was not allowed to see any of it proved. He closed his useful and energetic life on the 6th of January, 1828, having just finished his sixty-fifth year.
