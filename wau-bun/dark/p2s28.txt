In 1816 Mr. Kinzie and his family came back to Chicago. The fort was rebuilt, a little larger than the one that had stood there before. And it was not until the troops returned that anybody gathered up the bones of the Americans who had been killed four years earlier, and buried them.

An Indian Agency was established under the charge of Charles Jewett, Esq., of Kentucky. In 1820 he was succeeded by Dr. Alexander Wolcott, of Connecticut, who held the post until his death in 1830.

The troops were withdrawn from the garrison in 1823 and put back in 1828, after the Winnebago war. That was a disturbance between the Winnebagoes and the white settlers on and near the Mississippi. After some murders had been done, the young chief Red Bird was taken and held at Prairie du Chien to wait for his trial, and there he killed himself, out of chagrin and the irksomeness of confinement. The fear afterward was that the Pottowattamies would make common cause with the Winnebagoes and open a general season of havoc and bloodshed along the frontier. What probably stopped it was the work of Billy Caldwell, Robinson and Shaw-bee-nay, who went out among the Rock River bands and talked and argued and persuaded them into staying quiet.

The few citizens of Chicago in those days lived a very quiet, unvaried life. Game was everywhere and the land they farmed was so fertile that they had more of every luxury of garden, corn-field and dairy than they knew what to do with. A friend in the "East countrie" once put the obvious question:

"How do you dispose of all the good things you raise? You have no market?" "No." "And you can't consume them all yourselves?" "No." "What then do you do with them?"

"Why, we manage, when a vessel arrives, to persuade the captain to accept a few kegs of butter, and stores of corn and vegetables, as a present, and that helps us to get rid of some of our overplus."

The mails came at very rare intervals, as may be supposed. Sometimes they were brought from Fort Clark, at Peoria, but more often from Fort Wayne, or across the peninsula of Michigan, which was still wilderness and still peopled with savages. The hardy man who carried the express often had to do what the birds of heaven do and lodge among the branches, to keep himself and his charge safe through the night.

Visitors were very rare — unless it was a friend come to stay for months and share the life of the place. Now and then a traveler did find his way to the spot, passing to or from parts unknown, and any such man was certain of a hospitable and hearty welcome.

A gentleman coming up from the southern settlements arrived late one evening at Wolf Point, where George Hunt and a Mr. Wallace kept a small trading-establishment. He stopped and asked whether he could be accommodated for the night, himself and his horse. They told him they were ill provided to entertain a stranger — the house was small, and they were keeping bachelor's hall.

"Is there no place," the traveler asked, "where I can obtain a lodging?"

"Oh, yes — you will find a very comfortable house, Mr. Kinzie's, about half a mile below, near the mouth of the river."

The stranger turned his horse's head and rode the way they had pointed.

He came to the spot and his first question was: "Is this the residence of Mr. Kinzie?"

"Yes, sir."

"I should be glad to get accommodation for myself and horse."

"Certainly, sir — walk in."

His horse was taken to the stable. He was shown into a parlor where two ladies were sitting. They went through the usual preliminary questions and answers, since in a new country people get acquainted fast, and before long the gentleman found himself sitting down to a comfortable hot supper — a fine supper, we will venture to say, the table in that house having always been somewhat famous.

He appeared to enjoy it. He made himself quite at home. After tea he called for a boot-jack and drew off his boots. The ladies were a little surprised. But they had been out of the world a good while, and they did not know what changes in etiquette might have taken place during their retirement.

Before he went up for the night he let them know what he would like to have for breakfast, and it was duly prepared. The next day came on stormy. The gentleman was satisfied with his quarters, and having taken care to ascertain that there was no neglect or deficiency of accommodation where his horse was concerned, he got through the day very comfortably.

Now and then, when he tired of reading, he would converse with the family, and seemed on the whole not at all disposed to hold himself aloof, but to indulge in a little becoming sociability, seeing they were all out there together in the woods.

The second day the weather cleared. The traveler announced that he would be leaving. He ordered his horse to the door. Then he called for his bill.

"My house isn't a tavern, sir."

"Not a tavern! Good heavens! have I been making myself at home in this manner in a private family?"

He was profuse in his apologies, and they were entirely unnecessary. The family had understood his mistake from the first hour. They had spent his whole visit quietly amusing themselves with the thought of the moment he would find it out.

It was in 1816 — the same year the fort was rebuilt, after the Indians destroyed it — that the tract of land Chicago now stands on, and the country around it, was ceded to the United States by the Pottowattamies. They remained the peaceful occupants of it for twenty years more. Not until 1836 were they removed by the Government to lands set aside for their use on the Upper Missouri.

In 1830 the town of Chicago was laid out into lots by Commissioners appointed by the State. The lots sold then for something between ten and sixty dollars.

Mr. Kinzie had always said what this place would become. He knew where it sat on the map and he knew the country behind it, and he had predicted its eventual prosperity and importance for years. He did not live to see one line of it come true. He closed his useful and energetic life on the 6th of January, 1828, having just completed his sixty-fifth year.
