The payment was over. The Indians had scattered to their wintering grounds and the traders had gone too, carrying off a good share of the silver, in exchange for which each family had provided itself, so far as it could, with clothing, guns, traps, ammunition and the rest of what a winter takes. The Indians are good at a bargain. They are not easily overreached. They know an exorbitant charge the moment they hear one, and a trader who tries his shrewdness on them is certain to be given an expressive nickname that sticks to him ever after.

M. Rolette, for instance, they called Ah-kay-zaup-ee-tah — five more — because, they said, whatever number of skins a man offered him for an article, his terms were invariably five more.

A lady once said to him, "Oh, M. Rolette, I wouldn't be engaged in the Indian trade, it seems to me a system of cheating the poor Indians."

"Let me tell you, madame," he replied, with great naïveté, "it isn't so easy a thing to cheat the Indians as you imagine. I have tried it these twenty years, and have never succeeded!"

We settled down after that into a quiet, domestic life. The military system marked out the whole of it. The bugle-call at reveille, answered by a very good band; the light, animated strains for sick-call, and, shortly after, for breakfast; the longer ceremony of guard-mounting; the Old English Roast-Beef to announce the dinner-hour; the sweet, plaintive Lochaber no more at retreat, followed most incongruously by The Little Cock-Sparrow; and at last the long, rolling tattoo late in the evening. The day came to us in pieces, and with books and music and drawing on top of the household work, the time flew faster than it had ever gone before. It was Sunday when I most missed my Eastern home. I had planned out beforehand what we should do on the first return of that sacred day under our own roof. At the very least, I told myself, we shall have the Sabbath's quiet and repose; and I can, among other things, do poor Louisa some good by giving her additional lessons of a serious character.

So while she was taking away the breakfast-things I said to her, "Now, Louisa, get your work all finished, and everything put neatly aside, and then come here to me again."

"Yes, ma'am."

We sat down to our books. We read and waited. We waited and read another hour. No Louisa.

There was music and the sound of voices on the parade in front of our windows, but that did not disturb us. We were used to it every day of our lives.

At last I had to go down and see what was keeping my damsel. The breakfast-things stood where she had set them on the table. The kettles and the spider were on the hearth. The fire was out. The kitchen was empty.

Coming back through the hall, which runs the whole length of the house and opens at the front onto the parade, I saw a group collected out in the area, of all shades and colors, and in the middle of it one round woolly head I could not possibly mistake, bobbing up and down, now on this side and now on that, while peals of laughter came out of the whole group at once.

"Louisa," I called. "Come here. What are you doing there?"

"Looking at inspection."

"But why aren't your breakfast-things washed, and your kitchen swept?

Did I not tell you I wished you to come up and learn your lessons?"

"Yes, ma'am, but I had to see inspection first. Everybody looks at inspection on Sunday."

I found it was useless to expect anything more of Louisa than an afternoon's lesson, and with that I had to content myself.

It seemed to me that it would be pleasant, and perhaps profitable, if all the inmates of the garrison were to assemble on that day, and one of our number be found who would read a portion of the church-service, with a sermon out of one of our several selections.

I approached the subject cautiously, with an inquiry to this effect:

"Are there none among the officers who are religiously disposed?"

"Oh, yes," replied the one I put it to, "there is S — —, when he is half tipsy, he takes his Bible and 'Newton's Works,' and goes to bed and cries over them, he thinks in this way he is excessively pious."

S — — was among the officers who had never visited us. It seemed fair to infer that if his religious principles did not correct his own evil habits, they would not do much toward improving anybody else's, and that there was no use calling in his co-operation for any scheme for a better observance of the Lord's day.

We had to content ourselves with writing to our friends in the East, to interest them in getting a missionary sent out to us who could officiate as chaplain in the garrison — a plan the officers seemed to favor. The hope of any united religious service was, for the present, laid aside.

The post-surgeon got a furlough about then, and his place was filled by Dr. Newhall, of Galena; and with the addition of his gentle, quiet wife, our circle of ladies was enlarged to three. Here we were, in a wilderness. And yet how contented and happy.

The gloom that replaced that tranquillity came to the door on its own legs. A Frenchman named Letendre presented himself one day without warning. He had come from Chicago, and he brought the distressing intelligence that our dear relative Dr. Wolcott was extremely — indeed hopelessly — ill. My husband began at once to prepare for instant departure. I begged to be allowed to go with him, but he meant to travel at a speed that made it impossible, and he refused my entreaties. Within a few hours his provisions and horses and everything else necessary for the trip were ready, and he set off with Petaille Grignon, his usual attendant on such expeditions, leaving Letendre to follow when he had recovered from his fatigue.

The hours of his absence were sad and dreary ones, for all the kind efforts of our friends to cheer me. In a few days another messenger came in with the news of the fatal termination of Dr. Wolcott's illness. That noble heart, so full of warm and kindly affections, had ceased to beat, and sad and desolate indeed were those who had so loved and honored him.

My husband came back as soon as he could possibly leave the family, and it was fortunate he had delayed no longer, because the winter was setting in now, and setting in with severity.

Our quarters were spacious, but they had been built out of green trees cut down in the forest and sawed into boards by the hands of the soldiers, and green wood shrinks and warps. It left a great many yawning crevices. Stuffing the cracks with cotton batting and pasting strips of paper over them was the employment of many a leisure hour.

Then there were the chimneys. In spite of all the currents of air moving through those rooms, which might have been expected to create a draught, the chimneys had a sad habit of smoking. To remedy it, a couple of gun-barrels were sawed off, by order of the commanding officer, and set into the hearth, one on each side of the fire-place, in the hope that air from the room below would help carry the smoke up where it belonged.

The next morning after this had been done, Louisa was washing the hearth.

"Pray, ma'am," she said, "what are these things put in here for?"

I explained what they were for.

"Oh, I am so glad it is only that! Uncle Ephraim (Major Twiggs's servant) said they were to be filled with powder and fired off Christmas Day, and he was terribly afraid they would blow the house up, and we in it."

Ephraim was a most faithful and valuable servant, and he often amused himself by playing on the credulity of the younger portion of the colored fraternity.

"Is it true," Louisa asked me another day, "that Pillon and Plante were once prairie-wolves?"

"Prairie-wolves! what an idea! Why do you ask such a foolish question?"

"Because Uncle Ephraim says they, and all the Frenchmen about here, were once prairie-wolves, and that, living so near the white people, they grow, after a time, to be like them, and learn to talk and dress like them. And then, when they get to be old, they turn back into prairie-wolves again, and that all the wolves that the officers bait with their dogs used to be Frenchmen, once."

After a while I stopped correcting Uncle Ephraim's stories. I was arriving, by slow degrees, at the conviction that my little colored damsel was by no means so simple and unsophisticated as she would have me believe, and that the person being imposed upon in that house was myself.

The snow that winter was prodigious and the cold was intense. Water would freeze in our parlors a very short distance from the fire. The fatigue-parties kept the halls filled with wood almost to the ceiling, but that could not counterbalance the inconvenience of having the wide doors standing open to the outer air a great part of the day while they carried it in. We Northerners ought to have had wood-houses built for the purpose, and ought not only to have kept our great hall-doors shut but to have protected them with a hurricane-house besides. But the Florida frontier is not a climate in which our Southern bachelors could have picked up any knowledge that would be of use when the thermometer stands at twenty-five degrees below zero — a point at which the brandy congealed in the sideboard.
