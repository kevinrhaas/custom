The payment was over and the Indians had scattered to their wintering grounds. The traders went too, carrying off a good share of the silver; against it every family had laid in what it could — clothing, guns, traps, ammunition, whatever else the winter would call for. The Indians are hard to get the better of in a bargain. They know an exorbitant price the moment they hear one, and a trader who tries his shrewdness on them earns a name for it, an apt one, which stays on him ever after.

M. Rolette, for one, they called "Ah-kay-zaup-ee-tah," which is to say five more. The reason they gave was that a man might offer him any number of skins he pleased for an article and the terms would come back the same every time: "five more"

A lady said to him once, "Oh, M. Rolette, I wouldn't be engaged in the Indian trade, it seems to me a system of cheating the poor Indians."

He answered her with great naïveté. "Let me tell you, madame, it isn't so easy a thing to cheat the Indians as you imagine. I have tried it these twenty years, and have never succeeded!"

We settled into a quiet domestic life. The military system divided the day up for us: the bugle-call at reveille, with a very good band after it; the light, quick strains for sick-call, and a little later for breakfast; the longer business of guard-mounting; "Old English Roast-Beef" to say that dinner was ready; at retreat the sweet, plaintive "Lochaber no more," followed as incongruously as could well be managed by "The Little Cock-Sparrow"; and late in the evening the long roll of tattoo. Between those markers, with books and music and drawing on top of the household work, the hours went faster than they ever had at home. Sunday was the day I missed my Eastern home most. I had settled well in advance what we should do when the first of them came round in a house of our own. "We shall have at least," I said to myself, "the Sabbath's quiet and repose, and I can, among other things, benefit poor Louisa by giving her some additional lessons of a serious character."

So, as she was clearing away the breakfast-things, I told her, "Now, Louisa, get your work all finished, and everything put neatly aside, and then come here to me again."

"Yes, ma'am."

We opened our books. We read, and waited. We read another hour and waited. No Louisa.

There was music out on the parade below our windows, and voices with it, but we heard as much every day of our lives and thought nothing of it.

In the end I had to go down myself and find out what was keeping my damsel. The kitchen was empty. The breakfast-things stood on the table still, the kettles and the spider still on the hearth, and the fire had gone out.

I went back into the hall, which runs the whole length of the house and opens at the front on the parade, and there was a crowd gathered in the area, of every shade and color. In the middle of it was one round woolly head I could not possibly mistake, bobbing to this side and then to that, and peals of laughter were coming out of the whole group.

"Louisa," I called, "come here. What are you doing there?"

"Looking at inspection."

"But why aren't your breakfast-things washed, and your kitchen swept?

Did I not tell you I wished you to come up and learn your lessons?"

"Yes, ma'am, but I had to see inspection first. Everybody looks at inspection on Sunday."

An afternoon lesson was plainly all I was ever going to get out of Louisa, and I made up my mind to be content with that.

It struck me that it would be a pleasant thing, and perhaps a profitable one, if everybody in the garrison gathered together on that day. Somebody among us, surely, would be willing to read out a portion of the church-service, and a sermon chosen from one of our different selections.

I opened the subject carefully, by way of a question:

"Are there none among the officers who are religiously disposed?"

"Oh, yes," came the answer from the one I had asked, "there is S - -, when he is half tipsy, he takes his Bible and 'Newton's Works,' and goes to bed and cries over them, he thinks in this way he is excessively pious."

S - - was one of the officers who had never called on us. If his religious principles had done nothing to correct his own evil habits, it was fair to suppose they would not do much toward improving anybody else's, and there seemed no use in asking his help with any scheme for keeping the Lord's day better.

So we wrote to our friends at the East instead, and asked them to take up the business of getting a missionary sent out who could serve as chaplain to the garrison. The officers appeared to like the plan. For the present we set aside any hope of united religious services.

The post-surgeon took a furlough, and Dr. Newhall, of Galena, came out in his place; with his gentle, quiet wife added to us, our circle of ladies came to three. A wilderness — and yet how contented and happy we were.

The gloom that ended that enviable quiet arrived in the person of a Frenchman named Letendre, who presented himself one day without warning. He had come from Chicago to tell us that our dear relative Dr. Wolcott was gravely ill; hopelessly ill, in fact. My husband began at once to get ready to leave. I begged him to take me, but he meant to travel too fast for that and had to refuse me. Within a few hours the provisions and the horses and everything else needed for the trip were assembled, and he rode off with Petaille Grignon, who always attended him on such expeditions, leaving Letendre to follow when he had recovered from his fatigue.

The hours of his absence were sad and dreary ones, for all that our friends did to cheer me. Within a few days another messenger came with word that Dr. W.'s illness had ended fatally. That noble heart, full of warm and kindly affection as it was, had stopped, and those who had loved and honored him were left sad and desolate indeed.

My husband came back as soon as he could leave the family, and it was well he did not put it off any longer, because the winter set in then, and it set in hard.

Our quarters were spacious, but the soldiers had built them out of green trees felled in the forest and sawed into boards by hand, and the boards shrank and warped and left gaping cracks everywhere. Many a leisure hour went into stuffing them with cotton batting and pasting paper strips over the top.

The chimneys smoked, too, and steadily, for all the currents of air that might have been expected to give them a draught. The commanding officer's remedy was to have a pair of gun-barrels sawed off and set into the hearth, one at each side of the fire-place, on the theory that air coming up out of the room below would help push the smoke where it belonged.

Louisa was washing the hearth the next morning, after they had been put in.

"Pray, ma'am," she said, "what are these things put in here for?"

I explained their use.

"Oh, I am so glad it is only that! Uncle Ephraim (Major Twiggs's servant) said they were to be filled with powder and fired off Christmas Day, and he was terribly afraid they would blow the house up, and we in it."

Ephraim was a most faithful and valuable servant, and he entertained himself by playing on the credulity of the younger sort among the colored fraternity.

"Is it true," asked Louisa, one day, "that Pillon and Plante were once prairie-wolves?"

"Prairie-wolves! what an idea! Why do you ask such a foolish question?"

"Because Uncle Ephraim says they, and all the Frenchmen about here, were once prairie-wolves, and that, living so near the white people, they grow, after a time, to be like them, and learn to talk and dress like them. And then, when they get to be old, they turn back into prairie-wolves again, and that all the wolves that the officers bait with their dogs used to be Frenchmen, once."

In time I gave up setting Uncle Ephraim's stories straight. I was coming round by degrees to the view that my little colored damsel was nothing like as simple and unsophisticated as she wished me to believe her, and that the one being imposed upon was me.

The snow that winter was prodigious and the cold was intense. Water froze in our parlors a very short way from the fire. The fatigue-parties kept the halls stacked with wood nearly to the ceiling, but that was no answer to having the wide doors standing open on the outer air for a great part of the day while they carried it in. Northerners as we were, we ought to have had wood-houses built for the purpose, and kept the great hall-doors shut, and put a "hurricane-house" over them besides. But the Florida frontier is no place for a Southern bachelor to pick up the sort of knowledge that comes in useful when the thermometer stands at twenty-five degrees below zero — the point at which the brandy in the sideboard congealed.
