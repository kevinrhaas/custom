The next morning came up bright and lovely, which for that season of the year was a gift, and we said our good-byes to Mr. and Mrs. Dixon in high spirits. For the first few miles our way lay along the banks of the Rock River, which rise and fall in long easy swells, and we held always eastward, on the beaten path — road, rather — that runs down to Fort Clark, or Peoria. The Sauk trail, we had been told, cut across this road about six miles out.

When we had covered, by our own reckoning, the whole of that distance, a trail opened in front of us bearing northeast, and we halted to argue whether this was the one we wanted.

My husband thought not. It leaned too far north for his liking, and it was faint — far fainter than any path could be that was trodden every year by so great a body of Indians on their journeys.

Plante had no doubt whatever. This was the exact place where he and "Piché" had come out upon the great road the year before, on their way to Port Winnebago. "On that very rising-ground at the point of woods, he remembered perfectly well stopping to shoot ducks, which they ate for their supper."

Mr. Kellogg would commit himself to nothing. He agreed with whoever had spoken last.

Plante was "the guide," and so sure of himself that it was settled we should follow him — though not without grumbling from the bourgeois, who kept calling a halt to reopen the whole question.

"Now, Plante," he would say, "I am sure you are leading us too far north. Why, man, if we keep on in this direction, following the course of the river, we shall bring up at Kosh-ko-nong, instead of Chicago."

"Ah! mon bourgeois," the Canadian would answer, light as ever, "would I tell you this is the road if I weren't quite certain? Only one year ago I traveled it, and can I forget so soon? Oh, no - I remember every foot of it."

What convinced Monsieur Plante at last was the water swinging away ahead of us in the river's great bend, and the bold rocky bluffs standing over it.

"Are you satisfied now, Plante?" my husband asked him. "By your leave, I will now play pilot myself." He quit the trail there and set our course as nearly due east as he could hold it.

The weather had turned by then, and turned hard; the cold was intense. Even supposing we were right at last, the days we had thrown away were no small matter. We had taken on nothing more in the way of provisions at Dixon's, choosing to load what forage we could carry for the horses instead, since the picking around our camping-grounds was thin and never made them a proper meal. Still, the belief that we had found the right path at last carried us along, and we rode until dark, when we made a comfortable "encampment" at the edge of a grove beside a little stream.

I have never known cold like that night. Salted provisions were what I was used to, and they left me with a thirst past bearing, so my husband had got into the way of setting the little tin coffee-pot full of water at the head of my bed when we lay down. That night it was a solid block of ice long before midnight. Inside the tent, wrapped up as we were in blankets, we took no harm at all. Outside the tent the air was pitiless.

March 15th. — The bourgeois had us up and packing at the first show of light. The Sauk trail had to be found today, whatever it cost us. And if it were not found — what then? No one asked it aloud. No one had an answer to give.

A little way from camp the ground softened under the horses and we were into marsh country. Wild geese, brant and ducks went up out of it screaming in their thousands, and the farther lakes and ponds lay black with them. The night's cold had frozen the shallow water we were trying to cross, but not enough: the ice would not carry a horse, and it cut their feet and ankles at every step as they broke through. Now and then we could not get forward at all and had to turn back and work our way round the head of the marsh — so that on top of everything else we carried the knowledge that we were laboring all day long and going nearly nowhere.

We were clear of that swampy country at last, and onto firmer footing, mostly open prairie. But the trouble only changed its shape. The weather moderated, and with it came a snow-storm that blinded us. We had no trail we could trust and no compass; the sun was the one guide left to us, and now the air had thickened until not a man among us could have said which quarter of the heavens he stood in.

We kept moving, and our track must have wandered badly. Many miles on we crossed an Indian trail cut deep into the ground, running square across the line we had been holding. The snow gave out, the cloud thinned enough to show us where the sun was, and we saw that this new trail ran north and south. Now what? Hold to the east, or take the path, on the chance it would carry us into the road we had spent so many days hunting? And if we took it, which way — up or down? My husband wanted south. By his reckoning we were still too far north, somewhere about the Grand Marais, or Kish-wau-kee. Mr. Kellogg and Plante both wanted north, Plante insisting that his bourgeois had overshot to the south already, that we must by now be somewhere near the Illinois River. Outvoted, my husband gave way, and we turned the horses' heads north with his whole will against it. A few miles of that was all he could stand. "You may go north, if you please," he said, "but I am convinced that the other course is right, and I shall face about - follow who will."

So we came about and rode south, and the miles were long and heavy, and the only thing that broke them was poor Mr. Kellogg's fretting. "I am really afraid we are wrong, Mr. Kinzie. I feel pretty sure that the young man is right. It looks most natural to me that we should take a northerly course, and not be stretching away so far to the south."

My husband gave no sign of hearing any of it. The Frenchmen said nothing. They would have parted with their right hands sooner than set themselves against the bourgeois once he had spoken his decision, and it would never have entered their heads to volunteer an opinion or a remark unless they were asked for one.

For mile after mile the road ran on through prairie, and then it bent round a point of woods and put us, without any warning, at the edge of an Indian village. The whole party gave a shout. Nothing answered it. We galloped up among the wigwams and not a voice called back to us, not a dog barked. The place was as still as a grave. We rode round it and round it, then got down and put our heads into several of the big huts. They had stood empty a long while. Bare bark walls, and nothing else — every article of furniture stripped out and carried off by the owners to their wintering-grounds, to be brought back in the spring when they came again to lay out their corn-fields and take up their summer cabins.

What that did to us I will not try to set down. We got back into the saddle heavy-hearted and went on, and the snow began again, and made a bad situation worse. In time we halted for the night. We had known for some while that our provisions would not stretch to another day, and here we were — no one could say where — with woods and prairie all round us, a long way from any human home, and food enough for one thin supper.

The dogs came whining about us for their share, as they always did, and got a bare bone between them. We lay down that night knowing that if we were not hungry yet, we would be hungry by morning.

Morning came. Plante and Roy had a good fire going and a nice pot of coffee ready, and that was breakfast, for when the bag was shaken out and turned inside out it gave up three crackers and nothing more, and the others would not hear of my leaving them; I was to put them in my pocket for my dinner. Mr. Kellogg touched me very much then. He went into his wallet and brought out a piece of tongue and a slice of fruit-cake, which he said "he had been saving for the lady since the day before, for he saw how matters were a going."

Poor man. He would have done better to take my husband's advice at the start and lay in a larger store for himself. What he had brought ran out early on the second day, and for the last two meals he had been boarding with us.
