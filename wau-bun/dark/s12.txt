Before we left there was room in the calendar for one more party, and this one was to be a "real Western hop." Nobody who went to that dance at Mrs. Baird's ever forgot it. Everybody on Green Bay came — that was the way people said it then, on the Bay, not at it — young and old alike, the whole settlement turned out. The young officers came up from Fort Howard and looked very smart in their uniforms. Finery that had lain folded away for years was brought out again, because every one of us meant to do the strangers honor by appearing at his absolute best. It was agreed beforehand that nothing given there had ever equalled it. The house was taken over from end to end. Mr. B.'s office made one wing, and the desks and the seats in it were carried out without any ceremony at all to give the dancers more floor. The front rooms were put in order, and so was the kitchen, since a primitive sort of visitor did now and then appear who would rather sit quietly back there and smoke his cigar than stand about with the company. In those days that was an emergency you had to be ready for.

There was no holding down the mirth of that room. Nothing restrained anybody but good manners; nobody was weighed down with conventionalities. It was the plain, warm, hearty enjoyment that pleasant hospitable people can raise between them when there is just enough of the French element in the house to put an edge on any amusement. In a country that new, where what counted at a gathering was the number of the company rather than the quality of it, the circle could not honestly be called a select one.

I knew that going in. So when a clumsy little man with a broad red laughing face came waddling across the floor to the chair I had dropped into after a dance, I was more amused than surprised by what he said.

" Miss K - -, nobody hain't never introduced you to me, but I've seen you a good many times, and I know your husband very well, so I thought I might just as well come and speak to you - my name is A - -."

"Ah! Mr. A - -, good-evening. I hope you are enjoying yourself. How is your sister?"

"Oh! she is a great deal worse - her cold has got into her eye, and it is all shot up."

Then he turned bodily on a lady[4] seated near me, young, beautiful, quick, sparkling with wit and with humor that was entirely her own.

"Oh! Mrs. Beall," he began, "what a beautiful gown you have got on, and how handsome you do look! I declare you're the prettiest woman in the room, and dance the handsomest."

"Indeed, Mr. A - -," replied she, putting down her love of fun and arranging her face into demureness, "I am afraid you flatter me."

"No, I don't - I'm in earnest. I've just come to ask you to dance."

That is what it costs to be too charming.
