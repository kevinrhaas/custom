In the meantime we had leisure for one more party, and this one was to be a real Western hop. Everybody will remember that dance at Mrs. Baird's. All the people, young and old, who could be gathered from anywhere throughout the country — or, as the fashion was to express it, on Green Bay — were assembled there. The young officers came up from Fort Howard, looking so smart in their uniforms. Treasures of finery that had gone uncalled-for a long while were brought back into the light. Everybody was bound to do honor to the strangers by appearing in their very best, and the entertainment was to be unequalled by any given before it. The whole house was put in requisition for the occasion. Desks and seats were unceremoniously dismissed from Mr. B.'s office, which formed one wing, to make more space for the dancers. Not only the front portion of the house but even the kitchen was made fit for the reception of company, in case some primitive visitor, as sometimes happened, should prefer to sit down quietly in there and smoke his cigar. In those days that was an emergency you always had to provide for.

Nothing could exceed the mirth and hilarity of the company. There was no restraint but good manners, no excess of conventionalities — only genuine, hearty good-humor and enjoyment, the kind that pleasant, hospitable people with just enough of the French element in them to add zest to any amusement can furnish, to make an entertainment agreeable. In a country so new, where the number of the company at a social gathering mattered more than the quality, the circle was not always, strictly speaking, select.

I knew that, so I was more amused than surprised when a clumsy little man with a broad, red, laughing face waddled across the room to where I had taken my seat after a dance, and addressed me in this fashion.

"Miss K——, nobody hain't never introduced you to me, but I've seen you a good many times, and I know your husband very well, so I thought I might just as well come and speak to you — my name is A——."

"Ah! Mr. A——, good-evening. I hope you are enjoying yourself. How is your sister?"

"Oh! she is a great deal worse — her cold has got into her eye, and it is all shot up."

Then he turned full upon a lady sitting near me, radiant with youth and beauty and sparkling with wit and genuine humor.

"Oh! Mrs. Beall," he began, "what a beautiful gown you have got on, and how handsome you do look! I declare you're the prettiest woman in the room, and dance the handsomest."

"Indeed, Mr. A——," she replied, suppressing her love of fun and assuming a demure look, "I am afraid you flatter me."

"No, I don't — I'm in earnest. I've just come to ask you to dance."

Such was the penalty of being too charming.
