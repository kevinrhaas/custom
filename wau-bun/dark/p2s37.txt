Four years had gone by since little Nelly was carried off. Her heart was warm and affectionate by nature, and warmth of that kind answers what it is given; the people she lived among had been unstinting with her, and she had paid them back in the same coin. She loved the chief and his mother, and something in her feeling for them went past love into reverence. She had taken up their language and their customs so completely that she had all but lost her own.

She belonged to the tribe now. Her old home and her own family had gone dim in her — all of it except her mother. Her mother had not faded at all. That love had been fierce in her from the beginning, as everything in her was fierce, and no amount of time or change was ever going to touch it.

Then came the peace of 1783 between Great Britain and the United States. A general pacification of the Indian tribes followed it, and in the hearts of Mr. and Mrs. Lytle the old hope stood up again.

They moved the whole family to Fort Niagara. Not far from it, on the American side, burned the Great Council-Fire of the Senecas. Colonel Johnson took up the negotiation again willingly, and, to leave no chance of success unused, he went once more in person to the village of the Big White Man.

He could not have chosen a better week. He arrived in the middle of the Feast of the Green Corn — an observance so like the Jewish Feast of Tabernacles that it has helped persuade many people, along with other of their customs, that the Indian nations are what became of the lost ten tribes of Israel. Everything else in the village stopped for it. The people gave the days over to one another, out in the open air or under arbors built of green branches, and everyone came out in gala-dress. The little adopted child wore a petticoat of blue broadcloth bordered with gay-colored ribbons and, over it, a sack of black silk carrying three rows of silver brooches — the center row large, running from her throat to the hem, the rows from either shoulder no bigger than a shilling-piece and set as close together as they would go. Around her neck were more strings of white and purple wampum than anyone could have counted, worked out of the inner surface of the muscle-shell. Her hair was clubbed behind and heavy with beads of every color. Scarlet cloth leggings, and moccasins of deer-skin embroidered with porcupine-quills, finished her.

Colonel Johnson was received with every consideration owed to his position, and to the long friendship between him and that tribe.

He could see that the festival had warmed and opened all of them, and he used it. In an interview with the chief he spoke about what the child's father and mother had done for love of her — given up their friends, given up their home, come hundreds of miles into this country on the single hope of one day looking at her and putting their arms around her. The chief listened, and the chief softened. He would come to the Grand Council soon to be held at Fort Niagara, on the British side of the river, he said, and he would bring his little sister with him.

He wanted something in return, and he named it plainly. Not only was no effort to be made to reclaim the child; no proposal to part with her was to be put to him at all.

The day came, and Nelly was lifted onto a horse to ride with her Indian brother to the Great Council of the Senecas, and her heart went the whole way at a gallop. She had given him her promise, long before, that she would never leave him without his permission. He had her word, and he rode easy on it.

Band after band of chiefs and warriors came in to meet their Father, the agent, at the council-fire — and with every one of them the parents' hearts went up and came down again. The officers of the fort had given them quarters for as long as this took. The ladies of the garrison, who had taken the whole business to heart, walked out with the mother to the place of council and stood there with her, watching for the first sign of the band from the Alleghany River.

At last it was there, coming out of the forest on the opposite side, the American side. The commanding officer sent boats across for the chief and his party. The father and mother went down to the grassy bank with all the officers and ladies behind them and waited, and they had seen it in the first glance across the water: the little captive was with them.

At the boat the chief stopped and spoke to some of his young men. "Stand here with the horses, and wait until I return."

He was told the horses could be ferried over with him and looked after on the other side.

"No," he said. "Let them wait."

He kept hold of his darling's hand as the boat went out. He held it the whole way across, and while the river went under them, and until the bow came against the bank — and until the child tore loose and ran forward into the arms of the mother she had been taken from.

The chief watched that happen, and he could hold out no longer.

"She shall go," he said. "The mother must have her child again. I will go back alone."

He made one silent gesture of farewell, turned, and stepped down into the boat. Nothing anyone said could get him to stay for the council — not argument, not entreaty. On the far bank he swung up onto his horse, and he and his young men were gone into the depths of the forest.

They stayed a few weeks at Niagara. Then Mr. Lytle began to think about what a man's resolution is worth after the man has gone home alone, and about what might be done to take his child a second time, and he decided to move again. He crossed Lake Erie with his family at the first opportunity and settled near Detroit, and there he lived out the rest of his life.

Little Nelly never saw her friend the chief again. She never forgot him either. To the day of her death she spoke of her brother the Big White Man with tenderness and gratitude, and of her friends and playfellows among the Senecas.
