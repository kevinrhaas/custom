One of the first women I came to know was the wife of Wau-kaun-zee-kah, the Yellow Thunder. She had gone east with her husband when he was one of the deputation that visited the President, and ever since then she had been known as the Washington woman. There was a pleasant, old-acquaintance air about the way she greeted me, as much as to say, you and I have both seen something of the world. Not one word of surprise or admiration got past her lips. Her companions exclaimed over everything I showed them and clapped their hands with a childlike, laughing simplicity, and she looked on with a deportment that said plainly, yes, yes, my children, I have seen all these things before. What broke her was a shell. I had a little cabinet of them, and I held a tropical one up to her ear, and she heard the murmur come out of it. She poked her finger into the opening to get at the animal inside. She shook it hard. She put it to her ear again. And then she burst out in a hearty laugh and set it down, admitting with her face what she would not say, that this was past her understanding.

I had one shell of peculiar beauty, the favorite of the whole collection, a small conch covered over with rich dark veins. Every one of the visitors took that shell up in turn, and said and signed her admiration of it, plainly showing that she had an eye for beauty. This was on the occasion of the parting visit of my red daughters.

Shortly after the payment was finished and the Indians had gone, I found that my valued shell was missing from the collection. Could one of the squaws have taken it? It was possible. They will sometimes do such things, though rarely, under a strong enough temptation. I went back over the party in my mind, trying to fix on the one who looked most likely to have been the culprit. It could not have been the Washington woman. She was partly civilized. She knew better.

A few weeks afterward Mrs. Yellow Thunder appeared again. She was carrying a gay-colored chintz shawl rolled up in her hand, and she unfolded it carefully, and took out the shell, and laid it on the table in front of me. I could not decide whether to let my face show displeasure at the trick she had played me or joy at having my treasure back, and settled at last on the policy of showing no emotion at all.

She stayed on until my husband came in, and he then questioned her about the matter.

"She had taken the shell to her village, to show to some of her people who did not come to the payment."

"Why had she not asked her mother's leave before carrying it away?"

"Because she saw that her mother liked the shell, and she was afraid she would say, No."

That was not the first time Madame Washington had displayed the shrewdness that was the ruling trait of her character. During the visit of the Indians to the Eastern cities they were taken to all sorts of exhibitions, museums, menageries, theatres. It did not escape their notice that a little silver always changed hands before anyone went in, and they asked why. It was explained to them. The woman brightened up like a person who has just been handed an idea.

"How much do you pay for each one?"

Her Father told her.

"How do you say that in English?"

"Two shillings."

"Two shinnin — humph." Good.

The next day, when visitors began flocking as usual to the rooms where the Indians were staying, the woman and a young Indian she had taken on as confederate posted themselves by the door and kept it shut. Whenever anyone knocked, the door opened a cautious few inches, and a hand came out of it, and the woman said, "Two shinnin."

It was readily paid every time, and the game went on until she had accumulated a considerable sum. But that did not satisfy her. At the first attempt of a visitor to leave the room, the door was held close as before, the hand was put out, and "Two shinnin" met his ear again. He tried to explain that having paid to come in, he must be allowed to go out free. She gave an innocent shake of the head. "Two shinnin" was all the English she could understand.

The Agent had come in a little while before, and had overheard the whole of it, and had been sitting behind his newspaper laughing and waiting to see how it would end. He now came forward and interfered, and the guests were permitted to go out without a further contribution.

The good woman was also admonished that it is far from the custom of white people to tax their friends and visitors in this manner, and that the practice must be laid aside in future.

Another instance of the disposition of the Indians to avail themselves of every good thing fortune throws in their way was the following.

On the same trip, while they were passing through Ohio, one of the party asked the Agent, "Do you pay for all those provisions that are set before us at the hotels?"

"Yes. Why do you ask?"

"Nothing: I thought you maybe paid for just what we ate of them."

At the next stopping-place a fine breakfast was set on the table, and they partook of it plentifully, as usual. Just as they had finished, the horn sounded for everyone to take their places in the stage-coaches. Every man sprang to his feet. One seized the plates of biscuits and poured them into the corner of his blanket. Another took the remains of a pair of chickens. A third emptied the sugar-bowls. Each one laid hold of whatever was nearest him, and in a trice there was nothing left on that table but empty plates and dishes. The landlord and the waiters stood watching it and laughing, enjoying the trick as much as any of the spectators.

On another occasion their Father had tried to impress upon them the unseemliness of throwing their refuse pieces and bones and fragments of food about on the table-cloth, pointing out the orderly manner of the whites at table and the propriety of keeping everything neat and nice around them.

At their next meal they were served first with a chicken-pie, of which they ate very heartily, so that the accumulation of bones on their plates was very abundant. Then another and more favorite dish appeared — a fine, large, roasted turkey. A gentleman sitting near it was evidently preparing to carve. There was no time to lose. But what was to be done with the bones? They looked around them in some perplexity. A large apple-pie stood close by. The most eager of them drew it toward him and, quick as thought, laid every bone on top of it; then, with a triumphant laugh at the happy idea, he coolly transferred the bird to his own dish and carried it off to distribute among his companions. The amazed stranger soon joined in the laugh at the unceremonious manner in which his share of the dinner had vanished.
