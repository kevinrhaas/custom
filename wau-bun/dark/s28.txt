One of the first women I came to know was the wife of Wau-kaun-zee-kah, the Yellow Thunder. She had gone east with her husband when he went as one of the deputation to visit the President, and ever since she had been called "the Washington woman." She greeted me pleasantly, the way one old acquaintance greets another, as much as to say that she and I had both seen something of the world. While her companions went through my things with a childlike, laughing simplicity, crying out and clapping their hands at every wonder I brought forward, she let nothing at all cross her face. Her whole bearing said it for her: yes, yes, my children, I have seen these things already. The apathy held until I took a tropical shell out of the little cabinet I kept them in and held it to her ear. She heard the murmuring inside it. She pushed her finger down into the opening, after the animal inside it. She shook the shell hard. She put it back to her ear and listened again. Then she laughed out loud, and set it down, and her face admitted freely that this one was past her.

There was one shell in the collection I loved above all the others, a small conch with rich dark veins running through it. Every woman there picked that one up in her turn, and every one of them said and gestured how beautiful she found it; they had an eye for it, plainly. This was on the day my red daughters came to take their leave of me.

The payment was finished soon after and the Indians went away, and it was then I noticed the conch was gone from the cabinet. Had one of the squaws taken it? It was not impossible. Under a strong enough temptation they would do such a thing now and then, though rarely. I went back over the faces of that afternoon, trying to fix on the likeliest one. Not the Washington woman, certainly. She was partly civilized, and knew better.

Some weeks later Mrs. Yellow Thunder walked in again with a gay-colored chintz shawl rolled up in her hand. She unfolded it carefully, took out my shell, and set it down on the table in front of me. I could not decide whether my face ought to show displeasure at the trick or gladness at having my treasure back, and settled at last on showing nothing whatever.

She stayed on and on, until my husband came home, and then he asked her about it.

"She had taken the shell to her village, to show to some of her people, who didn't come to the payment."

"Why had she not asked her mother's leave before carrying it away?"

"Because she saw that her mother liked the shell, and she was afraid she would say, No."

That was not the first proof I had of Madame Washington's shrewdness, which was the leading thing in her character. On the trip to the Eastern cities the party had been taken about to exhibitions, museums, menageries, theatres, and it did not get past them that a piece of silver changed hands at every door before anyone was let in. They asked why. It was explained. At that the woman brightened, like a person who has just had an idea.

"How much do you pay for each one?"

Her Father told her.

"How do you say that in English?"

"Two shillings."

"Two shinnin - humph" (good).

The next day the visitors came flocking as usual to the rooms where the Indians were lodged, and found the door shut. The woman had posted herself beside it with a young Indian for a confederate. At each knock the door was opened cautiously, a hand came out, and a voice said, "Two shinnin."

Every caller paid without argument, and it went on that way until she had gathered up a considerable sum. Even that was not enough for her. When the first visitor made a move toward the door to go, the door was held fast, the hand came out again, and again he heard "Two shinnin." He tried to explain that a man who has paid to come in goes out for nothing. She shook her head, all innocence. "Two shinnin" was the whole of the English she could understand.

The Agent had come in a little while before and heard the entire performance from behind his newspaper, where he sat laughing and waiting to see how far it would run. Now he stepped in and put an end to it, and the guests were let out without any further contribution.

The good woman was told, besides, that white people do not tax their friends and visitors in this fashion, and that she would have to give the practice up.

Here is another instance of how readily the Indians helped themselves to whatever good things fortune laid in their path.

On that same journey, somewhere in Ohio, one of the party put a question to the Agent. "Do you pay for all those provisions that are set before us at the hotels?"

"Yes. Why do you ask?"

"Nothing: I thought you maybe paid for just what we ate of them."

At the next stopping-place a handsome breakfast was laid out, and they ate of it plentifully, as they always did. They had just finished when the horn blew for everyone to take their seats in the stage-coaches. Every man was on his feet at once. One took up the plates of biscuits and poured them into a corner of his blanket. Another took what was left of a pair of chickens. A third turned out the sugar-bowls. Each of them laid hands on whatever stood nearest, and in a trice the table held nothing but empty plates and dishes. The landlord and the waiters watched the whole thing and laughed as hard as anybody else in the room.

Another time, their Father had done his best to impress on them how unseemly it was to drop their bones and scraps and leavings about on the table-cloth. He pointed out how orderly white people were at table, and how proper it was to keep everything neat and clean about one.

At the very next meal they were served a chicken-pie first, and ate it with a will, and the bones came up in great numbers on their plates. Then a second dish arrived, one they liked better still — a fine large roasted turkey. A gentleman sitting near it had taken up the carving knife. There was no time to lose, and there was still the question of the bones. They looked about them, at something of a loss. A large apple-pie stood within reach. The quickest of them pulled it over, and in the space of a thought every bone was piled onto it, and, laughing in triumph at his own happy idea, he lifted the bird calmly onto his own dish and carried it off to share out among his companions. The astonished stranger was soon laughing too, at the very unceremonious way his share of the dinner had disappeared.
