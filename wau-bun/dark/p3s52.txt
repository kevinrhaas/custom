One day, while I was doing this, a face appeared at the window that I had once known well. It was the pretty daughter of the elder Day-kau-ray. She used to visit us often, and would watch everything we did with great interest — our sewing, our weeding and cultivating in the garden, our reading. Of the reading I had tried many times to give her some idea, showing her the plates in the Family Bible and doing my best to explain them. But of late I had quite lost sight of her. Now, how changed she was, how wan. I said what I always said to her — "Tshah-ko-zhah?" — what is it — and she gave a sigh that was nearly a sob. She did not beg. Her face said the whole of it.

I took up my dish and handed it out to her, and I expected to see her fall on the contents where she stood. She did not. She took it and made signs that she would soon come back, and walked away with it. When she brought it back to me I was almost certain she had not tasted a morsel of it herself.

Oh, the boats. The boats with the corn in them. Why did they not come? We wrote, and we sent, and we begged them to hurry, and everything and everybody in those unenterprising times moved so slowly that it made no difference at all. We could be certain only of this: that the boats would come when they were ready and not one moment before.
