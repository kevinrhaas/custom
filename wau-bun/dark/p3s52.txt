One day, while I was at that work, a face came to the window that I had known well once. It was the elder Day-kau-ray's pretty daughter. She used to come to us constantly and watch whatever we were doing with great interest — our sewing, our weeding and cultivating in the garden, our reading. That last I had tried many times to give her some notion of, opening the Family Bible to the plates and explaining them as well as I could. But I had lost sight of her for a long while. And how changed she was now. How wan. I said to her what I always said - " Tshah-ko-zhah?" (What is it?) - and what came back was a sigh with a sob most of the way inside it. She asked me for nothing. Her face said the rest.

I picked up my dish and put it into her hands. I expected her to fall on it. She did not. She took it, made signs that she would come back soon, and walked away with it. When she brought it back to me I was nearly certain she had not swallowed a mouthful herself.

Oh, those boats. The corn was on them. Why would they not come? We both wrote, and we sent word to hurry them, and it changed nothing — in those unenterprising times nothing and nobody could be hurried along. All we could be certain of was that the boats would come when they were ready, and not one moment sooner.
