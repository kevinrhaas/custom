Of all the women of the tribe we came to know in those first months, our favorite was a daughter of one of the Day-kau-rays. That family, as I have said elsewhere, claimed a cross of French blood somewhere back up the line, which may explain her fair skin and the soft curl in her hair. She had a fine broad forehead, full expressive eyes, good teeth. She had not gone brown and haggard with age the way the women of her people usually did. Except for one feature, you would have called her beautiful.

Years before, she had married a Mus-qua-kee, a Fox Indian, and by the custom that holds among all the tribes it was the husband who came home to the wife's family, so he had been living among the Winnebagoes.

It is this custom, the exact reverse of ours, that makes the birth of a daughter something to be congratulated on in an Indian family. She will bring another hunter to our lodge — that is how they say it to one another.

The Mus-qua-kee stayed on among his wife's relations for a few years. No children came, and after a while he had had enough of these new people of his and wanted to go back to his own. For a time he worked on his wife to leave her home and go with him to the Mississippi, where the Sauks and Foxes lived. She would not. She could not make herself do it.

One day, after a great many attempts that came to nothing, he lost his temper.

"Then, if you won't go with me," he said, "I will leave you, but you shall never be the wife of any other man — I will mark you!"

And then he was on her, and he bit off the end of her nose. That is the usual punishment for infidelity in a wife, and it is the worst disgrace she can carry, because it shuts her out of marriage for the rest of her life. He ran off to his own people afterward. His revenge did not take, though. Day-kau-ray was too widely known and too thoroughly respected for any shame to stick to a member of his family, and this bright, warm creature in particular won everyone she met on the first meeting. She certainly had us from the start.

She suffered badly from rheumatism, and a remedy we gave her relieved her almost entirely, after which her gratitude had no bottom to it. Long pain had left her partly crippled, but that did not stop her walking the whole ten miles from the Barribault as often as once every two or three weeks just to see us. Then she would sit and look at us, and laugh out loud with a child's delight at anything new or strange we happened to be doing, and pat us and stroke us whenever we came within reach, and sometimes lift one of our hands or arms and kiss it. That was how she showed love. We loved her back. It was always a happy shout in that house when somebody looking out along the Portage road called, "The Cut-Nose is coming!" In time, though, we learned to call her by her baptismal name, Elizabeth, because she too was one of Mr. Mazzuchelli's converts.
