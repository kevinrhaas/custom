Spring came on, and with it the thing we had spent the whole winter dreading. We learned how bad it had become from the ones who came to the door — small parties, a few at a time, asking for food.

Their Father gave out rations for as long as there were rations to give. But the stores in the Commissary Department had run down so far that Colonel Cutler could not in conscience authorize more than the barest relief, and that only where the case was already desperate.

We had been careful with our own stores since the first cold, afraid of reaching the bottom of the flour and the meal before more could be brought up from "below." We had even bought a quantity of sour flour that the commissary had condemned; with enough saleratus worked into it and a fair proportion of potatoes, it made a bread we were glad enough to eat. But party after party came and stood before us and told us plainly what they were enduring, and we gave, and gave again, until the day came when there was nothing left in the house to hand over.

The half-breed families near us had done as we had, sharing while their own stock lasted, and now they too were obliged to refuse. Their women came often to sit with us and repeat what was being carried in from the wintering grounds. The winter had been an open one. The snow had never lain deep enough at any time for the Indians to track deer, and in any case there were no deer; the troops and the war-parties had swept the country clean the summer before.

They were dying in whole companies, we heard, of nothing but want of food, and lying stretched where they fell along the road to the Portage, which they had been trying to reach, dragging frames that had no strength left in them. For weeks together some had lived on soup boiled from the bark of the slippery elm, or on stewed acorns.

Our own food came now as a daily ration from the garrison; matters had reached the point where a barrel of flour could not be had at one time. When a meal was finished I went into the pantry and gathered every last particle that remained, and set it aside, because someone would be at the door needing it, and someone always was.
