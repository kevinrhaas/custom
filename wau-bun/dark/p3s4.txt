"Whoever pleases may take my place in the carriage," our mother said. "I have traveled so many years on horseback, that I find any other mode of conveyance too fatiguing."

And then, at sixty years of age, she went up onto sister Margaret's pacer with the ease of a girl of sixteen.

Lieutenant Foster had gone from us early that morning — he had to rejoin his command — and now the Sau-ga-nash, having seen us ready to start with a clear sky over us and everything right and tight for the trip, said his good-byes and turned back toward Chicago.

We rode on through a beautiful country, glade and forest by turns, until we came to the Fox River. The current ran clear over its bed, rippling as it went, and as we started down the steep bank toward the water the question came up that always comes up in a country you do not know. Is it fordable?

Petaille knew this ground. Petaille had not yet caught up to us. Lecuyer knew nothing whatever about it. The troops had plainly gone ahead of us by this same trail — true enough, but the troops had been on horseback. The thing in doubt was whether we could get the carriage over. And understand, the doubt was never about how deep the water was. It was about what lay underneath it, and whether that would hold.

We agreed that two or three riders should try it first. My mother, Lecuyer, and I went across slowly, each of us picking a different place to come out on the far bank, so as to find the firmest footing. The bottom was hard and good almost the whole way. Near the shore it gave a little under the hooves. Then one more step and each of us stood on dry ground.

"Est-il beau?" my husband called. He was driving.

"Oui, monsieur."

"Yes, John, come just here, it is perfectly good."

"No, no — go a little farther down. See the white gravel just there — it will be firmer still, there."

That was the sort of direction he was getting, all of it at once and none of it agreeing. He took the second. One step short of the bank, both horses went down until there was hardly more of them showing than their backs.

The white gravel was not gravel. It was a bed of yellow clay, and the water lying over it had made it gleam like something solid.

The horses were half suffocated with mud and water and they fought the harness in a frenzy. My husband went out of the seat and onto the pole. "Some one give me a knife," he shouted. I was in the water again at once, and I went as close as I dared and put mine into his hand, out of the scabbard I wore around my neck.

"Whatever you do, don't cut the traces," his mother cried.

He cut through some of the side-straps. He had worked his way out to the very end of the pole and was reaching forward to get at the head-couplings when one horse gave a wild plunge and the other reared and went over almost onto its back. My husband was between them. For a moment we thought he was gone, trampled down under them. Then he came up out of it, so covered in mud and water that we could barely make him out. Harry was near him, and Harry went forward along the pole with the quickness of a cat, and with the sharp jack-knife he already had open he cut the straps that held their heads.

By then both horses were lying floating on the surface, one of them to all appearances dead and the other gasping what looked like its last breath. But the instant they felt their heads come free, both of them made another furious effort together to get loose from the pole, which still held them by the neck-strap.

That failed, so they tried something else. With a few well-judged twists and turns they wrenched the pole apart, and finally carried it off across the river in triumph and up the bank, where they stood together and waited to see what we intended to do next.

Here was a predicament. A few hours earlier we had thought ourselves badly used because a few of our horses had gone missing. This was worse. The wagon was standing in the river, the harness was cut to pieces and had then been carried off in the most independent manner by Tom and his companion, the pole was twisted into fragments, and there was not so much as a stick on our side of the water to make another one out of.

Then a whoop came from the far bank, and two or three good ones went back to it from our party, and it was Petaille Grignon, come at last. He got down and took charge of the horses, who were standing under a shady tree resting after their exertions, and by that time Lecuyer had crossed over and the two of them brought the delinquents back.

Meanwhile we had been doing what we could for our sister Margaret. She had been in the wagon when it went down, with her little son Edwin, and it had taken some work to get the two of them out and carried to shore on horseback. The shock of it brought on a fit of the ague, and she lay now in the long grass of the little prairie along the bank, blue and shaking. The tent had been packed at the back of the wagon and was too soaked with mud and river water to shelter anyone; all we could do was spread it in the sun to dry. We opened an umbrella over her head. Then we started on the question of repairs. First the wagon needed a new pole, which meant the master and the men had to recross the river and pick out an iron-tree in the forest.

Then the harness. Someone had had the foresight to put a little box under the wagon seat with an awl in it, and waxed ends, and a number of other small things exactly suited to a situation like this one.

It went question and answer, like Cock Robin:

"Who can mend the harness?"

"I can, for I learned when I was a young girl to make shoes as an accomplishment, and I can surely now, as a matter of usefulness and duty, put all those wet, dirty pieces of leather together."

So we all sat down on the grass in the shade of the only two umbrellas we could muster between us.

I stitched away steadily at it. I will admit that I blistered my hands in no small degree.

They had brought a suitable young tree back across, and the hatchets — nobody travels in the woods without them — were shaping it into a pole when a peculiar hissing noise started up, and the cry went up at once: "Un serpent sonnette! A rattlesnake!"

Every one of us came to our feet, even the poor shaking invalid, and we were up just in time to see the thing slide past within three inches of my mother's feet, while the men fell on the place it had left with whips and missiles and anything else that would add to the general commotion.

That little incident turned out to be an excellent cure for the ague. One excitement drives out another, and on that homoeopathic principle sister Margaret came along so well that by the time the day's mischiefs were all repaired she was ready to take her place in the cavalcade, as bright and cheerful as the rest of us.
