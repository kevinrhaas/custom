That night we were to camp somewhere short of the Four Lakes. The heat had taken everything out of us, and the day's riding had taken the rest, and we watched the ground ahead for the low clumps of willow and alder that meant water was under them. None of us had another hour in us. We could have pushed on to the bank of the nearest lake, but the lake lay off our line, and off our line was a luxury we could not afford.

It was late when we found a place that would do. We told each other, more than once, that this was the last night we would sleep on the road. Tomorrow we would be at Winnebago.

The whole trip had been a pleasure — one long stretch of high spirits, and even the mishaps, which had been maddening while they lasted, had turned into things we laughed about afterward. Still, people are made the way they are made. We were ready to trade all that excitement for a quiet room and a door that shut.

The next morning's ride was calmer than any that had gone before it, because early in the day we came out onto what everyone called the Twenty-mile Prairie, and I will say here that miles on the prairie are longer than miles anywhere else. Crossing it was like crossing a desert with the sand taken out. Mile after mile of open ground, unbroken. Not a tree. Not one living thing in sight except ourselves.

The sun seemed to be making up for two months of hiding, and it burned twice as hard for the arrears. You cannot carry an umbrella on horseback. The ones in the wagon could, and did.

Our mother's strength had held her in the saddle every day until this one. On this one it simply ran out, and she gave up her place on little Brunet to sister Margaret.

So we rode, one low knoll lifting past another, and from the top of each of them in turn we looked for the far line of timber that was, to us, the promised land.

"Take courage," came the encouraging word, again and again, "very soon you will begin to see the timber."

Another hour would drag itself past.

"Now, when we reach the rising ground just ahead, look sharp."

We looked sharp. The same landscape, unvarying, out to every edge of the world.

There were not even streams out there to touch the thirst that fatigue and impatience had built in us.

Then a whoop from Shaw-nee-aw-kee broke the silence we had been riding in.

"Le voilà!" (There it is!)

Our unpractised eyes could not find it at first — a faint blue strip laid along the horizon — and then we could, and it grew, and went on growing, and as it grew the fatigue and the thirst and the misery went out of us in exact proportion.

We were in high spirits by the time we came in under Hastings's Woods, a fine, tall forest with a clear, quick stream running through it.

The green shade and the cold water were food and drink to us, but we did not let ourselves forget that the day was moving on, and that we meant to be in before sunset. So we made ourselves get up and go, when another hour under those trees would have been the easiest thing in the world.

The marsh at Duck Creek, after a week of that heat, was nothing like what it had been a few months earlier, when I had gone down into its ice water.

We crossed it without trouble and came out on the bank of the creek itself.

The stream was held to be fordable at that point, and in any case most of our party would not have cared much either way. To the ladies the question seemed to want a little more attention than that.

"This water looks very deep — are you sure we can cross it on horseback?"

"Oh, yes! Petaille, go before, and let us see how the water is."

Petaille went. He was mounted on a horse built like a giraffe, and by sticking his legs straight out to either side he got across with hardly a splash on him.

The water was plainly going to come into the wagon. That mattered less, since at worst the passengers could climb up and stand on the seats.

My horse, Jerry, was a good deal taller than average, so I was over quickly, with no trouble beyond having to pull my feet out of the stirrups and tuck them up tight against his mane.

Sister Margaret was still on Brunet. Somebody said she had better change to one of the taller horses, and while that was being discussed the perverse little wretch settled it himself by walking into the creek after the others, without consulting anyone.

He was out of his depth almost at once. We were frightened and delighted at the same moment, watching her draw her feet up out of the stirrups with astonishing quickness and perch herself on the very top of the saddle, where she stayed, and steered her small mutinous horse safely up onto the far bank.

That was the last of our adventures. Four easy miles brought us to the Fort with the sun laying its last light across the whole glowing country, and at the ferry the friends who had been waiting for us took us straight up to the hospitable roof of Major Twiggs.
