From Ouilmette's house the party went on to Mr. Kinzie's. They came into the parlor where the family sat with their faithful protectors, and sat down on the floor without a word. Black Partridge read their looks — moody, bent on revenge — and knew what was in their minds. He did not argue. He did not dare to. He only leaned toward Wau-ban-see and said it low: "We have tried to save our friends, but it is in vain — nothing will save them now."

Then, across the river, a whoop. A friendly one. Black Partridge sprang out to meet their leader, and was down at the water as the hastily manned canoes touched the bank by the house.

"Who are you?" he demanded.

"A man. Who are you?"

"A man like yourself. But tell me who you are." He was asking the only thing that mattered: tell me your disposition, which side you are for.

"I am a Sau-ga-nash!"

"Then make all speed to the house — your friend is in danger, and you alone can save him."

Billy Caldwell — for it was he — walked into that parlor at an easy pace, nothing hurried in him, nothing in his face. He unbuckled his accoutrements without haste and set them with his rifle behind the door. Then he turned and saluted the hostile visitors.

"How now, my friends! A good-day to you. I was told there were enemies here, but I am glad to find only friends. Why have you blackened your faces? Is it that you are mourning for the friends you have lost in battle?" He knew what blackened faces meant. He asked anyway.

"Or is it that you are fasting? If so, ask our friend, here, and he will give you to eat. He is the Indian's friend, and never yet refused them what they had need of."

It caught them flat. Taken by surprise in that fashion, they were ashamed to own aloud what they had come to do. So they said modestly that they had come to beg of their friends a little white cotton, to wrap their dead in before burying them. It was given them, with some other presents. They gathered it up and left the premises peaceably.
