From Ouilmette's house the party went on to Mr. Kinzie's. They came into the parlor where the family sat with the friends who had guarded them all night, and sat down on the floor without a word. No greeting. Black Partridge looked at the faces — the black paint, the sullen inward look of men who have already settled a thing — and he knew what had come into the room. He did not argue. He did not dare. He only leaned toward Wau-ban-see and said it low: "We have tried to save our friends, but it is in vain — nothing will save them now."

Then, across the river, a whoop. A friendly one. Black Partridge was out of the house before anyone had moved, and he was at the water as the first of the hastily manned canoes touched the bank.

"Who are you?" he demanded.

"A man. Who are you?"

"A man like yourself. But tell me who you are." He was asking the only thing that mattered: tell me your disposition, which side you are for.

"I am a Sau-ga-nash!"

"Then make all speed to the house — your friend is in danger, and you alone can save him."

Billy Caldwell — for it was he — walked into that parlor at an easy pace, nothing hurried in him, nothing in his face. He unbuckled his accoutrements without haste and set them with his rifle behind the door, where every man could watch him do it. Then he turned and saluted the painted visitors like a caller come for the pleasure of it.

"How now, my friends! A good-day to you. I was told there were enemies here, but I am glad to find only friends. Why have you blackened your faces? Is it that you are mourning for the friends you have lost in battle?" He knew what blackened faces meant. He asked anyway.

"Or is it that you are fasting? If so, ask our friend, here, and he will give you to eat. He is the Indian's friend, and never yet refused them what they had need of."

It caught them flat. Held up in the light of their own courtesy, they could not say aloud what they had come to do. So they said modestly that they had come to beg of their friends a little white cotton, to wrap their dead in before burying them. It was given them, with some other presents. They gathered it up and left the premises peaceably, and the house was still standing.
