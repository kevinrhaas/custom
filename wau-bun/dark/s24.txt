Early the next morning, before I had even left my room, it reached me from the apartment next door — lamentation, woe. I went in. Several squaws were sitting on the floor with their eyes cast down, their whole posture condolence and sympathy, and in the middle of them sat a little ugly woman in tattered clothes, her face blackened, her hair loose and disordered, sobbing and wailing bitterly.

They had to be the family of the dead chief. It troubled me badly that I had no way of telling them I shared in their sorrow except with my hands.

I knew nothing whatever of their customs. Looking at how wretched they were, I took it for granted that part of what afflicted them was plain poverty and destitution. One of them at least appeared to be at the very bottom of misery. "Can it be possible," I said to myself, "that this poor creature has only these scanty rags to cover her?"

I stepped back into my room and came out with a pretty calico wrapper, and I put it into the hands of the little dirty blackened object in the middle of the floor. She took it, and began a fresh round of sobbing and sighing. I made signs that she should put it on. I opened it out for her, showed her how the thing was meant to be worn, and urged her with gestures to waste no time at all in getting herself more comfortable.

At this, the other women burst out laughing.

"Very mal-à-propos," thought I, "and somewhat unfeeling." Then my husband came in and explained it to me. The widow, Madame Four-Legs, was the chief mourner. She had, beyond any doubt, a perfectly comfortable wardrobe waiting at home; going for a season neglected, with a blackened face, is simply what the etiquette of mourning requires. He told me all of this in the gaps between handshakes, while condolences were offered and returned in a language more uncouth and guttural than any I had heard in my life. Their Father dismissed them at last with a promise of presents to help dry up the tears. Let nobody conclude from any of that the poor little widow's grief was not sincere. She had been greatly attached to her husband, and her influence had been great — over him, and over the whole nation besides. A Fox woman, she spoke the Chippewa, and the Chippewa is the court language of every tribe, so people came to her constantly to interpret; and she had made a practice of travelling with her husband and helping him with her counsels on every occasion that arose. She had great shrewdness and judgment. She also had, as I found out for myself later on, affections that were strong and would not let go.
