Early the next morning, before I had left my room, a sound came through the wall that brought me up out of my chair — a wailing, from the apartment adjoining. When I went in, several squaws were seated on the floor with their eyes down, their whole attitude one of condolence and sympathy, and in the middle of them sat a little ugly woman in tattered garments, her face blackened, her hair loose and wild, sobbing and crying bitterly.

I had no doubt they were the family of the dead chief, and it troubled me that I could say nothing to them about their sorrow except with my hands.

Knowing nothing of their customs, I took it for granted from the state of them that poverty was one of the things they were grieving over. One of the party, at the very least, looked to be at the bottom of everything a person can lose. Can it be possible, I said to myself, that this poor creature has nothing but these scanty rags to cover her?

I stepped back into my own room and brought out a pretty calico wrapper, and put it into the hands of the little dirty blackened object in the middle of the floor. She took it, and began sobbing and sighing all over again. I made signs to her to put it on. I opened it out and showed her how it was to be worn, and urged her, by gestures, to lose no time in making herself more comfortable.

At this the other women burst out laughing.

Very mal-à-propos, I thought, and somewhat unfeeling. Then my husband came in and explained to me that the chief mourner was Madame Four-Legs, the widow; that she had, beyond question, a comfortable wardrobe waiting for her at home; and that it is part of the etiquette of mourning to go for a season with the person neglected and the face blackened. All this was told me in the intervals of shaking hands and offering and receiving condolences in the most uncouth, guttural language I had ever heard. Their Father dismissed them at last, with the promise of some presents to help dry their tears. Let no one infer from the calico wrapper that the poor little widow's grief was anything but sincere. She had been greatly attached to her husband, and had held great influence, not with him only but with the nation at large. She was a Fox woman, and spoke Chippewa, which is the court language among all the tribes, so that she was often sent for to interpret, and had been in the habit of traveling with her husband and assisting him with her counsel on every occasion. She was a person of great shrewdness and judgment, and, as I afterward had reason to know, of strong and tenacious affections.
