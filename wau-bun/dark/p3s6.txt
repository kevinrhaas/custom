The third day of our trip came up brilliantly clear, the same as the two before it, and we bent our course farther to the north than we had been going, toward Big-foot Lake — which people now call, with a good deal less imagination, the Lake of Geneva.

That day nothing went wrong. Nothing at all. The air was soft, the foliage of the forest was fresh and gave off its scent as we passed through it, the little brooks ran clear and sparkling, and every part of it spoke the praises of the Creator who had been good to us.

It is in country like this, a long way off from the noise and the striving and the sin of civilized places, that a person most completely feels the presence of the great Author of the Universe. Here the mind can adore his majesty and his goodness without anything standing in between. Here, and only here, is the command actually kept: Let all the earth keep silence before Him.

And it can hardly be missed that the deepest and most solemn devotion lives in the hearts of those who are shut out from the worship of God in temples made with hands, and are driven instead to commune with him in the middle of the boundless magnificence that his own power framed.

The day was not entirely without incident, either. We had stopped for our noon food and drink, and dismounted, and thrown ourselves down on the fresh herbage right at the edge of a pleasant thicket, when a tender bleating started up somewhere close to us. Then the low branches parted and a sweet little dappled fawn came through them and out among us, plainly hunting for its mother. It showed no fear of us whatever. Poor Selkirk's lines might be parodied for it: it was so unacquainted with man, its tameness was charming to us.

Then the children began shouting with delight, and that sent the fawn bounding straight back into the woods, and any hope of catching it and keeping it for a pet ended right there.

We had covered good ground that day and were beginning to feel it, when just before sunset we came up onto a ridge that looked down into one of the loveliest little dells I have ever seen. It was an oak opening. Under the shade of the tall scattered trees, the cattle and horses of the soldiers were browsing — they had gotten that far. Two or three white tents stood at the bottom of the valley beside a clear stream. The camp-fires were already lit, and the men were moving about them singly and in groups, seeing to their own comfort or their animals'.

Lieutenant Foster came out to welcome us with great delight, and accepted without a moment's hesitation an invitation to join our mess again for as long as we should be traveling together.

We found ourselves a pleasant encamping-ground, far enough off from the other party to spare us any inconvenience, and our supper was improved by a kettle of fine fresh milk brought over by Mrs. Gardiner, the hospital matron, who with her little covered cart was no unimportant feature of that military group. We ate our evening meal with a great deal of hilarity and enjoyment.

If people are ever good company for one another, it is under circumstances like these. There has always been enough incident in the day to supply the talk and the laughing for as long as anyone is inclined to keep it up — which, to tell the truth, is not an unconscionable length of time after their supper is over.

The poor Lieutenant looked grave enough when we set out ahead of him the next morning. Nobody in his party knew the road. Mr. Kinzie gave him directions, general and particular, and promised that at every place that looked more than usually doubtful he would blaze a tree or set up a chip to show the way.
