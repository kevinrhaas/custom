There was almost nobody at Barney Lawton's when we got there — a Frenchman, and a small number of Indians. My sister and I got down and went into the house. The door stood open. Two Indians sat on the floor, smoking, and they lifted their eyes as we came in. I will never forget what came over both their faces. Wonder first, and then horror. The lips of one of them went slack and his pipe dropped out of them onto the floor. Their eyes seemed to be starting out of their heads. They raised their hands, spread wide, as though to push us back out of the room, and said, slowly, "Manitou!" A spirit.

We pulled up our masks and came forward smiling, with our hands out, and they were on their feet in an instant, letting out a cry of pure delight at the sight of two faces they knew.

"Bon-jour, bon-jour, Maman!" And they went straight out the door to tell the others what had just happened to them.

Our ride that afternoon lay over a prairie that ran away to the northeast with nothing living on the whole broad face of it. Nothing on the ground, at least. Over our heads flew and circled uncountable flocks of curlews, screaming their wild notes to the listening waste.

Their cry is shrill and particular — crack, crack, crack, rackety, rackety, rackety — and coming out of dozens of throats at once, sometimes stooping down quite close to our ears, it became after a while almost more than we could stand. They seemed to have lost their senses over the excitement of so unusual and splendid a procession moving through their desolate country.
