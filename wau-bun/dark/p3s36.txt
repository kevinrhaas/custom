Lieutenant Hunter arrived with leave of absence in order to escort us, and after that everything fell into train for our return to Fort Winnebago. There was no Mackinac boat to be had. In its place we got a Durham boat, which is longer and shallower than the other, with no way of rigging an awning or any shelter over the center of it. What it did have was size, and size suited a party like ours, which counted, besides our own family, Lieutenant and Mrs. Hunter, the wife of another officer now stationed at Fort Winnebago, and our cousin, Miss Forsyth. We made up our minds, as may be supposed, to pretty close quarters.

Our crew was made up partly of Frenchmen and partly of soldiers, and, all things being ready, we set off on a fine bright morning in the latter part of July.

The second day of alternate rowing and poling brought us to the Grande Chûte early in the afternoon.

The custom here is to put everyone ashore at the foot of the rapids and let them climb the high bank and walk around the fall, while the men haul the boat up through the foaming water.

Most of our party had already stepped out onto the shore when a sudden thought took hold of one of the ladies and myself.

"Let us stay in the boat," we said, "and be pulled up the Chûte." The rest of the company went on up the bank. We sat where we were and watched the men make their preparations with great interest. They were over the side and into the water in no time, and they made a strong rope fast to the bow and all lent their weight to it, marching slowly forward with their heavy load behind them. The cargo, which was only our trunks and our stores, and of no very considerable weight, had not been taken out.

Up we went, taking now and then a tremendous bump against a rock we never saw, and often catching a shower of foam as the water roared and boiled around us.

The men kept as close as they could to the high, sheer bank, where the water ran smoothest. At the head of the cordel was a merry simpleton of a Frenchman who kept turning his head to grin back at us, delighted with how plainly we were enjoying ourselves.

We were in high glee. "Isn't this charming?" one of us cried. "I only wish — "

The wish, whatever it was going to be, was cut off by a shout and a crash. "Have a care, Robineau! Mind where you are taking the boat!" But the warning came too late. Being more occupied with the ladies than with his duty, the leader had walked us straight into a sharp, projecting tree that hung out from the bank. The first tug tore the side out of the boat, and the water came in at once.

My companion and I jumped for the nearest rocks that showed their heads above the foam. Our screams and the shouting of the men brought Lieutenant Hunter and some Indians down off the bank at a run. They carried us in their arms to land, while the men worked lustily at fishing up the contents of the boat, every article of it now thoroughly soaked.

We scrambled up the high bank in a miserable state, to join the general lamentation over what the accident had probably cost us.

"Oh! my husband's new uniform!" cried one, and, "Oh! the miniatures in the bottom of my trunk!" sighed another, while, "Oh! the silk dresses, and the ribbons, and the finery!" made up the general chorus.

Nobody thought about the provisions, though we had all seen, on our way to shore, the barrel of bread and the tub of ice that Lieutenant Hunter had so providently brought for our food and drink, sailing off down the dancing waves. Among the boxes carried to land and toted up the steep bank was one holding loaves of sugar and packages of tea, which I had bought from the sutler at the post for our winter's supply. The young Indian who had carried it set it down on the ground, and soon called my attention to a thick white stream oozing out of the corners. I made signs for him to taste it. He dipped his finger in, and when he understood what it was he called out to his companions with delight. I pointed then at his hatchet, and motioned him to open the box. He did not require a second invitation. It was hacked to pieces in no time.

Then I beckoned up all the rest of the youngsters, who were standing about full of wonder, and there followed such a scrambling and shouting that we all went into fits of laughter, the boys worst of all. Bowls, dippers, hands — everything that could hold even the smallest quantity was put in requisition. The squaws were the most active of any. Those who could do no better took the stoutest fragments of the blue paper the sugar had been wrapped in, and in a trice there was nothing left but the wet yellow bundles of tea and the splinters of the box that had held it.

By this time fires had been made, and the articles out of the trunks were soon spread over every shrub and bush in the vicinity. Fortunately the box with the new uniform in it had been piled high above the others, in the center of the boat, and had taken very little damage. The state of the wardrobes in general was sad.

Not a white article was to be seen anywhere. Everything was mottled — blue, green, red and black running into one another in streaks, and dripping from the ends and corners.

To add to the trouble, the rain began to fall, as rain will at an inconvenient moment, and before long the half-dried garments had to be gathered out of the smoke and huddled away in a most discouraging condition.

The tent was pitched wet as it was, and the blankets, wrung out and only partly dried, were spread on the ground for us to lie on that night.

A Hamburg cheese, which had been part of my stores, was voted to me for a pillow, and after a supper whose best feature was a portion of one of the wet loaves — it had stayed in a barrel wedged too tightly to drift away — we betook ourselves to our repose.
