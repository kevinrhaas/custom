Lieutenant Hunter had taken leave of absence for the purpose of escorting us, and once he arrived our return to Fort Winnebago came together quickly. There was no Mackinac boat to be had. What we got instead was a Durham boat, which is longer than the other and shallower, and which offers nowhere to rig an awning or any other sort of shelter over the middle of it. Its one advantage was room, and we needed room: besides our own family the party took in Lieutenant and Mrs. Hunter, the wife of another officer then stationed at Fort Winnebago, and our cousin, Miss Forsyth. We understood, before we ever pushed off, that we were going to be packed in tight.

The crew was made up half of Frenchmen and half of soldiers. Everything ready, we started on a fine bright morning toward the end of July.

The second day was rowing and poling by turns, and early in the afternoon it brought us to the Grande Chûte.

The custom there is to put the passengers ashore at the foot of the rapids. They climb the high bank and walk around the fall on dry ground while the crew hauls the boat up through the white water.

Most of our people had already stepped out onto the shore when an idea took hold of one of the other ladies and me at the same moment.

"Let us stay in the boat," said we, "and be pulled up the Chûte." The others went on up, and the two of us sat where we were and watched the men get ready, which was worth watching. They went over the side into the river, made a strong rope fast to the bow, and set themselves to it, every man pulling, walking slowly forward with all that weight behind them. Nothing had been taken out of the boat. The cargo was only our trunks and our stores, and it did not amount to much.

Up we went. Every so often the hull found a rock that had been hidden from us and took a tremendous bump off it, and the spray came over us while the water roared and boiled along the sides.

The men held in as near as they could to the high, sheer bank, where the current runs smoothest. At the head of the cordel was a merry simpleton of a Frenchman who kept turning his head to grin back at us, delighted that we were delighted.

And we were in high glee. "Isn't this charming?" cried one. "I only wish - - "

Whatever the wish was, a shout and a crash took the end off it. "Have a care, Robineau! Mind where you are taking the boat!" That was the cry, and it came too late. The man at the head of the line had been giving his attention to the ladies instead of to his work, and he had walked us straight into a sharp branch of a tree that leaned out from the bank. One pull tore the side out of the boat, and the water began to come in.

My companion and I went off onto the nearest rocks that stood clear of the foam. Our screams and the men's shouting brought Lieutenant Hunter and some Indians down off the bank at a run, and they took us up in their arms and carried us in to land, while the crew set to work lustily fishing our things out of the water, every one of them soaked through.

We climbed the high bank in a miserable state and joined the general lamentation over what the accident was likely to have cost us.

"Oh! my husband's new uniform!" cried one, and "Oh! the miniatures in the bottom of my trunk!" sighed another - while, "Oh! the silk dresses, and the ribbons, and the finery!" formed the general chorus.

Not one of us gave a thought to the provisions, though on our way in to shore we had all seen the barrel of bread and the tub of ice, which Lieutenant Hunter had providently laid in for our food and drink, riding away from us on the dancing water. Among the boxes carried ashore and "toted" up the steep bank was one holding loaves of sugar and packages of tea that I had bought from the sutler at the post to last us the winter. The young Indian who had brought it up set it down, and then called my attention to a thick white stream running out of the corners of it. I made signs for him to taste it. He put his finger in, and when he understood what it was he called out in delight to the others. Then I pointed at his hatchet and made a motion of opening the box. He did not need to be asked twice; the box was in pieces in no time.

Then I beckoned up all the other youngsters, who had been standing about full of wonder, and what followed was such a scrambling and shouting that we were all in fits of laughter, the boys most of all. Bowls, dippers, bare hands, anything that would carry even a little — everything was pressed into service. The squaws were the quickest of all. Those with nothing else to hand tore off the stoutest pieces of the blue paper the sugar had been wrapped in and used that, and in a trice there was nothing left on the ground but the wet yellow bundles of tea and the splinters of the box that had held it.

Fires had been lit by then, and one by one the contents of the trunks came out and went over every shrub and bush within reach. As luck had it, the box with the new uniform in it had been stacked high above the rest in the middle of the boat, and had taken very little harm. The rest of the wardrobes were a sorry business.

There was not a white article left among them. Everything had gone mottled — blue, green, red and black running together in streaks, and dripping steadily from every end and corner.

Then the rain started, rain having a fine instinct for the wrong moment, and the half-dried clothes had to be pulled out of the smoke and bundled away again in a condition to discourage anybody.

The tent went up wet. The blankets were wrung out, dried as far as they could be dried, and laid on the ground for us to sleep on.

A Hamburg cheese out of my stores was voted to me for a pillow. Supper's best feature was a piece of one of the wet loaves, saved because its barrel had been wedged in too tight to float away, and after that we took ourselves off to our repose.
