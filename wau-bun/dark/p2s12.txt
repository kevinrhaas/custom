The morning of the 15th came. Everything stood ready, and nine o'clock was the hour set for starting.

Mr. Kinzie had offered to go with the troops on their march, and so his family had to be put in other hands. He gave them over to some friendly Indians, who undertook to bring them by boat to a point[34] on the St. Joseph's River, round the head of Lake Michigan, and there the troops would rejoin them if the troops were allowed to finish their march.

Word reached him early that morning from To-pee-nee-bee, who was a chief among the band at St. Joseph's. The Pottowattamies who had contracted to escort the detachment meant mischief by it. The chief urged him to give up the idea of traveling overland with the soldiers, and pledged that the boat carrying him and his own people would be let through to St. Joseph's without harm.

Mr. Kinzie would not take the offer. Most of those men held him and his family in real affection, and he believed that his being there among them might hold their fury in check.

In the boat were Mrs. Kinzie; her four younger children; Josette, who nursed them; a clerk of Mr. Kinzie's; two servants; the boatmen; and the two Indians standing as their protectors. The boat pushed off. It had barely come down to the mouth of the river — half a mile below the fort, it will be remembered — when a second messenger from To-pee-nee-bee overtook them with instructions to hold exactly where they were and go no farther. There was only one thing that could mean.

The wife and mother sat in the boat and could hardly breathe. She was a woman of uncommon energy, and of a strength of character that few could match. It did not help her. Her heart failed inside her while she gathered her arms around her helpless infants and watched her husband and her eldest child march away to certain destruction.
