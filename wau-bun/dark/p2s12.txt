The morning of the 15th came. Everything was ready. Nine o'clock was the hour named for starting.

Mr. Kinzie had volunteered to go with the troops on their march. He put his family into the care of some friendly Indians, who promised to take them by boat around the head of Lake Michigan to a point on the St. Joseph's River, where the troops would join them, if the troops were permitted to get that far.

Early that morning a message came to Mr. Kinzie from To-pee-nee-bee, a chief of the St. Joseph's band. Mischief was intended, the message said, by the very Pottowattamies who had engaged to escort the detachment. To-pee-nee-bee urged him to give up the idea of going with the troops by land, and promised that the boat carrying him and his family would be allowed to pass in safety to St. Joseph's.

Mr. Kinzie refused. He believed his presence with the column might act as a restraint on the fury of the savages, so warmly were most of them attached to him and to his family.

In the boat were Mrs. Kinzie and her four younger children, their nurse Josette, a clerk of Mr. Kinzie's, two servants and the boatmen, and the two Indians who were to protect them. The boat pushed off. It had barely reached the mouth of the river — half a mile below the fort, it will be remembered — when another messenger from To-pee-nee-bee came to hold them where they were. There was no mistaking why.

So the wife and mother sat, and breathed, and waited. She was a woman of uncommon energy and strength of character. It did not help her. She folded her arms around her helpless infants, and watched her husband and her eldest child march away toward certain destruction, and felt her heart die inside her.
