It was late in the day on the thirteenth of March before we got away from our kind hostess. She sent us off loaded with cakes, with good wishes, and with messages for her sister Dixon and for the children. The riding was pleasant. The country had winter all over it and was beautiful anyway.

There was a house at Buffalo Grove where we halted for half an hour, and a nice-looking young girl there made us a present of maple-sugar she had boiled herself. She had a story to tell us as well, about the rivalry between two claimants for the patronage of the stage-wagon. The proprietors had not yet settled whether to run it through Buffalo Grove or by another road, which she pointed out to us a short distance off. The driver, she was careful to let us know, was for Buffalo Grove. We asked her, "Is he a young man?" She said he was, and the blush that came with the answer explained the whole matter to our satisfaction.

The sun was just going down when we came to the Rock River, dark and running fast. We had gone a long way out of our road for the sake of that ferry. The ferry turned out to be a skiff. The larger boat had been torn loose the week before and carried off downstream when the ice broke up.

My husband thought first about getting me over. He put me into the boat with the saddles and the packs and the rest of it, and since it was so late and there was no time to spare, he took the bridles of the two most docile horses in the same hand, to guide them while they swam.

A few rods out from shore we heard a hard puffing and blowing close by us and turned to see what it was. It was little Brunet, come up on our weather-bow. He had decided, entirely on his own responsibility, that he would not be outdone by Jerry, whom he took for his model, and he made the opposite shore as soon as anybody in the party.

Everyone landed safely, and a short walk brought us to Mr. Dixon's house. He had not been long in that country, but he had already got everything about him comfortable, and when he showed us into Mrs. Dixon's sitting-room and sat us down by a glowing wood fire, and Mrs. Dixon went to work on a nice supper for us, I decided the comfort more than paid for the inconvenience of such a trip.

There were several children around her. One of them stood leaning against the chimney-piece in the full Indian costume — calico shirt, blanket, leggings. He had a dark complexion and full, melancholy eyes, and he kept them down on the ashes, where he was making marks with a stick. He hardly lifted them to look at us at all, which is the one thing children always do. He interested me exceedingly. There was a bright little girl close by, plainly a daughter of our host, and I asked her, "Who is that boy?"

"Oh, that is John Ogie," answered she.

"What is the matter with him? he looks very sad."

"Oh, he is fretting after his mother."

"Is she dead, then?"

"Some say she is dead, and some say she is gone away. I guess she is dead, and buried up in one of those graves yonder" — and she pointed out the window at two or three little picketed inclosures on the rising ground opposite.

My sympathy for the child was already strong, and it grew when I asked the little spokeswoman, "Has he no father?" and she answered, "Oh, yes, but he goes away, and drinks, and don't care for his children."

"And what becomes of John then?"

"He stays here with us, and we teach him to read, and he learns dreadful fast."

Then the boy raised those large dark eyes and turned them on me, and it went to my heart. It was such a motherless look. What was behind it I only understood long afterward, when I learned the rest of his history. His mother was living. He knew she was living. He never said so to the other children — his people keep such things behind them. She had married a surly, intemperate Canadian, and when she could no longer endure the way he used her she left him and went back to her own family among the Pottowattamies. Years later this boy and a brother, who had also been left behind with their father, found their way to the Upper Missouri to join her, the Government having by then taken her and the rest of her tribe away from the shores of Lake Michigan.

Supper came smoking to the board, ducks and venison and everything that belongs with them, and we did it ample justice. Traveling is a great sharpener of the appetite, and so is cheerfulness, and ours rose considerably while Mr. Dixon gave us his encouraging account of the road still ahead of us.

"There is no difficulty," he said, "if you keep a little to the north, and strike the great Sauk trail. If you get too far to the south, you will come upon the Winnebago Swamp, and, once in that, there is no telling when you will ever get out again. As for the distance, it is nothing at all to speak of. Two young men came out here from Chicago, on foot, last fall. They got here the evening of the second day, and, even with a lady in your party, you could go on horseback in less time than that. The only thing is to be sure and get on the great track that the Sauks have made, in going every year from the Mississippi to Canada, to receive their presents from the British Indian Agent."
