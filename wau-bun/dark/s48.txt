He was born at Quebec in 1163. His mother had been married before, to a gentleman by the name of Haliburton, and that marriage produced one daughter — the woman who would become the mother of General Fleming, of Nicholas Low, Esq., and of Mrs. Charles King, of New York. She is remembered as a lady of remarkable beauty and accomplishments. Of the second marriage there was one child only, and that was Mr. Kinzie. He was still an infant when his father died. His mother married a third time, a Mr. Forsyth, and the family afterward moved to the city of New York.

At ten or eleven he was sent to school at Williamsburg, on Long Island, along with two of his half-brothers. Every Saturday a negro servant came out from the city to collect the children and carry them home until Monday morning. One Saturday the man arrived to find the school in an uproar. Johnny Kinzie was gone. They hunted in every direction, turned out every corner of the place, and it came to nothing. The boy was not there to be found.

The news had to be carried home to his mother. Some said he had drowned. Others said he had only wandered off and would come back on his own. Weeks went by, and then months, and at last he was given up and mourned as a dead child. All that while he was carrying out a plan he had been forming for a long time: to get to Quebec, the city he had been born in, and make his own way in the world.

However he managed it, he crossed from Williamsburg over to New York, and at one of the docks on the North River he found a sloop bound up to Albany and took passage in her. Somewhere on the river a gentleman noticed him — a small boy traveling alone — and grew curious enough to ask him what his business was.

"He was going to Quebec, where he had some friends."

"Had he the means to carry him there?"

"Not much, but he thought he could get along."

As it happened, the gentleman was bound for Quebec himself. He took the boy in charge, paid his way the entire distance, and finally left him standing in the streets of the city — a city where, whatever he had told himself, he knew nobody at all.

He walked about for some time, putting his head into one "store" and workshop after another. At last he stepped into a silversmith's shop, looked at the master's face, liked what he saw written in it, and asked whether he wanted an apprentice.

"What, you, my little fellow! What can you do?"

"Anything you can teach me."

"Well, we will make a trial and see."

The trial went well enough. He lived with that kind man's family better than three years, until his parents — who, in moving to Detroit, had necessarily come back into Canada — found out where he was, and he was given back to them.

There were five younger half-brothers, all Forsyths. In the old family Bible there is an entry set down after the family had moved to Detroit, and it reads: "George Forsyth was lost in the woods 6th August, 1775, when Henry Hays and Mark Stirling ran away and left him. The remains of George Forsyth were found by an Indian the 2d of October, 1776, close by the Prairie Ronde."

That the same unhappy mother should be made to bear that particular grief twice seems a strange cruelty — a child gone in a way that is worse than a death, because it leaves the mind free to supply every horror it can invent. This is how the little brother was lost. He was walking home from school one evening when he met the colored servant-boy riding out to the common to bring in the cows. The school-house stood close by the old fort, and everything west of it was rough open country, uncultivated, known as "the Common." The child asked to be taken up for a ride. The servant said no and told him to go straight home. Two other boys, a little older, were with him, and the three of them trailed after the rider a good way, still hoping to be let up. When the dark came on, the two older boys gave it up and went back. The youngest went on. The servant did not see him again on his return, and no word of him ever came to anyone, though the whole small settlement searched and kept searching — not until the next year, when, as the record says, an Indian found his remains. Nothing about them could be identified except the auburn curls of his hair and the little boots he had worn. He cannot have lived long after he lost his way; the Prairie Ronde lay too close to the settlement for him to have missed the calls and the sounding horns of the men hunting for him, if he had still been alive to hear.

As he grew, Mr. Kinzie's restless and adventurous turn of mind kept him out on the frontier. He went into the Indian trade young, and kept establishments at Sandusky and at Maumee. Around 1800 he pushed still farther west, to St. Joseph's, in Michigan. That same year he married Mrs. McKillip, the widow of a British officer, and in 1804 he came to Chicago to make his home. That was the year the first fort was built there, by Major John Whistler.

Little by little he opened trading-posts farther and farther out, every one of them feeding the parent post at Chicago: at Milwaukie among the Menomonees; at Rock River among the Winnebagoes and the Pottowattamies; on the Illinois River and the Kankakee among the Pottowattamies of the Prairies; and among the Kickapoos in the country they called "Le Large" — that wide stretch of district afterward erected into Sangamon County.

Every post had a superintendent over it, and its full number of engagés, its string of pack-horses, its boats and canoes. From nearly all of the stations the furs and peltries came in to Chicago on the backs of the pack-horses, and the trade goods went back out to them the same way.

Two or three vessels a year, rarely more, came in spring and fall with the supplies and the goods for the trade, and carried away the furs already gathered, taking them to Mackinac, which was the depôt for the Southwest and the American Fur Companies. In the other seasons the furs went the same distance by boat, coasting the shore of the lake.
