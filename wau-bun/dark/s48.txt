He was born at Quebec in 1763. His mother had been married before, to a gentleman of the name of Haliburton, and the only daughter of that marriage became the mother of General Fleming, of Nicholas Low, Esq., and of Mrs. Charles King, of New York; she is described as a lady of remarkable beauty and accomplishments. Mr. Kinzie was the only child of the second marriage. His father died while he was still an infant. His mother married a third time, a Mr. Forsyth, and after that the family removed to the city of New York.

At the age of ten or eleven he was put to school at Williamsburg, on Long Island, with two of his half-brothers. Every Saturday a negro servant came out from the city to bring the children home, and kept them until the following Monday morning. On one such Saturday the messenger reached the school and found the whole place in commotion. Johnny Kinzie was missing. They searched in every direction. Every place was ransacked. It was all in vain. No Johnny Kinzie could be found.

The heavy tidings were carried home to his mother. By some it was thought the lad had drowned; by others that he had strayed away and would return of himself. Weeks passed by, and then months, and in the end he was given up and mourned as lost. All that time the boy was fulfilling a determination he had held to himself a long while: to see his native city of Quebec again, and to make his own way in life.

He had contrived, by some means, to cross from Williamsburg to the city of New York, and at one of the docks on the North River he found a sloop bound for Albany and took passage aboard her. On the way up the river a gentleman noticed the little lonely passenger, took an interest in him, and questioned him about his business.

"He was going to Quebec, where he had some friends."

"Had he the means to carry him there?"

"Not much, but he thought he could get along."

It happened, fortunately, that the gentleman was going to Quebec himself. He took the boy under his care, paid his expenses the whole distance, and at length parted with him in the streets of the city, where he was, in truth, a stranger.

He wandered about for a time, looking in at various stores and workshops. At length he stepped into the shop of a silversmith, and something in the master's face satisfied him, and he asked the man whether he wanted an apprentice.

"What, you, my little fellow! What can you do?"

"Anything you can teach me."

"Well, we will make a trial and see."

The trial was satisfactory. He stayed in the family of his kind friend more than three years, until his parents — who, in removing to Detroit, had necessarily returned to Canada — discovered his place of abode, and he was restored to them.

There were five younger half-brothers, all of the name of Forsyth. In the old family Bible we find this touching record of an event that occurred after the family had removed to Detroit: "George Forsyth was lost in the woods 6th August, 1775, when Henry Hays and Mark Stirling ran away and left him. The remains of George Forsyth were found by an Indian the 2d of October, 1776, close by the Prairie Ronde."

It seems a singular fatality that the same unhappy mother should have been called twice to suffer the same affliction — the loss of a child in a manner worse than death, because it leaves room for every horror the imagination cares to supply. The particulars of the little brother's loss were these. Coming from school one evening, he met the colored servant-boy on horseback, riding out to the common for the cows. The school-house stood quite near the old fort, and everything beyond it toward the west was a wild, uncultivated tract they called the Common. The child begged to be taken up and given a ride. The other refused, and bade him return home at once. Two other boys, somewhat older, were with him, and the three of them followed the negro some distance, still hoping to prevail on him for a ride. As it grew dark the two older boys turned back. The youngest kept on. When the negro came in he had not seen the child again, nor were any tidings of him ever received, for all the diligent search made by the whole little community, until, as the record tells it, his remains were found the following year by an Indian. There was nothing left to identify them by, except the auburn curls of his hair and the little boots he had been wearing. He must have perished very shortly after losing his way. The Prairie Ronde was too near the settlement for him to have missed the calls and the sounding horns of those searching for him, if he had been living to hear them.

As he grew older, Mr. Kinzie's enterprising and adventurous disposition led him to live much on the frontier. He entered the Indian trade early, and had establishments at Sandusky and at Maumee. About the year 1800 he pushed farther west, to St. Joseph's, in Michigan. In that year he married Mrs. McKillip, the widow of a British officer, and in 1804 he came to make his home at Chicago. It was in that same year that the first fort was built there, by Major John Whistler.

By degrees he established more remote trading-posts, all of them contributing to the parent one at Chicago: at Milwaukie with the Menomonees; at Rock River with the Winnebagoes and the Pottowattamies; on the Illinois River and the Kankakee with the Pottowattamies of the Prairies; and with the Kickapoos in what was called Le Large, the widely extended district afterward erected into Sangamon County.

Each trading-post had its superintendent and its complement of engagés, its train of pack-horses and its equipment of boats and canoes. From most of the stations the furs and peltries were brought in to Chicago on pack-horses, and the goods necessary for the trade were transported back out by the same method.

The vessels that came in the spring and the fall — seldom more than two or three in a year — brought the supplies and the goods for the trade, and took the furs already collected up to Mackinac, the depôt of the Southwest and American Fur Companies. At other seasons the furs were sent up to that place in boats, coasting around the lake.
