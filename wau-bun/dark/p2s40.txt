The Napoleon showed herself at last. (A pity that so great a name has to be carried in the feminine gender.) There was no harbor at Chicago in those days. A ship stood off and dropped anchor beyond the bar — the tongue of land that made the left bank of the river — and everything she took or gave up had to be rowed to her, boats going out and coming back through the river mouth some way below.

Which meant that loading fast was everything. A vessel wanted to be full and ready the moment a fair wind came, so that she could quit an anchorage that was open to the whole lake and safe in no weather at all.

So we had been living out of our packed boxes for days on end. Our only plan was to see our friends aboard, wave them off, and then set out on our own road back to Fort Winnebago.

The weighty things — furniture, trunks, all of it — had gone into the Napoleon already, to come round to us later by way of the Fox River. What we kept back was the little that would ride on a pack-horse, or in the light dearborn wagon Mr. Kercheval had lately fetched out from Detroit. It was the first luxury of that kind the prairies had ever seen. My husband had bought it so that his mother and his little nephew could travel in some comfort.

Moving the goods of that many families of officers and soldiers, a boat-load at a stretch, together with the thousand odds and ends that a complete removal drags behind it, took an enormous amount of hauling and an enormous amount of time. But it was done. Every last article went out over the water and into the hold. Nothing was left standing on the shore except the passengers, and their turn came next.

That was a considerable relief. Captain Hinckley had spent hours in a fever and a fuss, promising a change in the weather and grumbling that the number of boat-loads was more than any reasonable person required.

Whoever happened to be looking lakeward just then — the schooner had lain about half a mile out — had the satisfaction of watching her put up sail and leave her station for open water. Anyone who looked a little later saw only a shape drawing off into the distance, as though she had taken aboard everything she ever meant to take. And there we stood. There we might go on standing for a week or more, at the pleasure of Captain Hinckley, and the schooner Napoleon, and a stiff east wind that showed no sign of tiring.

Food was not the problem; there was plenty to be had. The problem was cooking it and eating it, and people met that difficulty however they could. Mrs. Engle, in her rooms upstairs, took her breakfast off a shingle with her husband's jack-knife, and when she had finished she sent the shingle and the knife down to Lieutenant Foster so that he might be accommodated too.

We were lodged in the old mansion on the north side, and word ran up the river fast enough: the Napoleon had sailed off with "the plunder" and left the people standing on the beach. Before long Mrs. Portier — our kind Victoire — had furnished us with dishes and knives and forks and every other convenience our mess-basket could not supply.

Two days of this. Then, early on a clear morning, the word went through the place like fire in dry grass: the Napoleon was at anchor again, out beyond the bar.

Nobody wasted a minute of it this time. By early afternoon we had said our goodbyes to friends we loved, and they were standing out from Chicago.[44]
