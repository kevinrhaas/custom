On the dry ground past the morass we gave ourselves only a few minutes. Bellefontaine lay forty-five miles ahead of us, and not one of those miles was of the short, obliging kind.

The horses were magnificent all through the afternoon. Charlie never broke his canter. But the light was going out of the sky, and none of the landmarks that were supposed to tell us we were close had shown themselves.

"Can we not stop and rest for a few moments under one of the trees?" I asked. I was very nearly finished, and a cold November rain had been coming down on us for hours.

"If it were possible, we would," came the answer, "but see how dark it is growing. If we should lose our way, it would be worse than being wet and tired."

So we rode on. Right at dusk the horses splashed through a clear little stream. "That," my husband said, "is, I think, two miles from Bellefontaine. Cheer up - we shall soon be there." That put heart into all of us, and we went forward in better spirits. We covered one mile, and another, and another, and no light showed anywhere among the trees.

"We have certainly traveled more than six miles now," I said.

"Yes - that couldn't have been the two-mile creek."

It was eight o'clock when Bellefontaine finally took us in. They brought us into a big room with a fire roaring in it, warmth enough to hurt. Mr. Wing and Dr. Philleo had got there ahead of us, and there were other travelers besides, come up from the Mississippi. The hostess was an enormous woman, known to everyone as "la grosse Américaine," and she received me with a torrent of kindness and talk, put me in the arm-chair in the warmest corner, and in her own good time laid an excellent supper in front of us.

Her generosity stopped short of her one bed. What she did instead was to heap everything soft she owned onto the bare floor in front of the fire and settle me on it as best she could. Then, watching my husband fuss over me and worry that the rain and the long ride would make me ill, she said, in a voice full of admiring sympathy, "How kind your companion is to you!" We had never heard the word used that way before, and it entertained us a great deal.

Our fellow travelers left at first light for the Fort, only twelve miles off, and they were good enough to carry a note home for us, asking that Plante be sent out with the carriage to bring us in over the last of the road.

We came into the Portage safe and whole, and that was the end of the first overland journey any white woman had ever made from Green Bay to Fort Winnebago. My own opinion of myself rose considerably when my husband told me what I had covered the day before, from Knaggs's to Bellefontaine — sixty-two miles.
