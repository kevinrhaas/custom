We did not stay long on the rising ground beyond the morass. Forty-five miles lay between us and Bellefontaine, and not one of them was a short mile.

The horses were magnificent all that afternoon. Charlie held a canter the whole way and never once broke it. But the light went out of the sky by degrees, and the landmarks that were to tell us we were near the desired haven did not come, and did not come.

"Can we not stop and rest for a few moments under one of the trees?" I asked. I was nearly past holding myself upright, and a cold November rain had begun to come down on us and showed no sign of stopping.

"If it were possible, we would," he said. "But see how dark it is growing. If we should lose our way, it would be worse than being wet and tired."

So we kept on. Just at dark the horses splashed through a clear stream, and my husband said, "That is, I think, two miles from Bellefontaine. Cheer up — we shall soon be there." Two miles. I could ride two miles. We went on more cheerfully, and the trees went by, and no light came friendly through them. Another mile. Another.

"We have certainly traveled more than six miles now," I said.

"Yes — that couldn't have been the two-mile creek."

It was eight o'clock when we reached Bellefontaine. They brought us into a large room with a huge fire blazing in it, and the heat of it went through me like something poured. Mr. Wing and Dr. Philleo had come in ahead of us, and there were other travelers besides, on their way up from the Mississippi. The hostess was an immense woman — la grosse Américaine, they all called her — and she received me with a kindness and a torrent of talk that allowed no answer, installed me in the arm-chair in the warmest corner, and in due time set an excellent supper in front of us.

Her hospitality stopped short of her bed, which was the only one in the house and stayed hers. Instead she spread every article she could muster on the hard floor before the fire and did what she could to make me comfortable on it. Then she watched my husband fretting over me, anxious the rain and the ride would make me ill, and she said, in a tone of genuine admiring sympathy, "How kind your companion is to you!" The expression was new to us then. We were tired enough that it struck us both as very funny.

Our traveling companions set out early in the morning for the Fort, which was only twelve miles off, and were good enough to carry a note to our friends at home, asking them to send Plante with the carriage to bring us the rest of the way.

We reached the Portage in safety. So ended the first trip by land that any white woman had made from Green Bay to Fort Winnebago. I confess I thought rather better of myself when my husband told me that the distance I had ridden the day before, from Knaggs's to Bellefontaine, was sixty-two miles.
