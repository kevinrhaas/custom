Then came the parting with the Twiggses, and I found I could not manage it lightly. We had leaned on that household all winter. Before the last of the goodbyes the Major drew me aside. He had one final report to deliver on our old acquaintance, Krissman.

"When I went into the barracks the other day," he said, "about the time the men were taking their dinner, I noticed a great six-foot soldier standing against the window-frame, crying and blubbering. 'Halloo,' I said, 'what on earth does this mean?'

"'Why, that fellow there,' said Krissman (for it was he), 'has scrowged me out of my place!' A pretty soldier your protége will make, madam!" added the Major.

That was the last word I ever had of my hero. Perhaps he carried those six feet of grievance south and displayed his prowess to the Seminoles and the Mexicans. Perhaps he went back instead to the fat soil of his native German Flats, and to the boatman's horn he so loved to blow. Some historian yet unborn will have to settle it.

One person is still to be accounted for: Louisa. A chance offered in the spring, and the Major put her into the keeping of a traveller bound for Buffalo, to be delivered home to her parents. She left in style. Out of compliment to the new acquaintances she had formed, she cut her skirts short and drew on a pair of scarlet leggings worked over with porcupine-quills, and so made her farewell to military life. Into the gentleman's hands she also placed sixty dollars for safe keeping, money she said "she had saved up, out of her wages at a dollar a week, through the winter."
