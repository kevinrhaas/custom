We went aboard the steamer Henry Clay on a dark, rainy evening in September of 1830, bound for Green Bay. Everyone we knew in Detroit had told us how fortunate we were. The alternative was passage in one of the little schooners that were, at that time, the ordinary means of reaching the few scattered settlements on Lakes Huron and Michigan, and nobody who had made that crossing could speak of it without a certain change coming over the face.

They all had a story ready, their own or a friend's. Days held fast on the St. Clair flats with nothing to do but wait out the water. Head-winds off Thunder Bay that came on as though something had aimed them. Calms at Mackinac, calms at the Manitous, a flat lake and a slack sail and no way of knowing how long it would last. The account that made us feel luckiest of all was true, and it belonged to our own family: a relative had left Detroit in the month of June and did not set foot in Chicago until the September following. Three months. A sail-vessel has been known to do the same run in four days.

None of it would have deterred me. I was going to see that country at last, and not merely see it — live in it. For years it had been a region of romance to me. When I was a child my greatest pleasure had been the letters of a dear relative describing his home and his mode of life in the Indian country; later there was his happy account of the tour he made with General Cass, in 1820, to the sources of the Mississippi. Earlier than either, there was a long word I had spelled out at my teacher's knee, Mich-i-li-mack-i-nac, syllable by syllable. Vast lakes. Boundless prairies. Forests with no end to them. Now I was to see it. It was to be my home.

We laughed the whole way to the quay. The streets of Detroit were unpaved and deep in mud, and a cart was the only vehicle that could get through them, so a cart carried us along the dark by-ways down to the water. We laughed again going below — the stair to the little room they called the Ladies' Cabin was narrow and near enough to upright to be called a ladder. The accommodations delighted us. Measured against a schooner they were the very height of comfort and convenience, and better still, the cabin held nobody but myself, one other lady, and two little girls.

For the first twenty-four hours nothing could have been pleasanter. Some of the passengers were officers, old friends of ours. We had brought plenty of books, and now and then one of the gentlemen read aloud from them, and broke off to admire the country going past — the untouched woods, the enormous reach of water with not one sail anywhere upon it, all of it magnificent and all of it empty — and then turned to a cigar, or to a game of euchre, to get through the hours.
