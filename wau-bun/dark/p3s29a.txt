Among the rumors that came to us in those days was one that the Sauks were considering an attack on Fort Winnebago itself. That the fort was in no state to be attacked, the Indians knew perfectly well. Every effective man had been pulled out of it on a requisition from General Atkinson and sent to join him at his newly built fort at Kosh-ko-nong.

Fort Winnebago was not picketed in. The barracks and the officers' quarters had no defences at all beyond thin panelled doors and Venetian blinds — nothing that would stand more than a minute against clubs or hatchets. There was no artillery. The Commissary's store was outside the bounds of the fort altogether, down under the hill.

Mr. Kinzie had been pointing all of this out to the officers from the beginning. What he generally got back was a laugh.

"Never fear," they would say. "The Sauks aren't coming here to attack us."

One afternoon we were over in the garrison visiting some of the ladies, with several officers in the room, and the talk went where it always went now.

"Do you not think it wiser," I asked a blustering young officer, "to be prepared against possible danger?"

"Not against these fellows," he said, with contempt.

"I don't think I would even take the trouble to fasten the blinds to my quarters."

"At least," I said, "if you some night find a tomahawk raised to cleave your skull, you will have the consolation of remembering that you haven't been one of those foolish fellows who keep on the safe side."

That nettled him a little, and he was more nettled still when sister Margaret said: "Personally, I am of Governor Cass's opinion. He was at Chicago during the Winnebago war. We were all preparing to move into the fort on the first alarm. Some were for being brave and delaying, like our friends here. 'Come, come,' said the Governor, 'hurry into the fort as fast as possible — there is no merit in being brave with the Indians. It is the height of folly to stay and meet danger which you may by prudence avoid.'"
