On the 10th of September he was taking that walk, close-watched by his guard of soldiers, when the whole party stopped where they stood. Guns. Out on Lake Erie, not far below them. What could it mean? It must be Commodore Barclay firing into some of the Yankees. The firing went on. The prisoner's allotted time ran out and neither he nor his guard noticed it go, because by then they all knew what they were listening to: ships of war, engaged, somewhere past the point. Eventually somebody remembered the hour and told him it was time to go back in. He asked for another half-hour.

"Let me stay," he said, "till we can learn how the battle has gone."

Very soon a sloop came round the point under a press of sail, and then two gun-boats behind her, chasing.

"She is running — she bears the British colors," he cried. "Yes, yes, they are lowering — she is striking her flag! Now," turning to the soldiers, "I will go back to prison contented — I know how the battle has gone."

The sloop was the Little Belt, the last vessel of the squadron taken that day by the gallant Perry.

It was the action he announced in the words nobody has since forgotten: "We have met the enemy, and they are ours!"
