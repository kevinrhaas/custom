On the 10th of September he was out at his promenade under the close watch of his guard of soldiers, when a sound stopped the whole party where they stood: guns, out on Lake Erie, and not far below. What was there to make of it? Commodore Barclay must be firing on some of the Yankees. The guns went on. The time allowed the prisoner for his daily walk ran out, and neither he nor the men set over him took any notice that it had, because by then they were listening — every one of them certain now that ships of war were engaged out there. At last somebody reminded Mr. Kinzie that the hour had come for him to go back into confinement. He asked for another half-hour.

"Let me stay," he said, "till we can learn how the battle has gone."

Then a sloop came round the point under a press of sail, and behind her, giving chase, two gun-boats.

"She is running - she bears the British colors," cried he - "yes, yes, they are lowering - she is striking her flag! Now," turning to the soldiers, "I will go back to prison contented - I know how the battle has gone."

That sloop was the Little Belt. She was the last vessel of the squadron to be taken by the gallant Perry on that memorable day, the one he reported afterward in words that will not be forgotten:

"We have met the enemy, and they are ours!"
