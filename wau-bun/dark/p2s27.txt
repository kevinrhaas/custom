Matters were growing critical, and the prisoners had to be moved somewhere safer than the frontier was now likely to be. It was resolved to send Mr. Kinzie to the mother-country. Nothing has ever come to light that explains General Proctor's course toward this gentleman. He had been taken out of the middle of his own family, where he was living quietly on the parole he had been given and under the protection of the stipulations of the surrender. He had been held for months. Now they set him on a horse under a strong guard, and the guard told him plainly that their orders were to shoot him through the head if he offered to speak to a single person on the road. They tied him onto the saddle so he could not get off it. Then they started for Quebec. One small thing happened on that road, and it illustrates as well as anything the course invariably pursued toward our citizens at that period by the British army on the Northwestern frontier.

The saddle had not been properly fastened. The animal's motion was rough, and the saddle turned under him, and it left him hanging in a position that was both ridiculous and agonizing. His limbs were tied. He could not free himself, and he could not get down. The men in charge of him made him ride that way until he was nearly exhausted before they found the humanity to let him loose.

At Quebec they put him aboard a small vessel bound for England. A few days out she was chased by an American frigate and driven into Halifax. She sailed a second time, sprang a leak, and had to put back.

The attempt to send him across the ocean was given up, and he was returned to Quebec. What came next is as inexplicable as the arrest that started it: he was released. So was Mr. Macomb of Detroit, who had also been confined at Quebec, and both men were given permission to return to their friends and families, although the war was not over. It is possible that in their treatment the British commander-in-chief had been sheltering behind the plea that they were "native-born British subjects," and that when it was learned Mr. Kinzie was in fact a citizen of the United States, letting him go looked like the safest course.

Meanwhile General Harrison had reached Detroit at the head of his troops. He landed on the 29th of September. The whole town went out to meet him, and Mrs. Kinzie was among them, leading her children by the hand. The General walked her home and took up his quarters in her house. Kee-po-tah happened to be visiting the family just then, and Mr. Kinzie's wife introduced them. It turned out no introduction was needed. The General had seen the chief the year before, at the Council at Vincennes, and the meeting between them was one of great cordiality and interest.
