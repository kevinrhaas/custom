What we had risen early to see was the Mission-house and the school beside it, planted here a few years before by the Presbyterian Board of Missions. Mr. and Mrs. Stuart cared about that place more than about anything else on the island, and they had reason to be pleased with it that morning: it was thriving, and it seemed to promise a great deal of usefulness in the years ahead. They had lived here a long while. They had seen what the island used to be — worldly, dissipated, given over to itself — and they had seen it turned around under God's blessing on Christian effort until you could nearly say of it that "Religion was every man's business." The mission was the beloved child of the handful of Protestant families gathered about it, and the thing they all had in common. Mr. and Mrs. Ferry ran it with zeal and good sense, the congregation kept it warm with encouragement, and its name stood high. It was pleasant to watch what teaching and religion did to the children: how they took the mischief and the trickiness out of the half-breed, and how they stirred the genuine Indian out of his stolid apathy.

Those were the palmy days of Mackinac. The island held the head-quarters of the American Fur Company, and it was the entrepôt for the whole Northwest. Everything moving in either direction — supplies and trade goods going out, furs and the products of the Indian country coming back — passed through the parent house here or through one of its many outposts, strung along Lake Superior and Lake Michigan, down the Mississippi, and off into country farther away than that.

Most people know that from the time the old French possessions passed to the British Crown, every Indian tribe but the Miamis and the Wyandots held firmly to the British side. Our winning independence changed nothing in that. Our Government could neither charm their friendship nor buy it. The British, to be fair about it, bid high to keep them. Year after year the leading men of the Chippewas, the Ottawas, the Pottowattamies, the Menomonees, the Winnebagoes, the Sauks and Foxes, and of tribes living farther off still, made the long journey from their own country to Fort Malden in Upper Canada to collect the yearly presents of their Great Father over the water. Keeping them on the payroll was a masterly piece of policy, and in the last war between the two countries it let the men who practised it do fearful execution with such allies at their back.

What was handed out was worth having: blankets, broadcloths and strouding, calicoes, guns, kettles, traps, silver-work of every sort — arm-bands, bracelets, brooches, ear-bobs — looking-glasses, combs, and a hundred other trinkets besides, and nobody counted them out with a mean hand.

To the magazines and store-houses of the Fur Company here at Mackinac came all the upper tribes, to sell what they had and to buy what they wanted, the same goods named above and ammunition as well. Ammunition their British friends very commendably neglected to furnish, along with money and liquor.

They brought furs first, of many kinds, and some of them worth a great deal — beaver and otter, marten, mink, silver-gray fox and red, wolf, bear, wild-cat, musk-rat, and smoked deer-skins. They brought maple-sugar in quantity, and a good deal of Indian corn and petit-blé,[1] and beans, and the folles avoines,[2] which is wild rice. The squaws put in their own share of the merchandise: moccasins, hunting-pouches, mococks — little birch-bark boxes worked over with porcupine-quills and packed with maple-sugar — matting of a neat and lasting weave, and small toy models of Indian cradles, of snow-shoes, of canoes, and of anything else you please.

In those years it was nothing out of the ordinary to look out and count a hundred canoes or more coming in on the island at one time, every one of them loaded with goods for trade. Set beside that the squadrons of big Mackinac boats arriving without pause from the outposts, carrying the furs and peltries and buffalo-robes that the distant traders had gathered up, and you begin to see the size of what the American Fur Company was doing here, and how many human lives, near and far off, hung on some thread of it.

It is no wonder that a philanthropic mind, looking out over all these races of uncultivated heathen, should reach ahead in imagination to the day when the white man, spending his energies without rest and sacrificing self and fortune without stint, should see his red brethren climb the scale of social civilization — when Education and Christianity would walk together and make "the wilderness blossom as the rose."

Not one of those good souls, glad that morning over what their work at Mackinac had accomplished, guessed what was coming. In under twenty-five years there would be nothing left of all these numerous tribes but a few scattered bands, squalid, degraded, hardly a trace remaining of the lofty character they once had. Their lands would be talked out of them or torn out of them. The graves of their fathers would be turned up by the ploughshare. They themselves would be pushed off toward the setting sun, farther with each removal, until the earth itself was literally begrudged them as a place to rest on.

We could not stay long at the school. The Henry Clay was to sail at two o'clock, and before that we meant to see something of the village and the country around it and then to dine with an old friend of my husband's, Mr. Mitchell. Walking at our ease along the white gravel of the road, I had the houses of the old inhabitants pointed out to me one after another. One was the home of Madame Laframboise, an Ottawa woman. Her husband had taught her to read and write, and from that time on she had used what he gave her for the good of the young people of her own nation. Every day she took a class of them into her house and taught them those branches, and with them the principles of the Roman Catholic religion, which she held to with her whole heart. She had a great deal of energy and enterprise in her, and she was tall, commanding, and dignified in the way she carried herself. Her husband had been killed at his post, off in the trading country, by a Winnebago named White Ox. After that she went out to the posts herself, kept the clerks and engagés under her eye, and made certain with her own judgement that the business was being run in a regular and profitable way.

The Agency-house stood at the foot of the hill the fort was built on, and it had a piazza and gardens, luxuries you did not expect to find here. It was a lovely spot, for all that everything under cultivation in this cold northern latitude comes up stunted and dwarfish.

Near it stood the buildings the Fur Company's officials used, a rickety and primitive-looking collection that did no great credit to the architectural skill of my husband. He had overseen the building of them, he told me, when he was hardly more than a boy.

Then there were the houses of the Dousmans, the Abbotts, the Biddles, the Drews, and the Lashleys, running away along the base of that beautiful hill, and above them all the white walls and buildings of the fort. The climb up is so steep that on the sheer face of it nearest the beach they have built staircases to get from the bottom to the top.
