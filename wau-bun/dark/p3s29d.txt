It was on one of those days that we were sitting at the windows that look out over the Portage — we were seldom anywhere else by then, our whole occupation being to watch the road and see what was coming next — when a whoop reached us from a long way off, loud and shrill and drawn out. The news-halloo. Something had happened. We stared down the road to its farthest end and made out a body of horsemen moving toward us, and as they came on we saw they were Indians. They were in full costume. Scarlet streamers ran from the ends of their lances, and their arms threw back the sun. Nearer, and the paint came up, and the feathers, and the brooches. Fifty warriors, or more than fifty. They came to the turning that goes down to the Fort and passed it, and rode straight on up the hill to the Agency. Shaw-nee-aw-kee was not at the house. The Interpreter had been sent for the moment we first saw them at a distance, and had not yet come. The party rode to the top, halted near the blacksmith's shop, and did not get down.

Our hearts went over. It could only be the enemy. And at that moment my husband came into view, walking up from the direction of the Interpreter's house. We called out to him to stop. He went on toward them.

Then the chief of the party swung down off his horse, and every man behind him did the same, and they came forward to shake hands, and I could breathe again.

They cleared a space around the leader and my husband, and the leader began to speak. He flourished a sword as he spoke and threw his arms about violently. It was the first time I had ever seen an Indian carrying that weapon, and I hated the sight of it in such hands. There were moments when the blade came around as though it were about to take my husband's head off at a stroke, and we sat there remembering the stratagems at Mackinac and Detroit in the old days. At last the speech ended. Hands were shaken again. Then we saw my husband turn and lead the way to his storehouse, and shortly his men carried out tobacco and pipes and laid them at the chief's feet.

Our suspense was ended by being told who these people were. It was Man-Eater, the principal chief of the Rock River Indians, come in with his band to hold a talk and bring us word.

Those Indians were under the special care of Mr. Henry Gratiot, whose efforts to keep this — the most dangerous portion of the Winnebagoes — in good feeling had been unremitting, and very shrewdly judged.

What Man-Eater had come to tell us, and he was as noble an Indian in character as he was in appearance, only confirmed what we already had. The Sauks were working north, toward the Portage. He plainly did not know where they were.
