Before long we were obliged to keep the doors and the windows fast, to shut out a sight of misery we had no means of relieving. If a door was opened to let in a member of the family, some wretched mother would push through it, take hold of my infant's hand and put the hand of her own famishing child inside it, and tell us, pleading, that he was imploring "his little brother" for food. The stoutest man alive could not have watched that with a dry eye, and it happened again and again. Screening the lower part of the windows with curtains did no good. They climbed up the outside, and tier upon tier of gaunt, wretched faces would rise above the curtain and look in at us, watching, to see whether we were truly as ill provided as we said we were.

The noble old Day-kau-ray came in one day from the Barribault to tell us what state his village was in. More than forty of his people, he said, had now gone many days with nothing to eat but bark and roots. My husband took him to the commanding officer so he could tell his story himself and learn whether any quantity of food could be had from that quarter. The result was a promise of a small allowance of flour, enough to take the edge off the hunger of his own family.

When this was explained to the chief, he turned away from it. No, he said. If his people could not be relieved, then he and his family would starve along with them. And he refused the offered succor, for those nearest and dearest to him, until every one of them could share alike.
