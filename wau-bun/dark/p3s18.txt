We were barely settled in the new house at the Agency before we tried starting a little Sunday-school. Edwin, Harry and Josette were the scholars we could count on, but there were also the two small Manaigres, Thérèse Paquette, and Thérèse's mother's half-sister, Florence Courville, a pretty girl of fifteen. Not one of the girls had ever learned her letters. They spoke only French, or rather the Canadian patois, and it was terribly hard work to give them the sound of a word and its meaning at the same time, and they always wanted the meaning. On top of that there was the business of undoing false ideas, and of clearing away the ignorance and superstition that stood in the road of any sensible improvement. We did what we could, and we had the satisfaction of seeing them, after a while, get on quite respectably with the spelling-book — and, better than that, come by a little light and knowledge about better things.

In time, though, Florence began to be missing from her class. Her sister, she said, could not always spare her. She wanted her to keep house while she herself went over on Sunday to visit her friends the Roys, who lived on the Wisconsin.

We took the matter up with Madame Paquette. Could she not spare Florence for one hour of the day? We would gladly teach her on a week-day, since she seemed anxious to learn — but we had always been told that for that there was no time.

"Well — she would see. Madame Alum" — she meant Helm — "and Madame John were so kind!"

The regularity never improved. After a while Manaigre was persuaded to send his children down to Mr. Cadle's mission-school at Green Bay. Thérèse went with them, and very soon after that Florence stopped coming at all.

From then on we had to keep our teaching inside our own household.
