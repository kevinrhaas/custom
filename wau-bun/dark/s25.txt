When breakfast was over the principal chiefs came to call on me. They had turned out in their best clothing and their best paint, to look at their new mother.

Naw-kaw was among them, Kar-ray-mau-nee, "the Walking Turtle," the head chief of the nation now — a big-framed man with a broad, agreeable face, and the whole of that face governed by an enormous under lip that hung down almost to his chin. Old Day-kau-ray came too, and there was no one more noble or more venerable in his own tribe, or in any tribe I have known. He had a fine Roman face, and his bald head made it finer, with one single tuft of long silver hair tied neatly and lying back over his shoulders. His dress was exact and proper and almost without ornament of any kind, and his courtesy never went off him, whatever the circumstances. All of it together put him first in the regard of everyone who knew him. What comes later in this book will show that what he was made of stood as high as the way he looked and carried himself.

Black-Wolf was there, and his name told you his face: heavy, surly, lowering. What sharpened it was the mass of black hair hanging loose all around it, which is not the Winnebago fashion at all. They generally take off a part of the hair and draw the rest back, clubbing it behind and dressing it with beads, ribbons, cock's feathers — or, where the man has earned it, one eagle's feather for every scalp he has taken from an enemy.

Talk-English was there, a strikingly handsome and powerful young man, and this is how he got the name. He had been one of a party of sixteen Winnebagoes who went the year before, on invitation, with their Agent and Major Forsyth — the Chippewa, they called him — to see the President at Washington.

All along the road, people who had never met a Western Indian asked them the same thing: "Do you talk English?"

The young man missed nothing, and he took the question to his Father. "What do they mean by this? Everybody says to me, talk English!"

The Agent translated it for him. "Ah, very well."

The next stop they made was Lockport, in the State of New York. He jumped off the canal-boat onto the lock and went straight at the first man he saw, pushing his face forward. "Talk Eengeesh?"

"Yes," said the man, "do you talk English?"

"Ya-as."

He carried the name from that day on, and it went onto the pay-rolls as his title, and he was not a little proud of it.

Hoo-wau-ne-kah, "the Little Elk," was another of the great men of that tribe, and had also made the Washington journey. Henry Clay came to see the deputation, studied every face and every bearing in the room, and then pointed to that one man as the one with the most ability in him. He was much gratified to be told that he had picked out the principal orator of the nation, a man decidedly above every other individual of his tribe in talent.

Wild-Cat I have spoken of already — our Indian Falstaff, cowardice and falsehood excepted.

Kau-ray-kaw-saw-kaw was there as well, "the White Crow," a Rock River Indian, who later made a name for himself as a friend to the whites through the Sauk war. The French called him "le Borgne," because he had lost an eye, and he wore a black silk handkerchief drooping over the left side of his face to cover the loss, which, with the rest of his native dress, made him a very singular figure to look at.

There was a nephew of the dead chief Four-Legs whom whites and Indians alike called "the Dandy," and no one ever disputed the justice of it. When he was out of mourning his dress was as studied and as fanciful as he could contrive it. A shirt, on the days he condescended to wear one, in the brightest colors going, covered with row upon row of silver brooches set close together. Never fewer than two pairs of silver arm-bands. Leggings and moccasins worked in ribbon and porcupine-quill embroidery of the most elaborate kind. Every ornament he could invent hung off the club of hair at the back of his head. A feather fan in one hand, and in the other a mirror, into which he looked at himself about every five minutes. Add the colors on his face, in their variety and brilliancy, the choosing and laying on of which took up no small share of the hours he gave to his toilet, and that was young Four-Legs, complete.

In a young dandy all that devotion to appearance seems natural enough. But we had an old one of exactly the same stamp. Pawnee Blanc, the White Pawnee, went past his younger rival in the care of his personal attractions, if such a thing were possible.

He arrived that day in the whole of his finery, made the customary salutations with an air of solemn dignity, and then walked through, as the rest of them did, into the parlor — I had received them in the hall — where they all sat themselves down on the floor. It was lucky for me that the room stood bare of furniture just then. But "alas!" thought I, "for my pretty carpet, if this is to be the way they pay their respects to me!" I sat and watched the ash dropping from those long pipes, and the other inconveniences that come with tobacco, or kin-nee-kin-nick, in absolute dismay.

After the chiefs came the interpreter and his wife, and with them all the Canadian and half-breed women whose husbands had work at the Agency or at the American Fur Company's establishment.

By that time my piano had been got out of its case and set up in our quarters, and to our great joy there was not a mark on it. Thanks to the skill of Nunns and Clark, not one note had gone out of tune.

The women had never seen such a thing in their lives, and their wonder and delight came out of them at the top of their voices.

"Eh-h-h! regardez donc! Quelles inventions! Quelles merveilles!" One of them caught sight of my fingers moving in the reflection on the nameboard and called out to the others in triumph. She believed she had found the hidden machinery that made the sounds, and she was not a little mortified when she learned better.
