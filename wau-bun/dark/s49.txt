The Canadian voyageurs — the engagés — are very nearly gone from the country now, and some notice of them belongs here.

No other class of men resembled them. Whatever it is that makes a poet, they had the equivalent of it for this work, and they came into the world already fitted for it. They were tough, they were tireless, they were clever with their hands, and they were cheerful past all reason. Set any emergency in front of them and their minds bent around it. Difficulty did not baffle them. Hardship did not put them off. And they loved easily and hard: they formed attachments of the warmest kind to their bourgeois, the master who hired them, and to the native people among whom their engagements carried them.

Montreal was the depôt — Marrialle, in their own mouths. That was where the agents went when they had a quota to fill for one of the companies or for some independent trader, and that was where they picked over the material and made their selections.

The wages ran from four to six hundred livres a year, in the old Quebec currency. The rations were one quart of lyed corn and two ounces of tallow a day, or, as the paper had it, "its equivalent in whatever sort of food is to be found in the Indian country." Men have been known to live a whole winter on fresh fish and maple-sugar, every other supply cut off from them, and to do it cheerfully.

There was a saying about them: "Keep an engagé to his corn and tallow, he will serve you well - give him pork and bread, and he soon gets beyond your management." A bargain, to them, meant exactly what its words said and nothing else, which is how the crew of the old trader M. Berthelet came to grief. He signed them at Montreal on the terms that they should eat when their bourgeois ate, and eat what he ate. He meant it as a piece of fun. The simple Canadians took it for a rare stroke of luck, and for such luxurious prospects as those they counted themselves fortunate men. Berthelet, before they started, had stuffed his pockets with crackers. Now and then, when he was quite certain nobody had an eye on him, he slipped one out and ate it. The pipes went round, and went round again. The men grew hungry. They watched for some sign that a meal was being got ready for the bourgeois, and no such sign came, and since he was fasting they held their tongues and fasted with him.

Then it stopped being a joke and turned serious, and they could not carry it any further. They asked, in some distress, to be let out of the bargain, and settled gladly for the ordinary rations in place of the dainties they had been promising themselves.

At Mackinac, which was where the whole fur trade came through, a man drew a small portion of his wages in advance to buy his winter outfit — his pipes and tobacco, his needles and thread, some lengths of bright-colored ribbon, red and yellow gartering, which is quality binding. Those were the small coin he would trade to the Indians for whatever little things he wanted. If he was bound for Lake Superior, or for some post so far north that such articles were not to be had at all, he added one or two smoked deer-skins to make moccasins from.

Fitted out that way, he entered his three years. Labor through the day; and then, when evening handed him back to himself, the laughing and the joking and the singing and the long stories.

Here and there you found a man who held to the exact letter of his agreement about what kind of work he had hired on to do. As a rule, though, an engagé stood ready for whatever his bourgeois asked of him, and was as faithful in it as any vassal ever was to his chief.

They tell a story about M. St. Jean, who traded on the Upper Mississippi. He gave one of his Frenchmen an order: go out with the others, into the forest, and cut wood. The man refused. "He wasn't hired," he said, "to chop wood."

"Ah! for what, then, were you hired?"

"To steer a boat."

"Very well, steer a boat, then, since you prefer it."

It was mid-winter. They walked the objector down to the river and set him in the stern of the boat, which lay where it was, held fast in the ice.

Two hours of the employment he had contracted for, with the thermometer below zero, and he was perfectly content to go and take his place among the choppers. He never afterward thought it wise to choose his own work.

There is an aristocracy inside the service, and it is a comical thing to watch. The engagement generally runs three years. A first-year man is a mangeur-de-lard, a pork-eater, and an hivernant — one who has a winter in the country behind him — looks down on him with a contempt that has something sovereign in it. He will not associate with him. If the pork-eater invites him to take a friendly glass, he will manufacture some excuse and decline it. The most inveterate drunkard among them will be tortured by wanting that glass and still never let himself be coaxed across the line. The custom holds.

Once his first winter is behind him the mangeur-de-lard rises out of the freshman class, and takes his own turn at lording it over whoever comes next.

They have another habit as well: taking the name of a bourgeois and working it into some absurdity that sounds nearly like it. Kinzie came back from one man as Quinze nez, fifteen noses, and from another as Singé, monkeyfied. Mr. Kercheval was Mons. Court-cheval, short horse. The Judge of Probate was le Juge Trop-bête, too foolish. Here is a case that shows how far it can be carried.

Mr. Shaw, an agent of the Northwest Fur Company, had spent a great many years on the frontier, and to the voyageurs he was Monsieur Le Chat. On quitting the Indian country he married a Canadian lady, and children came — several of them. His old foreman, Louis la Liberté, arrived in Montreal to pass a winter there some years after his return to Canada, and word of the marriage had reached him, and nothing would do but that he see his old bourgeois again.

He got his chance in the Champ de Mars, where Mr. Shaw was walking with two officers. La Liberté spotted him, ran straight over, caught him by both hands, and greeted him — "Ah! mon cher Monsieur le Chat: comment vous portez-vous?" (My dear Mr. Cat, how do you do?)

"Très-bien, Louizon."

"Et comment se porte Madame la Chatte?" (How is the mother cat?)

"Bien, bien, Louizon, elle est très-bien" (She is very well.)

"Et tous les petits Chatons?" (And all the kittens?)

That was further than Mr. Shaw could go. He said shortly that the kittens were all well, turned away with his military friends, and left poor Louizon standing there thoroughly astonished at how abruptly it had ended.

That was the shape of their isolation — everything beyond them shut away, the garrison their only society — and inside it the family of Mr. Kinzie lived contentedly enough, with every comfort about them and nearly all the luxuries besides.

The friendship they gave the Indians came back to them, and what it grew into was no ordinary attachment. Eight years passed from Mr. Kinzie's first coming among them. Then 1812 came, and showed exactly what that attachment was.
