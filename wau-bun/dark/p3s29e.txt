Not long after Man-Eater's people had taken their leave, another party of Winnebagoes arrived, and these asked leave to dance for their Father.

The compliment was accepted. They gathered, as they always did, on the esplanade in front of the house. My sister, the children, and I took our usual places at the open windows, and my husband sat down on the broad step at the door, the door that opens straight from the outside air into the parlor where we were.

The dance began. They went round and round, each man following the back of the man ahead of him, and as they came past my sister, Mrs. Helm, said to me, "Look at that small, dark Indian, with the green boughs on his person — that is a Sauk! They always mark themselves in this manner with white clay, and ornament themselves with leaves when they dance!" I had never seen that costume on any of our own people. I looked at him properly then: green chaplets bound round his head and round his legs, and his gun wreathed the same way, and on his body no paint at all except the white transverse streaks laid over the whole of it. He was a stranger. I want to be exact, because fear will improve a face. It did not need to improve his. His face was ferocious.

He carried the gun in his hand, and every circuit of the dance brought him directly in front of where we sat, and every time he came he turned his eyes full on us and clubbed the weapon in front of him, and we read defiance in it. We sat still as death. To show any sign of fear would have been the worst thing we could do, and we both knew it. Then my sister said, quite low, "I have always thought that I was to lose my life by the hands of the Indians. This is the third Indian war I have gone through, and now, I think, it will be the last."

It was the only time in my life I ever saw her lose her self-possession. She was the calmest and most resolute woman I have known, and I could see now that she was trembling. And still we sat there. There was a kind of fascination in it — the more frightened we became, the less able we were to look away. Then a few drops of rain began to fall. The Indians danced on for a minute or two more, and then, all at once, with whooping and shouting, they rushed the house. We fled into my room and shut the door. My sister held it fast at first, then let go of it and came and sat beside me on the bed, because she saw that I could not get hold of myself. Of all the ways there are to die, death at the hands of savages is the hardest one to face calmly, and I believed with my whole mind that our hour had come.

The dance never stopped. They simply carried it into the parlor and went on with it there, leaping and yelling until it seemed the roof would come down on top of us. Through a crack in the door we begged my husband and the children to come in with us. The children would not — they were having far too fine a time, and it had not entered their heads that anything was wrong. My husband came for a moment to reassure me and then judged it wisest to go back out, and stand there, and show by his presence that he was pleased by the compliment. He made light of our fears. He would not allow that the object of our suspicions was any Sauk at all, but only some young Winnebago got up in their fashion, which is a thing they sometimes do.

It may well have been good fun to him, to go home to his village and tell how he frightened the white squaws. A white boy would do the same trick without thinking twice about it. Human nature being everywhere much the same, I do not see why it should be so unlikely in an Indian.
