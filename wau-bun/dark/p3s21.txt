Watching the Indians come in, and watching them go out again, was a standing outrage to my sense of woman's rights. As a rule the master of the family came along at his leisure, carrying his gun and perhaps a lance. The woman came behind him with the mats and the poles of the whole lodge on her shoulders, her pappoose if she had one, her kettles, her sacks of corn and wild rice, and not seldom the family dog riding on the top of the load. If there happened to be a horse or a pony among the family's possessions, the man rode it and the squaw walked after.

This lopsided division of the work does not come from any lack of kind or affectionate feeling in the husband. It is more the instinct of the sex to assert its superior standing and importance whenever a decent opportunity to do it comes along. Away from anybody's eyes, where there is no danger to his dignity, the husband is willing enough to take some of the burden custom puts on his wife, and to carry a share of her labors and her hardships himself.

The payment had not gone by without its proper number of complimentary dances and medicine dances. The medicine dance comes only at long intervals. The complimentary dance comes whenever an occasion turns up that calls for a show of respect and courtesy.

The custom is to go first to the person to be complimented and ask leave to dance for him. Leave granted, the preparation begins: the face painted with great care, the body marked — it is generally bare across the chest and shoulders — after whatever pattern is most approved. Every ornament that can be got hold of goes into the hair or the headdress. And happy is the man who, having taken a scalp or more than one, has the right to announce it with the matching number of eagle's feathers.

The less fortunate make do with the feathers of a wild turkey, or, better yet, of the first unlucky rooster that crosses their path. My poor fowls, all through the time of the payment, were plucked absolutely bare.

When they are ready, the dancers gather at some convenient place and come marching to the appointed ground with the Indian drum and the shee-shee-qua, or rattle, going. They form a circle and dance with violent contortions and gestures, some of them graceful and some of them only strenuous. The squaws stand a little off to one side and put their own harsh voices in among the instruments, and they rarely dance themselves. Sometimes, though, when the general gaiety has got into them, a few will make a circle outside the men and do a kind of ungraceful up-and-down movement whose only merit is its perfect time — and the Indians, without exception, seem born with an ear for time. The dance ends only when the dancers have nothing left in them. Then a quantity of presents are brought and piled in the middle of the circle by order of the person who was complimented. One of their own number divides it all out fairly, and, the whole purpose of the display having been served, they go.

The medicine dance is held mainly to celebrate the Medicine-man's skill in curing disease. He belongs to a fraternity credited with the further power of interpreting the will of the Great Spirit concerning the conduct of his people. He sometimes makes offerings and sacrifices that are understood to be propitiatory, and in that sense the word priest fits him. He is a prophet too, so far as he is in a limited way a teacher — but he makes no claim to foretell what is coming.

A man is picked for the fraternity of the Medicine-man by those already in it, and generally because they have noticed some skill or shrewdness in him. Sometimes a man who has been dangerously ill and has recovered under the treatment of one of the members is judged a fit choice, on the strength of the claim that recovery establishes.

When he is to be initiated, there is a great feast, paid for, naturally, by the candidate himself, because in plain life as in civilized life the same rule of politics holds: honors must be paid for. An animal is killed and dressed and the people at large eat of it, and there are dances and songs and speeches without end. Then the chief Medicine-man takes the candidate aside and instructs him privately in all the ceremonies and all the knowledge that make a member complete. Now and then the new member chosen is still a child. In that case the Medicine-man takes him as soon as he is old enough and brings him up by instruction and example into a creditable member of the fraternity.

There is a good deal of belief in magic among the Winnebagoes. Every Medicine-man has a bag or sack that is understood to have some animal shut inside it, and in the course of his pow-wows he speaks to that animal, calling out in the cry proper to the species he supposes it to be. The people appear entirely satisfied that the answers he announces are real communications, in that form, from the Great Spirit.

The Indians seem to have no notion of any reckoning beyond this life. They hold hard to the great fundamental virtues of natural religion — the worship of the Great Spirit, brotherly love, love of one's children, honesty, temperance, chastity. Any breach of the Great Spirit's laws, by a falling away from those virtues, they believe will rouse his anger and bring punishment down. Those are their principles. That their practice shows a wider and wider departure from them, under the debasing influence of living close to the whites, is a melancholy truth, and no one will admit it with so much sorrow as the people who lived among them, and thought well of them, before that change set in.

One of the first improvements that occurred to us about the new house was getting rid of some very ugly pickets standing around two or three Indian graves on the esplanade out in front. Burial-places are held in such reverence, though, that we understood we would have to come at the subject with a great deal of delicacy and care.

My husband did finally venture to propose to Mrs. Pawnee Blanc, the nearest living relative of the person buried there, that the pickets be replaced with a neat wooden platform.

The idea pleased her very much, since through her closeness to Paquette's family she had picked up something of a taste for civilization. So a little platform about a foot high, properly finished off with a moulding around the edge, went in where the worn black pickets had been, and it was a touching thing to see the mournful satisfaction with which two or three old women came every evening at sunset, and sat there gossiping over the ashes of their dead.

On fine moonlit nights, too, you would often see a group of them sitting there in what is to them a solemn hour, for they hold the poetic belief that the moon was made to give light to the dead.

The reverence the Indians pay to the memory of their departed friends, and the faithfulness with which they visit their graves and make offerings there to the Great Spirit, is an example their more enlightened brethren would do well to copy. Some of their other customs regarding the dead are another matter.
