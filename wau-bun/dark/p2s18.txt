The members of Mr. Kinzie's family who had stayed in the boat near the mouth of the river were carefully guarded by Kee-po-tah and another Indian. They saw the smoke first. Then the blaze. Then the report of that first tremendous discharge reached them. After that it was all confusion. They knew nothing at all until they saw an Indian coming toward them from the battle-ground, leading a horse with a lady on it who appeared to be wounded.

"That is Mrs. Heald," cried Mrs. Kinzie. "That Indian will kill her. Run, Chandonnai" — to one of Mr. Kinzie's clerks — "take the mule that is tied there, and offer it to him to release her."

By this time her captor was working the bonnet off her head so that he could scalp her. Chandonnai ran up and offered him the mule as a ransom, and ten bottles of whiskey besides, as soon as they reached his village. The whiskey was a strong temptation.

"But," said the Indian, "she is badly wounded — she will die. Will you give me the whiskey in any case?"

Chandonnai promised he would, and the bargain was made. The savage put the lady's bonnet on his own head. Some squaws tried to take her shoes and stockings and did not succeed. Then she was brought aboard the boat, where she lay moaning with the pain of the many bullet-wounds she had taken in both arms.

The horse Mrs. Heald had been riding was a fine, spirited animal. The Indians wanted it undamaged, so they had aimed their shots to disable the rider without touching the steed.

She had not been lying in the boat long when they saw a young Indian of savage aspect coming toward them. A buffalo robe was pulled hastily over her, and she was told to make no sound of complaint at all, if she valued her life.

The heroic woman stayed perfectly silent while he came on. He had a pistol in his hand. He rested it on the side of the boat and looked in, prying, with a fearful scowl on his face. Black Jim, one of the servants, was standing in the bow. He took up an axe that lay near him and made a sign that if the man fired he would split his skull, and told him the boat held nobody but the family of Shaw-nee-aw-kee. The Indian withdrew. It came out afterward that the person he was looking for was Mr. Burnett, a trader from St. Joseph's, with whom he had some account to settle.

When the boat was at last allowed to go back to Mr. Kinzie's mansion, and Mrs. Heald was carried up to the house, her wounds had to be dressed.

Mr. K. asked an old chief who was standing by — who, like most of his tribe, had some skill in surgery — to take a ball out of the sufferer's arm.

"No, father," he answered. "I can't do it — it makes me sick here." And he put his hand on his heart.

So Mr. Kinzie did it himself, with his penknife.
