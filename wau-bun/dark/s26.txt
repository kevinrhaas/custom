The boats were only a few days off, so it seemed sensible to start on whatever part of the housekeeping I could manage without them. That came down to cutting and stitching my carpets, and Mrs. Twiggs was kind enough to sit and sew with me. The wife of one of our Frenchmen came over from the Agency and put the rooms to rights and made them comfortable, and the carpets went down under tacks not long after. After that the rooms stood empty and waiting for the rest of the furniture.

Getting a servant-woman to come out with me had defeated me over and over, first in Detroit and then at Green Bay. Now and then one would offer herself. Then, before anything was settled between us, something would work on her — how far it was, or the savages, or what the trip itself would be like, or, as often as not, what her friends had to say about it — and the bargain would come apart in my hands. In the end I had nothing to fall back on but my husband's assurance that one of his French employés and the man's wife might be willing to live under our roof with us.

That was where the matter stood when Major Twiggs offered a way around all of it. He had a young colored girl in his household, brought up from Buffalo in the spring to wait on Mrs. T. until her own servants could come north from the South, and he proposed that we take the girl into our service.

So Louisa was sent for. She was an uncommonly handsome young negress with an intelligent face that she kept very demure, and she gave her age as fifteen. She cannot have been fifteen. What I learned later of the distance she had already travelled into vice and iniquity would have taken several more years than that. But none of that was visible to me then. Then she seemed to have nothing wrong with her but carelessness and inexperience, and I had every hope that careful training would cure her of both.

My first week's visit at Mrs. Twiggs's had just run out when the word went round that the boats were in sight — the boats with our furniture in them. If Louis Philippe had been expected on a visit to Queen Victoria the sensation could hardly have been greater than the one that announcement made in our little community. We knew perfectly well that hours had to pass before they could reach the place where they would unload, and we watched anyway, constantly. In the end every young officer in the garrison, with as many of the soldiers as were off duty behind them, went down the bank to the landing with Mr. Kinzie, to see the thing done and to lend a hand if a hand was wanted getting it all safely ashore.

What came ashore was in a sad state. Water ran out of the corners of the boxes as they were swung up one after another onto the bank. Nobody had the patience to wait for them to be carried up to the fort. The gentlemen found themselves hammers and hatchets and went at the lids then and there, to learn how bad it was. The mahogany was ruined. There was not one piece of it whose edges and veneering had not begun to lift and curl away. It looked as though it had lain days under the Grande Chûte. Poor Hamilton was loud with his protests and his excuses.

The men had done it. The weather had done it. The packing had done it. "Confound it! he had taken the best care of the things he possibly could - better than he had ever taken before - it would get done!"

There was nothing for it but patience and making do. And when the pretty sideboard and the work-table had been rubbed and rubbed and set on their feet, and the little knick-knacks stood along the mantel-piece, and the white curtains were up at the windows, and the chairs and the dining-table had each found its place with respect to the piano, our parlor was pronounced "magnificent." Hamilton certainly thought so. He came up to look at it once, admiringly, and to hear the piano played, which was a thing entirely new to him. What he told the young officers about it after he got back to the Bay carried all his wonder in it — "There it stood on its four legs! Anybody might go up and touch it!"

In time everything found a home. The dinner- and tea-sets were bestowed carefully in the "Davis," along with the jars of sweetmeats I had put up in Detroit. The iron and tin things went into a neat kitchen cupboard whose frame was my piano-box. The barrel of eggs and the tubs of butter, carried the whole way from Ohio, were ranged in the store-room. We bought a suitable quantity of salt pork and flour from the commissary, and of game there was never any shortage of any kind, that being the offering of our red children. We were ready to keep house.

A young housekeeper's first dinner in her own home is an era, and I will never forget mine. I was down in the lower regions standing over my very inexpert little cook when my husband appeared to tell me that the payment — which was the one subject anybody could think about just then — would not begin until the afternoon, and that he had accordingly asked M. Rolette, Mr. Hempstead, and four other gentlemen to dine with us.

"So unexpected - so unprepared for?"

"Never mind, give them anything you have. They have been living for some days in tents, and anything will taste well to them."

I had meant the dinner to be mostly a venison pasty, and I was saved by the single dish in my possession large enough to be worth mentioning, because there was already a pie smoking in the oven that came near the size of the famous Norwich pudding. A few small additions to the bill of fare and we came off perfectly well, and the master of the house had the pleasure of hearing his six guests praise that impromptu dinner a great deal.
