On the third day after the battle they put the family of Mr. Kinzie into a boat, with the clerks of the establishment, under the care of François, a half-breed interpreter, and took them up the lake to St. Joseph's. They stayed there until the following November, under the protection of To-pee-nee-bee's band. Then Chandonnai and their trusty Indian friend Kee-po-tah escorted them to Detroit and delivered them up, as prisoners of war, to Colonel McKee, the British Indian Agent.

Mr. Kinzie was not allowed to leave St. Joseph's with his family. His Indian friends insisted that he remain and try to recover some remnant of his scattered property, and when he went out with them on those errands he wore the costume and the paint of the tribe, because there were men still moving through that country who were not finished, and a white face was all the reason they needed. In time his anxiety for his family outweighed the property. He followed them to Detroit, where, in January, General Proctor received him and paroled him.

Captain and Mrs. Heald were sent across the lake to St. Joseph the day after the battle. He had taken two wounds in the engagement. She had taken seven.

Lieutenant Helm was wounded too. Friendly Indians carried him to their village on the Au Sable, and from there to Peoria, where Mr. Thomas Forsyth — Mr. Kinzie's half-brother — intervened and got him liberated. Mrs. Helm had gone with her parents to St. Joseph. They lived there for several months in the family of Alexander Robinson, and were shown every kindness those people had to give.

After they reached Detroit her husband joined her, and then both of them were arrested by order of the British commander and sent on horseback, in the dead of winter, through Canada to Fort George on the Niagara frontier. They rode that whole distance in the worst weather that country makes. When they finally came to the post, no official had been appointed to receive them, and so Mrs. Helm — a delicate woman of seventeen — was left sitting in her saddle outside the gate. She sat there more than an hour. Nobody brought her fire. Nobody brought her food. Nobody opened a door and offered her a roof. Colonel Sheaffe had been absent when they arrived, and when he was told what had been done he was openly furious about it. He waited on Mrs. Helm at once, apologized to her with the greatest courtesy, and from that hour treated her and Lieutenant Helm with the most considerate kindness, until an exchange of prisoners set them free and they found means to reach their friends in Steuben County, New York.

Captain Heald's captor was an Indian from the Kankakee who held a strong personal regard for him. When the man saw the state Mrs. Heald was in — wounded seven times, and failing — he released her husband so that he could go with her to St. Joseph, and Chandonnai and his party carried them there, as has been told. The Indian went home to his village on the Kankakee. There he found that what he had done had not been forgiven. His band was so displeased with him, and showed it so plainly, that he made up his mind to go to St. Joseph and take his prisoner back.

Word of that intention reached To-pee-nee-bee and Kee-po-tah, who had the prisoners in their care. They held a private council with Chandonnai, Mr. Kinzie, and the principal men of the village, and what they decided was to put the Healds out of reach entirely: to send Captain and Mrs. Heald to the island of Mackinac and deliver them up to the British.

So the two of them were put into a bark canoe and paddled three hundred miles up the coast of Michigan by Robinson and his wife, and surrendered as prisoners of war to the commanding officer at Mackinac.

As a measure of the procrastinating spirit of Captain Heald, it is worth recording that even after he had certain intelligence that his Indian captor was on the road from the Kankakee to St. Joseph to retake him, he would still have spent another day at that place, making preparation for a more comfortable trip to Mackinac.

The soldiers, with their wives and the children who had lived, were scattered among the Pottowattamie villages on the Illinois, the Wabash, Rock River, and at Milwaukie, and there they stayed until the following spring, when most of them were carried to Detroit and ransomed.
