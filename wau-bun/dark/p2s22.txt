Three days after the battle they loaded Mr. Kinzie's family and the clerks of the establishment into a boat and sent them off in the charge of François, a half-breed interpreter, down to St. Joseph's. There they remained until the November following, kept safe by To-pee-nee-bee's band. When they finally moved again it was to Detroit, with Chandonnai and their trusty Indian friend Kee-po-tah riding escort, and at the end of that road they were handed over as prisoners of war to Colonel McKee, the British Indian Agent.

Mr. Kinzie did not go with them out of St. Joseph's. His Indian friends would not hear of it; he was to stay, and to try what could be salvaged of the property that had been scattered. When he went out with them on those errands he went dressed and painted as one of the tribe, because there were men still abroad with a thirst for blood in them, and a white man they recognized could be taken, or worse. In the end his fear for his family outweighed all of it and he followed them to Detroit, and there, in January, General Proctor received him and gave him his parole.

Captain and Mrs. Heald went over the lake to St. Joseph the day after the battle. He had come out of the fight with two wounds in him. She had seven.

Lieutenant Helm was wounded as well. Friendly Indians carried him to their village on the Au Sable, and from there he was taken to Peoria, where Mr. Thomas Forsyth, half-brother to Mr. Kinzie, stepped in and got him set free. Mrs. Helm had gone with her parents to St. Joseph. For several months they lived in the household of Alexander Robinson, and there was no kindness or hospitality that family did not show them.

Her husband caught up with her after they reached Detroit, and the two of them were promptly arrested on the order of the British commander and sent off on horseback, in the depth of winter, across Canada to Fort George on the Niagara frontier. Nobody at that post had been appointed to receive them. After all those days of riding, in weather as cold and raw as that country can produce, Mrs. Helm — a delicate girl of seventeen — was left sitting her saddle outside the gate for more than an hour before anyone thought to offer her fire, or food, or so much as a roof over her head. Colonel Sheaffe had been away when it happened. When he was told of that piece of brutality he made no secret of his anger. He went to Mrs. Helm at once, apologized to her with the utmost courtesy, and from then until an exchange of prisoners set them at liberty he treated her and Lieutenant Helm with every consideration. They found their own way at last to their friends in Steuben County, N.Y.

The man who had taken Captain Heald prisoner was an Indian from the Kankakee who was genuinely fond of him. Seeing how badly Mrs. Heald was wounded and how little strength she had left, he let her husband go so that he could travel with her to St. Joseph, and they were carried there by Chandonnai and his party, as has been told. Then he went home to his village on the Kankakee — and found that his own people were furious with him for what he had done. The anger of the band ran so high that he made up his mind to go to St. Joseph himself and take his prisoner back.

Word of that reached To-pee-nee-bee and Kee-po-tah, who had charge of the prisoners, and they sat down in private council with Chandonnai, with Mr. Kinzie, and with the leading men of the village. What they decided was to put Captain and Mrs. Heald out of reach altogether: to send them to the island of Mackinac and hand them over to the British.

So the two of them were placed in a bark canoe and paddled three hundred miles up the Michigan shore by Robinson and his wife, and at the end of it were surrendered as prisoners of war to the officer commanding at Mackinac.

Something should be said here about Captain Heald's habit of putting things off. He had by then certain word that his Indian captor was coming from the Kankakee to St. Joseph to retake him — and even so he would have stayed one more day where he was, in order to make the trip to Mackinac a more comfortable one.

As for the soldiers, and the wives and the children who had lived through it, they were parceled out among the villages of the Pottowattamies on the Illinois, on the Wabash, on Rock River, and at Milwaukie, and there they stayed until the spring, when most of them were brought in to Detroit and ransomed.
