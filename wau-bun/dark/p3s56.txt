It had come to that point at last: we began to talk in earnest about giving up the home we loved and settling ourselves at Detroit for a time, while the arrangements went forward for a permanent place at Chicago.

When the news reached our Winnebago children, the grief of it was loud and it did not stop. They came in from all the country round about, wanting to know whether what they had heard was true, and begging us — pleading, in earnest — that we would stay and live and die among them.

Of all of them, none was so broken by it as Elizabeth, our poor Cut-Nose. On the day we first told her, she sat down in one spot and stayed there hour after hour, taking the corner of the chintz shawl she wore pinned across her bosom to blot the tears as fast as they came down her face.

"No! I never, never, never shall I find such friends again," she would exclaim. "You will go away, and I shall be left here all alone."

Wild-Cat grieved too — fat, jolly Wild-Cat — and he made no attempt to grieve quietly.

On the morning we left, he would not be satisfied until he had the baby in his arms and settled on his fat, dirty knee. "Oh, my little brother," he told him, "you will never come back to see your poor brother again!"

He had taken an extra glass for the occasion, and he cried like an infant.

So on the morning of the 1st of July, 1888, with heavy hearts, we said our good-byes to the long cortége that had walked us down to the boat. She was waiting to carry us to Green Bay, where Governor Porter and Mr. Brush would meet us and take us on to Detroit under their escort.

Their farewells finished, they turned about to go with their father across the Portage, on his road to Chicago. We watched them a long while, the line of them bending along the road, and we heard them wailing still — a parting they knew already was for good.
