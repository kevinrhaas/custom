After the council broke up, Mr. Kinzie went to Captain Heald and talked with him a long time. Mr. Kinzie understood the Indian character, and he understood the particular temper of these Indians at this hour, and he hoped to open the captain's eyes to how matters actually stood.

He reminded him that ever since the troubles on the Wabash there had been a settled plan of hostilities against the whites, and that because of it the policy of the Americans had been to withhold from the tribes anything that would help them carry that war to the defenceless people on the frontier.

He reminded him, too, of his own conduct the previous autumn. He had set out for Detroit, and had got as far as De Charme's when word reached him of the battle of Tippecanoe. He turned around at once and came back to Chicago so that he could send orders to his traders to furnish no ammunition to the Indians. All they had on hand was hidden. Those traders who had not already left for their wintering-grounds went out carrying neither powder nor shot.

Captain Heald was struck by the folly of arming the enemy — for that is what the old neighbors would have to be called now — against himself. He determined to destroy all the ammunition except what his own troops would need.

On the 13th the goods were distributed as agreed: blankets, broadcloths, calicoes, paints. That evening the ammunition and the liquor were moved. Part went into the sally-port and down a well that had been dug there to supply the garrison with water in an emergency. The rest was carried out as quietly as they could manage through the northern gate, the barrel-heads knocked in, and the contents poured into the river.

A large quantity of alcohol belonging to Mr. Kinzie went the same way. It had been stored in a warehouse near his house, across from the fort.

The Indians suspected something was happening. They crept in serpent-like, as close to the work as they could get, but the watch was vigilant and no one was allowed near except the men engaged in it. Every musket not needed for the march was broken up and thrown down the well, and after them the bags of shot, the flints, the gunscrews — everything, in short, that had anything to do with a weapon of offense.
