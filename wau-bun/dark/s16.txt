Thirteen miles was all the river had given us since the Kakalin, and we had hauled and strained and paid for every one of them. The light was nearly gone by the time the Grande Chûte came into view, and it was there, close under it, that we were to sleep.

We had already put the Little Chûte behind us — the place where the town of Appleton stands today — and I noted nothing whatever about it except the sheer quantity of extra labor it cost us to fight that rushing water and come out of the fight the winners.

The last of the sun lay along the high wooded banks, and down between them came the beautiful foaming rush of the Chûte. The men swung the boat toward a small headland on the left bank, chosen because a long level strip ran back from it, room enough and to spare for a good camp. I was over the side before they had fairly pulled her up, and with the Judge to steady me I went as quickly as I could manage to a point farther down the river, where he promised the Chûte showed itself best. I wanted a sketch of it. I wanted it before the daylight was entirely gone, and there was not much of the daylight left.

The left bank stood to the west, and where it dipped lower than the rest the parting rays came through and fell on our little scene: the boat, the men in their red caps and belts, the two tents already standing. Smoke was just beginning to lift off the evening fires. Behind all of it rose the high wooded bank with the steep portage path barely traceable up its face, and beyond that, farther off and already darkening under the overhanging forests, the long stretch of the fall. It made a lovely landscape. Only an artist's pencil could have done it any justice.

It was my first encampment, and the newness of every single thing in it enchanted me.

The fires were small ones, saplings and underbrush gathered in a hurry; the weather was mild enough that we wanted nothing beyond what would cook a supper and dry the men's clothing. The soldiers had pitched their tent some way off from ours — not so far, though, that we could not hear them perfectly well, laughing, enjoying themselves, after a day that had used them all up.

Corporal Kilgour kept a careful eye on them, and their high spirits never once crossed out of respectful propriety. Our own supper was cooked in the open air with a tea-kettle and a frying-pan and nothing else at all, and by the time it was eaten there was not one of us who was not ready to lie down.

What woke us at dawn the next morning was the bourgeois, calling across the camp, a call built to carry:

"How! how! how!" — uttered at the very top of his voice.

Nobody sleeps through that. The men come out of their tents, or pick themselves up off the ground beside the fire, and begin turning themselves toward the duties of the day.

The fire is fed. The kettles go on to boil. The mess-baskets are opened and enough is lifted out of them to make a breakfast. One of the Frenchmen spreads our mat inside the tent — the bedding is already up and folded and waiting to be stowed in the boat — and the tin cups and the plates are set out around the new-fashioned table-cloth. The dew is too heavy this morning for us to eat outdoors; on a drier one the whole business would be laid out on the green grass instead. Then, faster than seems possible, the coffee is steaming in front of us and the ham is broiled, and now and again there is toast besides, brought hot and fresh off the glowing coals.

Say what you please: there is no breakfast on earth like one eaten out in the woods, with a well-trained Frenchman running the ceremony of it.

What the men turned to that morning was hard labor — getting the heavy boat up the Chûte. The piano was judged safest left where it stood on board, but everything else had to come out and go up the steep bank by hand, then along the top of it for some hundreds of rods, to the smooth water on the far side, where all the difficulties of our navigation came to an end.

The Judge kindly took me off my husband's hands while "the bourgeois" superintended that important business, and between reading and sketching and wandering about, the morning slipped away from me. Twelve o'clock came, and the preparations for starting were still not finished.

I was out hunting the finest of the wild flowers, meaning to have a bouquet made up before my husband came back, when I walked into the soldiers' camp-fire. A tall young man was minding a kettle over it — red-faced, light-haired, in fatigue dress — and whatever was in that kettle smelled very good indeed.

He noticed me watching him work, and he ladled out a tin-cupful of it and politely held the cup toward me.

I said no, and thanked him, and told him our own dinner would be ready directly.

"They left me here to get their dinner," he said — he seemed glad enough of somebody to talk to — "and I thought I might as well make some soup. Down on the German Flats, where I come from, they always like soup."

"Ah! you are from the German Flats - then your name must be Bellinger or Weber."

"No, it isn't - it's Krissman."

"Well, Krissman, how do you like the service?"

"Very well. I was only recruited last summer. I used to ride horse on the Canawl, and, as I can blow a horn first-rate, I expect I will soon be able to play on a bugle, and then, when I get to be musician, you know, I shall have extra pay."

I had not known it, but I made all the proper pleased noises anyway, and left him to his kettle with every good wish for his dreams of ambition — though ambition is not quite the word for them. Avarice is nearer the mark. It was plainly the extra pay he had his eye on, and not the fame. Then I went back to my own quarters.

My husband has French tastes, and he was a little cast down when I told him what had happened and what I had turned away. We were both repaid soon enough. The tall awkward figure came over to us carrying his kettle, set the composition down in front of the two gentlemen, and had the infinite satisfaction of hearing it pronounced excellent.

When everything was at last in readiness, the tents came down and went around the Portage, and the three of us — my husband, the Judge, and myself — came along behind at our own pace.

The woods were brilliant with flowers. It was late enough in the season that the glory of the summer was nearly behind us, and still the lupin and the moss-pink and the yellow wallflower were out, and with them every variety of the helianthus, the aster, and the solidago. The gentlemen pulled down clusters of the bittersweet (celastrus scandens) from the boughs overhead and worked them into a wreath for my hat as we went, and the tangled path we went by was Christabel's own, and like hers it was "Now in glimmer and now in gloom,"

through one open glade and then a shady thicket and then another glade. And soon, like that same lovely heroine, "We reached the place - right glad we were,"

and nothing kept us then. We were aboard the little boat again and skimming out over water that had gone perfectly placid.
