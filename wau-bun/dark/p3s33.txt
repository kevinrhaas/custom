The "Mariner" came in at last, early, with the light still low on the water. Within an hour of her tying up, every house from one end of the settlement to the other had what she carried — "the cholera was in this country! It was in Detroit - it was among the troops who were on their way to the seat of war! Whole companies had died of it in the river St. Clair, and the survivors had been put on shore at Port Gratiot, to save their lives as best they might!" A savage enemy on one side of us and a pestilence on the other, and no ground between them to stand on.

Anyone with people in the East took it hardest. It went through the place and left men hollowed out. The first to bring it to us was an officer whose courage no one had ever thought to question — he had proved it on the battle-field and proved it again in private ventures that asked more daring than most men have in them. He got the words out. Then he laid his head against the window-sill and wept like a child.

Those who had to reach people near and dear to them went out again aboard the "Mariner." The rest of us judged that where we already stood was the safer place, and we were right: the scourge never came to Green Bay that season.

The heat that summer was punishing, and the mosquitoes came in such numbers that none of us so much as pretended to walk the parade after sundown without two fans, or green branches, kept swinging every moment to drive them off — which is, as it happens, the surest way in the world to bring them on. We had somehow let ourselves forget the flat indifference of Old Smoker, which used to astonish us: he would sit without moving and let his bare arms and body go literally gray with them, and then wipe off a handful, the blood following the track of his hand across the skin, and settle back and stoically wait for the chance to do it again. They say a mosquito left undisturbed until he has taken his fill leaves a far less inflamed bite than one brushed away in the middle of his feast.

The daylight hours belonged to what is called the Green Bay fly, a species of dragon-fly. There are days when the outer walls of the houses carry so many that you cannot tell what color the wall beneath them is. Their lives are ephemeral things, scarcely more than a day long. Indoors you find the bodies stuck fast to the walls and the window-glass; outdoors they fall in such quantity that once a high wind has swept them into long rows against the sides of the quarters, a person can walk down them and toss them up underfoot like dry leaves in autumn.
