The evening party was much what an evening party is anywhere else. What held me was the one thing I had never had before: the conversation of several young ladies I was presented to, all of them born at Green Bay or close by it. Their mother was a Menomonee; their father a Frenchman whose people had settled the country generations back, and who is thought to come off the same branch of the Grignon family that the daughter of Madame de Sévigné married into. The family is said to keep a quantity of old papers and records that would leave you with that impression, though the spelling of the name has drifted a little since. However that may be, the Miss Grignons were dignified and well-bred to a striking degree, and there was something in their soft voices and their unstudied, original way of putting a thing that a stranger found very attractive.

They also opened a door I would rather have left shut. I said how impatient I was to see my new home, and they looked at me in plain astonishment. "Vous n'avez donc pas peur des serpens?"

"Snakes! was it possible there were snakes at Fort Winnebago?"

"At the Portage! oh! yes - one can never walk out for them - rattle-snakes - copper-heads - all sorts!"

I am not a timid woman by nature. Still, I will admit that from that evening the serpens sonnettes and the siffleurs were not a subject I could be perfectly indifferent about.

One of those young ladies was tall and moved beautifully, with a rich, blooming color and a dark eye that caught the light, and she would have been noticed in any drawing-room in the country. Another had a gentle sweetness and a cultivated taste that made everyone who knew her regret it when she went, later, into the seclusion of a convent.

Captain Harney and his boat came in on time, and then the real preparations began, with Mrs. Doty overseeing every comfort of the trip with the greatest kindness. The mess-basket was packed with every good thing there was — ham and tongue, biscuit and plum-cake — besides the plain provisions, crackers and bread and boiled pork, the pork being an article a lady was assumed to be far too fastidious to touch, though she were starving in the woods.

We had taken on three Canadian voyageurs to have charge of the tent, the mess-basket, and matters and things in general. They were to cut the wood for our fires, get our meals, and lend a hand to whatever needed doing. A messenger had gone ahead as well, up to the Kakalin, or rapids, twenty-one miles above us, to tell Wish-tay-yun — the most accomplished guide there is through the difficult passes of that river — to hold himself ready for us on a named day.
