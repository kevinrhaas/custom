Our evening party went off as parties do everywhere else. The most interesting feature of it to me, because it was the most novel, was the talk of some young ladies I was introduced to, natives of Green Bay or of its vicinity. Their mother was a Menomonee. Their father was a Frenchman, descended from a settler some generations back, and there is reason to believe that he was a branch of the same family of Grignon to which the daughter of Madame de Sévigné belonged. At least it is said the family holds many old papers and records that would give that impression, though the orthography of the name has changed a little since. However that may be, the Miss Grignons were strikingly dignified, well-bred young ladies, and there was a charm in their soft voices and their original, unsophisticated remarks that a stranger found very attractive.

They also opened to me a new field of apprehension. I said something about how impatient I was to see my new home, and they looked at me with wonder and exclaimed, "Vous n'avez donc pas peur des serpens?"

Snakes. Was it possible there were snakes at Fort Winnebago?

"At the Portage! oh! yes — one can never walk out for them — rattle-snakes — copper-heads — all sorts!"

I am not naturally timid. I have to admit, though, that the idea of the serpens sonnettes and the siffleurs was not quite a subject of indifference to me.

There was one among those young ladies whose tall, graceful figure, rich, blooming complexion, and dark, glancing eye would have distinguished her in any drawing-room, and another whose gentle sweetness and cultivated taste made it a matter of universal regret that she was afterward led to adopt the seclusion of a convent.

Captain Harney and his boat arrived in due time, and active preparations for the comfort of our trip began under the kind supervision of Mrs. Doty. The mess-basket was stowed with good things of every description — ham and tongue, biscuit and plum-cake — not counting the substantials of crackers, bread, and boiled pork, the last of which a lady was thought to be far too fastidious to touch, even if she were starving in the woods.

We had engaged three Canadian voyageurs to take charge of our tent, our mess-basket, and matters and things in general. Their business was to cut the wood for our fires, prepare our meals, and give a helping hand to whatever was going forward. A messenger had also been sent up to the Kakalin, or rapids, twenty-one miles above, to notify Wish-tay-yun, the most accomplished guide through the difficult passes of the river, to be ready for our service on a specified day.
