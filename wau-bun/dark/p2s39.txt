It would be an injustice to the hospitable settlers of Hickory Creek to pass over the entertainment with which they honored our Chicago beaux about this time. The merry-making was to be a ball, and all five of the single gentlemen of Chicago were invited. Mr. Dole was a new-comer and declined. Lieutenant Foster was on duty and could not go, but he did a thing better than going: he loaned his beautiful horse to Medard Beaubien, who, with Robert Kinzie and Gholson Kercheval, promised himself a great deal of fun in eclipsing the beaux of Hickory Creek and creating a sensation among its belles.

Chicago was then, as it is now, looked upon as the City par excellence. Its handful of inhabitants were credited with having seen something of the world, and it may safely be inferred that the arrival of three smart and dashing young men was anticipated with rather more satisfaction by the fair of that little settlement than by the swains whose rivals they were about to become.

The day came and the gentlemen set off in high spirits. They allowed themselves plenty of time, the dancing being appointed for two o'clock in the afternoon. They were well mounted, each of them privately certain that his own animal was the best of the three, and they wore their best suits, as befitted city gallants riding out to cut out their less fashionable neighbors and break the hearts of the admiring country damsels.

At the place appointed they were received with great politeness. Their steeds were taken and led away and looked after. A dinner was set before them, and when they had eaten it they were shown into the dancing-hall.

All the beauty of the neighboring precincts had assembled there. The ladies were for the most part white, or what passed for white, with here and there a dash of copper color. There was no lack of bombazet gowns, nor of large white pocket-handkerchiefs perfumed with oil of cinnamon, and as they took their places in long rows on the puncheon floor they made as merry and happy a company as anyone could have wished to see.

The city gentlemen grew more gallant by the hour. The girls grew more delighted with their attentions by the hour. And the country swains, hour by hour, grew more scowling and more jealous. They pigeon-winged. They double-shuffled. They came within an inch of dislocating hips and shoulders at "hoe corn and dig potatoes." It got them nothing. They had the mortification of watching the smart young sprigs from Chicago take their pick and choose among their very sweethearts, until they were fairly danced off the ground.

The revelry lasted until daylight, and then it was time to think about going home. Nobody came forward with obliging politeness to bring the horses round from the stable. Nobody came forward at all.

"Poor fellows!" said one of the party, with a compassionate sort of laugh. "They couldn't stand it. They have gone home to bed!"

"Serves them right," said another. "They'd better not ask us down among their girls again!"

So they groped their way out to the stable themselves and went in. There were animals standing at the manger. They were not, however, their horses. The three of them stood in the dark trying to make out what these things were. Had the rogues gone so far as to cheat them outright, and put these strange nondescripts in the place of their own?

They led them out into the gray of the morning — and such a trio as met their gaze.

The bodies were their own, right enough. It was the manes and the tails that were missing. Along each neck ran a scrubby, pickety ridge, and behind, where a tail should have been, there projected a bare stump; and that was every particle left of the flowing honors with which they had come gallivanting down to Hickory Creek to bear away the bell, or, in the emphatic language of the country, to take the rag off the bush.

Gholson sat down on a log and cried outright. Medard took the thing more philosophically, the horse being none of his — it was Lieutenant Foster's.

Robert, characteristically, looked around to see whom he might knock down. There was nobody in sight to wreak vengeance on.

The bumpkins had stolen quietly away, and were by now in some safe, quiet nook, enjoying their triumph at leisure; and it is not to be doubted that the deceitful fair ones were at their sides by that time, sharing the mirth and the exultation.

The unlucky gallants mounted their steeds and set their faces homeward. Never was there a more crestfallen or sorry-looking cavalcade. The poor horses appeared to understand perfectly that they had met with the same treatment as the messengers of King David at the hands of the evil-disposed Hanun. They hung their heads. They plainly wished they might have tarried at Jericho for a season. There was in those days no back way into Chicago by which a man could steal in unobserved. The approach had to be made straight across the prairie, in full view of the entire community — and, stirring events being rare with us, it was the settled custom of the whole settlement to turn out and welcome the arrival of any new-comer.

They beat as hasty a retreat as they could manage, through the shouts, the jeers, and the condolences of their acquaintances, and it stands on record that these three young gentlemen were in no hurry, at any later day, to accept an invitation to partake of the festivities of Hickory Creek.
