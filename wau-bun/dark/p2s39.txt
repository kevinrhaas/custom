The people at Hickory Creek were generous, and I would be treating them badly if I left out the party they threw for our Chicago beaux around this time. A ball, no less, and every one of Chicago's five bachelors was asked to it. Mr. Dole had only lately come into the country and sent his regrets. Lieutenant Foster had the duty and could not go, but what he did instead was worth rather more than his company: he handed his beautiful horse over to Medard Beaubien. Medard, with Robert Kinzie and Gholson Kercheval alongside him, was already counting on a splendid time of it — putting the local beaux entirely in the shade, and turning the head of every belle at Hickory Creek.

Then as now, people spoke of Chicago as the City par excellence. Its handful of residents carried a reputation for having seen something of the world, and we may safely assume that three smart, dashing young men riding down was a prospect the girls of that small place welcomed rather more warmly than did the young men whose place they stood to take.

The day arrived and off they went in the highest spirits, early enough to be certain of it, since the dancing was to open at two in the afternoon. Each of the three sat a horse he was proud of. Each had put on his best suit. That was only proper in city gallants who meant to leave their less fashionable neighbors nowhere at all, and to break as many admiring country hearts as came within reach.

They arrived to find themselves treated with the greatest politeness. Somebody took charge of the steeds, a dinner appeared, and when they had eaten they were shown through into the dancing-hall.

Beauty from every precinct round about had turned out for it. Most of the ladies were white, or near enough to pass for it, though a dash of copper color showed here and there among them. Bombazet gowns, and big white pocket-handkerchiefs that smelled of cinnamon oil — there was no shortage of either — and once they had lined themselves up in long rows down the puncheon floor, you could not have found a happier or a merrier company anywhere in the world.

Then the evening began to lean. Hour by hour the city gentlemen grew more gallant; hour by hour the girls grew more pleased with the attention; and hour by hour the local swains went darker in the face. They pigeon-winged. They double-shuffled. They came near throwing hips and shoulders out of the socket at "hoe corn and dig potatoes," and not one bit of it counted. They had to stand there and watch three sprigs from Chicago take their "pick and choose" among the girls they had thought of as their own, until they had been danced clean off their own floor.

They had danced until daylight, and now it was time to be starting back. Nobody appeared, with any obliging politeness, to bring the horses out of the stable for them.

"Poor fellows!" one of them said, with a pitying sort of laugh. "They couldn't stand it. They have gone home to bed!"

"Serves them right," another put in. "They'd better not ask us down among their girls again!"

They groped their way over to the stable and stepped inside. Animals stood at the manger, right enough, but not the animals they had left there. So what were these? A trick of the rogues, then — strange nondescripts pushed in where their own horses belonged?

They walked them out into the gray of the morning, and then they saw what they had.

The bodies were their own bodies, no question of that. But the manes were gone. So were the tails. A scrubby, pickety ridge along the neck and a bare stump sticking out behind — that was every last remnant of the flowing splendor in which they had ridden down to "bear away the bell" at Hickory Creek, or, to say it the way the country says it, "to take the rag off the bush."

Gholson dropped onto a log and wept, openly, without shame. Medard bore up better. It was not his horse that had been spoiled. It was Lieutenant Foster's.

Robert did what Robert always did, and looked around for a man to knock down. There was nobody in sight at all to take it out on.

The bumpkins were long gone. They had tucked themselves away in some safe, quiet corner to enjoy the thing at leisure, and by that hour the deceitful fair ones were surely sitting beside them, laughing along in the mirth and the exultation.

The unlucky gallants got up on what was left of their horses and pointed themselves toward home. No sorrier or more crestfallen cavalcade ever crossed that country. The animals themselves seemed to understand that they had been used exactly as King David's messengers were used by the evil-disposed Hanun; they carried their heads low, and would plainly have preferred to have "tarried at Jericho" a while longer. And there was no quiet road home in those days, no back lane by which a man might slip in unseen. The way ran out over open prairie with the whole community free to watch it. Worse: stirring events being scarce out there, the entire settlement kept the habit of turning out in a body to greet any new-comer who appeared.

They retreated as fast as retreat could decently be managed, under a hail of shouting and jeering and mock condolence from everyone they knew; and the record shows that not one of those three young gentlemen was ever again in any hurry to accept an invitation to a festivity at Hickory Creek.
