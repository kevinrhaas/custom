On the morning of a bright autumn day, word came to the authorities that the chiefs of the nation would present themselves at the Agency to deliver the suspected people as prisoners to the Americans.

At ten o'clock, looking out along the Portage road, we could make out a moving concourse of people, brilliant with color and with glittering arms, and, as it came nearer, certain white objects among them of a kind we had never seen in such a procession before.

General Dodge, Major Plympton, and one or two other officers took their seats with Mr. Kinzie on the platform in front of the door of our mansion to receive them, while we stationed ourselves at the window, where we could both see and hear.

The procession wound up the hill and came on toward us, marching slowly. It was a grand and a solemn sight. First came some of the principal chiefs in their most brilliant array. Behind them the prisoners, every one of them dressed in white cotton, in token of their innocence, with girdles around their waists. The drum and the shee-shee-qua kept time under the death-song they were chaunting as they walked. They wore no paint and no ornaments. Their faces were grave and thoughtful. It might well be a serious moment to them, for they knew very little of the customs of the whites, and what little they did know was not of a kind to inspire cheerfulness. Nothing but their Father's assurance that they should receive strict justice would likely have brought them to fulfil the engagement of the nation in this manner.

The rest of the procession was made up of a long train of Winnebagoes, all decked out in their holiday garb.

The chiefs came up and shook hands with the gentlemen, who stood ready to receive their greeting. Then the prisoners came forward and went through the same salutation with the officers. When they offered their hands to their Father, he declined them.

"No," he said. "You have come here accused of great crimes — of having assisted in taking the lives of some of the defenceless settlers. When you have been tried by the laws of the land, and been proved innocent, then your Father will give you his hand."

They looked more serious still at this, as though it told them that their Father, too, believed them guilty. They stepped back a little and seated themselves on the ground in a row, without speaking, facing their Father and the officers. The other Indians all took seats in a circle around them — all except the one-eyed chief, Kau-ray-kau-say-kah, the White Crow, who had been deputed to deliver the prisoners to the Agent.

He made a speech. His countrymen asserted their innocence of the charges brought against them, he said, but they were quite willing to be tried by the laws of white men. He hoped they would not be detained long, but that the matter would be investigated soon, and that they would come out of it clear and white.

He was assured in reply that all things would be conducted fairly and impartially, exactly as if the accused were white men, and to that was added the hope that they would be found to have been good and true citizens, and peaceful children of their Great Father, the President.

When this was over, White Crow asked permission to transfer the medal he had received from the President as a mark of friendship to his son, who stood beside him, and whom the nation had chosen to fill his place as chief, an office he wished to resign. The speeches made on this occasion, as Paquette interpreted them, the modest bearing of the young man, and the dignified yet feeling manner of the father all through it, made the whole ceremony deeply impressive, and when the older man lifted the medal from his own neck and hung it around his son's, saying a few fitting words to him, I do not think anyone there could have watched it unmoved.

I had been watching the faces of the prisoners as they sat on the ground in front of me, through all of these ceremonies. With one exception they were open and calm and expressive of conscious innocence. About that one I could not help admitting there might be reasonable doubt. One of them was remarkably fine-looking. Another was a boy of certainly not more than seventeen, and all through the transfer of the medal he looked from one speaker to the other, listening to what was said with an air and an expression of almost childlike interest and satisfaction.

Our hearts felt sad for them when, the ceremonies over, they were led away by a file of soldiers and committed to the dungeon of the guard-house, to stay there until such time as they should be summoned before the court appointed to try their cause.
