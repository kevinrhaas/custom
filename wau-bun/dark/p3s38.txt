By then everyone was calling the war over. Word had reached us of the Bad Axe, where the regulars, the militia and the steamboat Warrior together had made a finish of the handful of Sauks still left, and with the news the hearts and the houses of the frontier settlers came back to rest.

It may seem strange that an enemy so few in number, with so little to fight with, could throw a whole country into a panic and take such an enormous force to put down. The whole of the trouble was that nobody ever knew where to find them, either to strike at them or to guard against them. At the outset, I suspect, every military man felt exactly as that fine old veteran General Brady felt. "Give me two infantry companies mounted," he said, "and I will engage to whip the Sauks out of the country in one week!"

True enough. But before you can whip an enemy you have to meet him, and to follow Indians to any purpose and take them requires a particular kind of schooling — one that in those days hardly a man could claim, the frontier militia included.

Through parts of that campaign there was a second trouble: the two branches of the service would not act as one. The regulars looked down on the militia's unprofessional way of going at things; the militia jeered at the slow, empty formalities of the regulars. Each side held it as an article of faith that the work would go better with the other out of it, and the militia, being quick to move, now and then took the business into their own hands and produced defeat and disgrace, as they did in the affair called "Stillman's Run."

The contempt the army officers felt for the militia ran downhill to their servants and dependants. The Ranger officers had paid their visit to Fort Winnebago before the battle of the Wisconsin, and afterward the officer of the mess that had entertained them called up his servant one day to go through the sutler's accounts. It was the same small "Yellow David" who had formerly been Captain Harney's.

"David," said the young gentleman, "I see three bottles of cologne-water charged in the month's account of the mess at the sutler's. What does that mean?"

"If you please, lieutenant," said David, respectfully, "it was to sweeten up the dining-room and quarters after them milish' officers were here visiting."
