The rapids took everything the men had. Wish-tay-yun's voice was clear and brassy, the bugle the whole crew worked to; he called, and the rest answered him in one throat and threw themselves at the oars again. Then the hull would wedge itself between two great stones and stop dead. "Whoop la! whoop! whoop!"

Another haul. Another. Every nerve in them drawn tight, and nothing moved.

"She won't budge!"

"Men, overboard!" The benches emptied before the words were finished, every rower in the river.

They shoved and hauled and worried her loose, and then walked alongside her in the shallows, steadying her, steering her, until the water ahead flattened into something like calm. Only then did they climb back aboard and take up the oars.

There were almost no songs that day. Pipes instead, one after another, the only comfort on offer after work like that.

I had never watched anything of the kind. Whatever I had seen of the world until then, it had not included men driving their bodies to the far edge of what a body will do, and so the sight worked on me harder than it would have worked on someone used to it. The labor alone seemed punishment enough. But to wade waist-deep in cold water, and then to sit down again in soaked clothes with no fire anywhere near them — poor men, it was more than anyone ought to have to bear. Then I saw my husband. After our noon meal he had startled me by appearing in a pair of duck trowsers and a light jacket, and I had not understood why. At the first shout of "Fast, again!" he was over the side with the crew, and he "bore a hand" through the whole rest of that long stretch.

He came back aboard only to take the oar of a boy — one of the company of soldiers, slight and fine-boned, who had bled from the nose since the first day at any effort beyond the ordinary. I asked about him, and was not surprised by the answer: a recruit, only just entered the service. He went by the name of Gridley, though I have no doubt that was a name he had chosen rather than been given. Everything about him said he had been raised gently and had enlisted without the least idea what a soldier's life would ask of him. You could read it in him afterward, too, in the discontent he never once put down, until at last he deserted his post. That was some months after the days I am describing here. They took him once and held him a while in confinement, and the moment they released him he went again. His remains were found the following spring, not many miles from the fort. Cold, or starvation; no one could say which. A grim thing to set down — I will go back to the boats.
