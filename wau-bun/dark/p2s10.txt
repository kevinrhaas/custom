Something lifted the general despondency a little on the 14th of August, when Captain Wells came in with fifteen friendly Miamis.

Of this brave man, who stands so plainly in our frontier annals, it is enough to say here that he had lived among the Indians since he was a boy, and so knew their character and their habits perfectly.

At Fort Wayne he had heard of the order to evacuate Chicago. He knew what the Pottowattamies had decided to do. He made a rapid march straight across the country to keep his relative, Captain Heald, and Captain Heald's troops, from walking into certain destruction.

He came too late. All too late. When he reached the post he found the ammunition already destroyed and the provisions already given away to the Indians. There was no alternative left to anyone now. Every preparation was made for the troops to march out the following morning.

That same afternoon a second council was held with the Indians. They expressed great indignation at the destruction of the ammunition and the liquor.

For all the care that had been taken to keep it quiet, the sound of barrel-heads being knocked in had carried, and betrayed the whole night's work. And there had been so much liquor poured into the river that the water tasted of it in the morning. Strong grog, as somebody put it.

Murmurs went through them, and threats after them. It was plain enough that the first moment the troops stood out in the open, they would be shown exactly how much that disappointment was resented.
