The woods had turned. Every tint of autumn was in them at once, and the ground under them was full of movement — groups of Indians in every direction, lodges pitched here and there around the Agency buildings, smoke going up thin and straight in the cold. On the low ground below stood the white tents of the traders. They had come early and set up already, stocked to furnish the winter supplies, and waiting on the annuity money that was about to pass into Indian hands and straight back out of them again.

A great concourse had been gathering for days. They were waiting on the payment, and the payment was waiting on Shaw-nee-aw-kee, who was bringing the silver.

Before any of it could begin, the great chief of the nation had held a carouse. Four-Legs, whose village we had passed at the mouth of Winnebago Lake. It is too apt to be the custom when the tribes come into the neighborhood of a sutler's establishment, and in this case the means were more than usually ready to hand, because there were traders on the ground who held the very stringent laws against selling liquor to the Indians as no particular obstacle.

Four-Legs could not stand this full tide of prosperity. His Father the agent was not there to check him, and there was nothing else in the way, and he carried his indulgence so far that within a few days it killed him. They had buried him with unusual pomp the day before we arrived, and my chief feeling, when I learned it, was disappointment at having come too late to witness the ceremonies.

They had wrapped the body in a blanket, as their custom is, and laid it in a rude coffin along with his guns, his tomahawk, his pipes and a quantity of tobacco. Then they carried it up to the highest point of the hill facing the fort, an immense procession of his people coming behind it, whooping, beating their drums, howling — making altogether what is emphatically called a pow-wow.

When the body was in the ground they planted a stake at its head, painted over in vermilion with a series of hieroglyphics setting out the great deeds and events of his life. Around the whole of it they drove pickets cut from the trunks of tamarack-trees. To that enclosure his friends would come for many days together, to renew the expression of their grief and to throw tobacco and other offerings over the grave to the Great Spirit.

There was one consolation in having come late. We were still in time to furnish a quantity of white cotton for a flag to wave over the grave, and to settle a considerable bill at the sutler's for the various articles the funeral parade had been found to require — it being a duty expected of their Father to bury the dead suitably.

The observances were not finished with him. All that day, and then all that night, the sound of their instruments came across to us mixed with lamentation, and with the discordant whoops and yells of men who were still partly drunk, and there was no sleeping through it. Every so often something else threaded through the noise: the plaintive notes of the Indian flute, which a young man plays when he is in love. Grief and whiskey had made their hearts tender, and the woods gave back their melancholy strains until morning.
