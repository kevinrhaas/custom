There were no boats ready to ascend the river. Our old friend Hamilton promised to have one in preparation at once, and time passed, and no boat was made ready.

It was the beginning of November now. We were passing our days pleasantly enough with the Irwins and the Whitneys, and at the residence of Colonel Stambaugh, the Indian Agent, but the delay was inconvenient and it was becoming vexatious.

I suggested making the trip on horseback. "No, indeed," was the answer I got, every time I raised it. "No mortal woman has ever gone that road, unless it was some native on foot, nor ever could."

"But suppose we set out in the boat and get frozen in on the way. We can neither pass the winter there, nor possibly find our way to a human home. We have had one similar experience already. Is it not better to take it for granted that I can do what you and others of your sex have done?"

Dr. Finley, the post-surgeon at Fort Howard, heard the matter being debated and offered me his favorite horse Charlie on the spot. He was very sure-footed, the doctor said, and capital in a marsh or a troublesome stream.

By land, then, it was decided we should go; and as soon as our old Menomonee friend Wish-tay-yun, who was as good a guide over ground as over water, could be summoned, we set off — leaving our trunks behind to be forwarded by Hamilton whenever it should please him to carry out his intention of sending up his boat.

On the morning of our departure we waited until a late hour for our fellow-travelers, Mr. Wing, of Monroe, and Dr. Philleo, of Galena. They did not come. We resolved to lose no more time, being confident that we should all meet at the Kakalin in the course of the evening.
