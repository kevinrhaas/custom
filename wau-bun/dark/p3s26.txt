There was not a boat ready to take us up the river. Our old friend Hamilton gave his word that one would be got into preparation immediately. The days went on. No boat appeared.

November had begun by then. We were spending the time pleasantly enough — with the Irwins, with the Whitneys, and at the house of Colonel Stambaugh, the Indian Agent — but the delay was an inconvenience, and it had begun to vex me.

So I proposed that we make the journey on horseback. The answer never varied. "No, indeed," they told me. "No mortal woman has ever gone that road, unless it was some native on foot, nor ever could."

I put the other case to them. "But suppose we set out in the boat and get frozen in on the way. We can neither pass the winter there, nor possibly find our way to a human home. We have had one similar experience already. Is it not better to take it for granted that I can do what you and others of your sex have done?"

The argument was overheard by Dr. Finley, post-surgeon at Fort Howard, who at once offered me the loan of Charlie, the horse he liked best. "He is very sure-footed," the doctor alleged, "and capital in a marsh or troublesome stream."

That settled it: we would go by land. We sent for our old Menomonee friend "Wish-tay-yun," who could guide as well over ground as over water, and the moment he came we started, leaving the trunks behind for Hamilton to forward whenever it might suit him to make good his intention about the boat.

On the morning we left we held on till late for our fellow-travelers, Mr. Wing, of Monroe, and Dr. Philleo, of Galena. They did not come. Rather than waste more of the day we went without them, sure enough that the whole party would be together again at the Kakalin before the evening was out.
