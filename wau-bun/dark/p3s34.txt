Crossing the parade, we would sometimes catch a sound that turned our heads — a tapping, over and over, on the bars of the dungeon where a criminal was being kept. The man doing it had murdered Lieutenant Foster.

That amiable young officer may be remembered here: he had been our traveling companion the year before, on the journey up from Chicago. Some months after he reached Fort Howard he had occasion to send one of his own company, a soldier named Doyle, into confinement for intoxication. A few days later Doyle worked on the sergeant of the guard until the man agreed to walk him across to the quarters of Lieutenant Foster, the plea being that he wished to speak with him. Up he went, by the stairs, toward the young officer's room. The sergeant and another soldier waited at the bottom of them, near the door.

Doyle stepped inside and addressed Lieutenant Foster. "Will you please tell me, lieutenant, what I am confined for?"

"No, sir," replied the officer, "you know your offense well enough, return to your place of confinement."

The man went down the stairs at a run, wrenched the gun out of the sergeant's hand, carried it back up, and fired it into Lieutenant Foster's heart.

The lieutenant turned toward his inner apartment. "Ah me!" he said, and fell dead before he reached the entrance.

A civil court had tried Doyle, and he lay now under sentence, waiting for the day of his execution. He was a hardened villain. Not once, in all that time, did he show the slightest compunction for his crime.

Major Clark, the commanding officer, sent one day to ask what he might want for his comfort.

"If the Major pleased," he answered, "he should like to have a light and a copy of Byron's Works."

There was some fear that he would contrive to make away with himself before the appointed day, so everything that might have served him for a weapon was taken from him. His food was brought to him in a wooden bowl. A piece of broken crockery, they reasoned, would be quite enough for a man bent on destroying himself.

Then, one morning, a little package came out from him to the commanding officer, offered as a present. In it was a rope — a strong one, twisted out of strips of his blanket that he had separated one by one with great care — and a large stout spike made fast at the end of it. The message that went with the gift was this: "He wished Major Clark to see that if he chose to put an end to himself, he could find means to do it in spite of him."

He held that hardened frame of mind to the very end. As he was led out for execution and taken beyond the gate, he noticed a quantity of lumber lying there, lately collected for putting up a new Company's warehouse.

"Ah, captain, what are you going to build here?" he asked of Captain Scott, the officer attending him.

"Doyle," his captain told him, "you have but a few moments to live - - you had better employ your thoughts about something else."

"It is for that very reason, captain," he said, "that I am inquiring - as my time is short, I wish to gain all the information I can while it lasts."
