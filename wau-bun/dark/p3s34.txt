When we walked across the parade, our attention was sometimes caught by a tapping on the bars of the dungeon where a criminal was confined. It was the murderer of Lieutenant Foster.

It may be remembered that this amiable young officer had been our traveling companion on the trip from Chicago the year before. Some months after he reached Fort Howard, he had occasion to order a soldier of his company, a man named Doyle, into confinement for intoxication. A few days later Doyle persuaded the sergeant of the guard to walk him over to Lieutenant Foster's quarters, on the plea that he wished to speak with him. He went up the stairs to the young officer's room. The sergeant and another soldier stayed below at the foot of them, near the door.

Doyle came in and said, "Will you please tell me, lieutenant, what I am confined for?"

"No, sir," the officer replied. "You know your offense well enough. Return to your place of confinement."

The man ran back down the stairs, wrenched the gun out of the sergeant's hands, came up again, and discharged it into Lieutenant Foster's heart.

Foster turned toward his inner apartment. He said, "Ah me!" and fell dead in the doorway.

Doyle had been tried by a civil court and was now under sentence, waiting to be executed. He was a hardened villain. He never showed the slightest compunction for what he had done.

The commanding officer, Major Clark, sent to him one day to ask whether he wanted anything for his comfort.

"If the Major pleased," he answered, "he should like to have a light and a copy of Byron's Works."

There were some fears that he would find a way to make away with himself before the day of execution, and to guard against it he was stripped of everything that might furnish him a weapon. His food was served to him in a wooden bowl, in case a shard of broken crockery should give him the means of self-destruction.

One morning he sent a little package to the commanding officer as a present. Inside was a strong rope, made out of strips of his blanket that he had patiently separated, with a large stout spike fastened at the end of it. The message that came with it was that he wished Major Clark to see that if he chose to put an end to himself, he could find the means to do it in spite of him.

That hardened frame of mind held to the last. As he was led out for execution, passing beyond the gate, he noticed a quantity of lumber lately collected there for the construction of a new Company's warehouse.

"Ah, captain, what are you going to build here?" he asked of Captain Scott, who was attending him.

"Doyle," his captain replied, "you have but a few moments to live. You had better employ your thoughts about something else."

"It is for that very reason, captain," he said, "that I am inquiring. As my time is short, I wish to gain all the information I can while it lasts."
