The danger had come close enough by then that my husband decided to send his family to Fort Howard, which everyone believed lay well outside the enemy's range. I pleaded to be allowed to stay. It made no difference at all. He was firm.

"I must not leave my post," he said, "while there is any danger. My departure would maybe be the signal for an immediate alliance of the Winnebagoes with the Sauks. I am certain that as long as I am here my presence will act as a restraint upon them. You wish to stay and share my dangers! Your doing so would expose us both to certain destruction in case of attack. By the aid of my friends in both tribes, I could hope to preserve my own life if I were alone, but surrounded by my family, that would be impossible — we should all fall victims together. My duty plainly is, to send you to a place of safety."

The means of doing it turned up soon enough. Paquette, the Interpreter, was also an agent of the American Fur Company, and he had a boat-load of furs to send down to Green Bay, on their way to Mackinac. Mr. Kinzie had the boat made as comfortable as an open boat of that kind can be made — a tent-cloth stretched over a framework of hoop-poles across the center and lined with a dark-green blanket — put an abundant store of provisions and other comforts aboard, and gave us into the joint care of my brother Arthur and our faithful blacksmith, Mâtâ.

Mâtâ was a tall, gaunt Frenchman with a freckled face and a great crop of crisp, sandy hair, and an incurable determination to speak English. His command of the language was not large, and he made it stranger still by putting an s on the end of nearly every word and delivering each phrase with a jerk, like a man throwing something.

"Davids," he would say to the little yellow fiddler, after an evening's frolic at the Interpreter's, "Davids, clear away the tables and the glasses, and play fishes-hornspikes." He was a kind, affectionate creature, and his devotion to Monsieur Johns and Madame Johns had no limit that anyone ever found.

Besides those two, three trusty Indians were engaged to escort us, the chief of them a man called Old Smoker. The crew of the boat were all French engagés in the service of the Fur Company — six of them, gay-hearted and merry, easing the work with their pipes and their songs, and they would have taken it as a high compliment if the ladies aboard had joined in the singing. Our hearts were too heavy that day to give them the pleasure.

We left home on the Fourth of July, and it was a gloomy day for those of us who went and for the one who stayed. Nobody said the thing everybody was thinking, which was whether we would ever see one another again. Several of that circle had been through Indian warfare before and knew exactly what the worst looked like. And there was not even the comfort of knowing that going was safe. The Sauks might be on the other side of us. The road we were taking might — not likely, but it might — carry us straight into the middle of them. So it was a solemn leave-taking, and every one of us felt it might be a parting for this world.

Not every one. The Frenchmen laughed and sang and cracked their jokes and assured Monsieur John that they would take Madame John and Madame Alum safe to the bay, spite of Sauks or wind or weather.

So we set off. The Fort stayed in sight a long while, the river swinging us in toward its walls and out away from them and in again, and it was nearly mid-day before we lost the last of it and our home with it.

At the noon-tide meal — the voyageurs measure their day in pipes — somebody made an alarming discovery. No bread had been put aboard for the crew. How it had happened, nobody could say. One man was positive a large quantity had been brought over from the garrison bakery that very morning for their use; another had seen the sacks of loaves standing in Paquette's kitchen with his own eyes. Wherever the bread was, it was not with us, and we were many miles down the river with nothing to feed six Frenchmen but some salted pork, a few beans, and some onions. We held a consultation. Should the boat turn back to the Portage for supplies? But the same danger that had put us on the water in the first place was still there, and we had been ordered to make all possible speed. On examination our own store of bread and crackers turned out to be far more than we could ever want, and we judged it would stretch to rations for the Frenchmen as far as Powell's, at the Butte des Morts, which we would reach the day after the next. We would certainly get a fresh supply there.

That settled, we went on — and from then on we went in total silence. A song or a loud laugh was flatly forbidden until we should be past the outermost limit of the country where the enemy might be. We had been told before we started about a certain point on the river, where the low marshy meadows it had been winding through rise into firmer, higher ground. That is the border of the Menomonee territory. It is also the place where the Sauks, if they had crossed north of the Wisconsin and were making for the Chippewa country, would most likely be met.
