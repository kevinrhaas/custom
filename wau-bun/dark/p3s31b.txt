The run down the rapids was a lively business and not entirely a safe one, and at the end of it came the Grand Chûte, a fall of several feet, which the boat went over and came out of right side up. Toward evening we reached the settlement of the Waubanakees at the head of the Little Chûte. These are the Stockbridge, or Brothertown, Indians — what is left of the old Mohicans — who had come out a few years before from Oneida County, in the State of New York, to a tract the United States granted them on the fertile banks of the Fox River. They had already cut wide openings into the forest and put up substantial, comfortable houses near the water, where the banks stand high and carry, for most of their length, enormous trees.

We decided to ask these people for hospitality, though only a corner of somebody's fire, enough to boil our tea-kettle and bake the short-cake that had stood in for bread for nearly two days now. The making of that cake had been the great joke of the trip. Powell's black flour, some salt, and a little butter went into the tin box that had held our meat; the box was then turned over, and scoured, and became the dough-board; the vinegar-bottle was the rolling-pin; a shallow tin dish did for the baking. The Waubanakees were good enough to lend us an iron bake-kettle as well, and to stand over the cake and see it properly cooked, once Harry had carried it up to their house.

They were so kind about all of it that the crew took the notion of asking for a lodging on shore — three nights packed into that boat had been enough for them. Arthur and Mâtâ came round to the same idea shortly after, and we were pressed to do likewise, with the assurance that the houses were extremely neat and orderly.

We chose to stay aboard. It was a fine night and everything in the boat had been arranged so comfortably, and we kept Edwin and Josette with us.

The boat was moored tight, because the Little Chûte lay just below us, and a craft that broke loose in that current and went down over the falls would be a very serious matter indeed. As a further precaution one man was left aboard to keep everything safe and in order. Then the rest of them climbed the bank and went off to their beds in the Waubanakee cabins.

It was a beautiful, calm, moonlit night, the air just warm enough to be pleasant, and the low steady murmur of the rapids and the fall not far off put our party to sleep in no time. How long we slept I could not say. What woke us was a rush of wind. It caught the poles holding up the awning and bent them, and then snapped them, and a second gust took the tent and the blanket clean off the boat and away downstream. The moonlight was gone. Then a flash of lightning showed them to us, once, far off down the water, sailing away like a spectre in the distance.
