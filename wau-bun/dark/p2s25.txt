Mr. Kinzie joined his family at Detroit in January, as has been told. Not long after, a suspicion took hold in General Proctor's mind: that he was in correspondence with General Harrison, who was at Fort Meigs and was believed to be planning an advance on Detroit. What happened next was that Lieutenant Watson of the British army called on him one day with an invitation to the General's quarters across the river — a matter of business, he wished to speak with him. Mr. Kinzie suspected nothing and went. As soon as he arrived he was ordered into confinement and put under strict guard in the house of his own former partner, Mr. Patterson of Sandwich. When he did not come home, Mrs. Kinzie sent word to some of the Indian chiefs who were his particular friends. They went straight to the commanding officer's head-quarters, demanded that "their friend" be released, and brought him back to his own house. The General waited. When a favorable opportunity came he sent a detachment of dragoons to arrest him, and this time they got him away and out onto the river. And at that moment a party of friendly Indians appeared.

"Where is the Shaw-nee-aw-kee?" was the first thing they asked.

"There," his wife said, pointing across the water. "In the hands of the red-coats, who are taking him away again."

They ran to the river, seized what canoes were lying there, crossed to Sandwich, and compelled General Proctor a second time to give up what he intended.

The third time, he managed it. Mr. Kinzie was arrested and carried in heavy irons to Fort Malden, in Canada, at the mouth of the Detroit River. At first they used him harshly. Later the rigor of his confinement slackened somewhat, and he was allowed to walk along the bank of the river for air and exercise.
