The great bug-bear of this road was the next thing in our way, and we came to it at about nine o'clock. Ma-zhee-gaw-gaw Swamp lay across our way, half a mile from edge to edge and running off to either side further than the eye could follow. Water stood over much of it. Out of the water rose black knobs, scattered anyhow, and those knobs were all the footing our animals were going to get. Where there was no water there was mud instead, black and oozy, the sort of surface that looks as though it will open under a hoof and let the leg go down as far as it likes.

That was what we had to cross. All three of the gentlemen pushed out ahead of me, each one announcing that he meant to find the safest and firmest line for me to follow. One would call back, "Here, madam, come this way!" Another would call, "This is the best path, wifie, follow me." Charlie, more often than not, was of a different opinion, and chose his own route, and his was the best route in the swamp.

He took it slowly. He would stand on one little rise of ground and study the next, and step across, and stand again, and now and then he would refuse a stretch of mud altogether and go around it, and by this method the rest of the party gradually drew away from us. I had been watching nothing but my horse's feet. When at last I lifted my eyes, this is what I saw: my husband down off his horse, hauling at the bridle to get the animal out of the place it had walked itself into; Mr. Wing's great white beast lying over on its side in the mud, floundering, with Mr. Wing working to free himself from the stirrups; and Dr. Philleo standing a little apart from his own steed, which had pitched into a deep bog and was heaving to get up out of it. It was a formidable sight. All three of them found their voices at once. "Oh, don't come this way!"

"Indeed," cried I, "I have no thought of it. Charlie and I know better." I left it entirely to the sagacious creature, and he went on picking and choosing until he had carried me safely past the whole dismounted company. I looked back at them climbing toward their saddles again, and I am afraid I could not keep myself from a small triumphant flourish of the whip.

Three hours we spent in that place, and still we were not out of the Slough of Despond. At last the far edge came in sight — and with it a stream, deep, and five or six feet across. The gentlemen reached it first, got down, and stood there debating.

"Jump off, jump off, madam," cried Mr. Wing, and "Jump off, jump off," echoed Dr. Philleo, "we are just consulting how we are to get you across."

"What do you think about it?" asked my husband.

"Charlie will show you," replied I. "Come, Charlie." I lifted the bridle smartly, gave his neck a pat and made the little chirping sound he knew, and he went over that water as lightly as a deer and set me down on solid ground.

Of the whole company Mr. Wing had come off worst. The clumsy animal he was riding held the view that whenever he found himself in difficulty his part was to lie down and submit to whatever came next, and his rider, who was neither light nor especially quick, was generally beneath him and half bedded in the mire before he had settled on what to do about it.

So it was good sense in him, when he came to the little stream, to walk straight into it and wade across, and rinse off what he could of the morning from his horse and from himself. The other gentlemen had no better idea, and did the same.
