Six miles above the Butte another water comes down to meet the Fox. It is called Wolf River, and it is wide, and it runs straighter and looks more promising than the crooked thing it empties into, which is exactly the trouble with it. Strangers have taken it for the main stream. They have gone up it a long way before learning, much to their chagrin, that every one of those miles would have to be undone.

Past that junction the river begins to play games with the compass. I was always watching the banks for something worth a sketch, and at one bend I found it: a strip of ground close on the water, broad trees leaning out over it, two or three wigwams tucked in under the shade of them. Bright-looking small children were running about with nothing on at all. Two women sat on the ground, at work on a mat for their lodge. It was a scene worth having, and I began. The bank saw us then and knew Shaw-nee-aw-kee, and the shout went up as it always did — "Ee-awn-chee-wee-rah, Hee-nee-kar-ray-kay-noo."[12] It was one of the occasions on which they let their feeling show. Then we moved on, and I kept working at the drawing.

The trouble was the sun. It had been squarely behind me when I started. After a while it was off my left shoulder. Then it was straight in front of me. I shifted where I sat, and shifted again, and by and by it had settled on my right hand and stayed there. I could not account for it, and I looked up from the paper to see why. "Oh, here is a pretty scene. I must have this too! But how surprisingly like the one I have just finished, only in a different direction." The laughing came off the bank a second time. It was the same bank. We had passed it not an hour earlier, and we had rowed the better part of four miles since, and all we had to show for that labor was the width of the little shelf of ground the trees and the lodges stood on. I was a traveler in a hurry. The river was not.

We came out onto Lake Puckaway late in the evening of our second day from Butte des Morts. A white man named Gleason lived there — the one poor Hooe used to aim his little pun at, since nothing Gleason ever told you stayed the size he had told it: "All isn't gold that Gleasons." We did not go to his house. Late in the season as it was, the mosquitoes along that shore were beyond bearing, and we were glad to put ourselves as far off the bank as the water allowed and make our beds in the boat.

The lake is named for the flags that grow in it, long rushes that grow in its water in great abundance, and out of them the women make the coarse matting that covers a lodge. The way of it is old and wants almost nothing. A woman sits on the ground with the rushes laid side by side and made fast at either end. Her shuttle is a long flat needle cut from bone, with a cord of tree bark run through it, and she drives that needle through one rush and then the next until every stalk is bound hard against its neighbor and the whole is one fine solid mat. They are seldom made longer than five or six feet. Anything bigger is unhandy when a lodge is being set up or taken down.

The work falls to the older women of a family. When a woman has been worn through — when the weather and the hardship have gone on long enough that she can no longer bring down a tree, or hoe the corn, or carry heavy loads — she is set to weaving mats, and to minding the children, and to keeping order among the dogs, of which there is never any shortage in an Indian lodge.

Our course took us next into Lac de Boeuf, Buffalo Lake, and it is a lovely sheet of water. In places the shore is very fine, with headlands running out into the clear depths and the great stands of timber crowning them lying upside down in the water as if in a mirror. Now and then a deer would cross one of the glades that opened at intervals in the woodland and be gone. A pair of sand-hill cranes got up ahead of us, beating their wings slowly, looking for somewhere quieter to be. Teal came in flocks, skimming low over the surface and then lifting toward the shelter of the forest, and our sportsman felt every one of them; but a bird brought down in that country would most likely never be found again, and he would not give way to the wanton pleasure of killing for its own sake.

Then the lake let us out and the river had us back, narrow again, doubling on itself again, and with it went the last of anything worth looking at between there and the end of our trip.
