We breakfasted cheerfully the next morning, with the Rev. Mr. Kent, of Galena, at the table with us, and then made ready to go on. My husband had wanted to turn back; I had talked him out of it by telling him, truthfully, that I felt as fresh and as bright as I had the day we left home.

The one thing anybody seemed uneasy about was whether we would manage to strike the trail to Hamilton's diggings, which was where we were bound next.

The directions were not what I would call clear. Follow the trail we were on for so many miles; come to a crossing; turn into it and hold easterly; after a while that would put us on a deep trail running straight to Hamilton's. Out on that open country there is nothing to steer by. Every rise of ground is the twin of the last one, and a man who loses his trail out there has about as much chance of picking it up again as of finding a footpath in the middle of the ocean. And a trail is not a road. It is a narrow line cut into the sod by the hoofs of horses that the Indians ride single file, and it sinks so deep into the turf that there are times you cannot make it out at a distance of a few rods.

None of it was country Mr. Kinzie knew; his journeys from the Portage down to Chicago had always gone the direct way, by Kosh-ko-nong. So he made Mr. Morrison say the directions over, and over again after that, while Plante, who was our guide, swaggered about and talked big, and declared that "he knew every hill and stream and point of woods from that spot to Chicago."

We were not many miles out before it became plain that Monsieur Plante did not know the country at all. My husband took the lead himself after that and worked his way forward as best he could on the directions he had been given. No crossing trail appeared, none of what we had been promised. The path under us kept bending further from the direction we knew to be right, until at last there was nothing to do but leave it behind.

We rode the whole of that day and stopped only briefly at noon, to bait the horses and get a little luncheon into ourselves. I cannot describe how desolate that ride was. Rolling prairie without a tree or a watercourse in it. One swell of ground after another, and at the top of each we looked out hoping for a far-off fringe of timber, and got instead the same cheerless country running on without any end to it. The only break in it anywhere was an occasional long, narrow opening cut in the ground, like a giant's grave, where somebody had dug for signs of a lead-mine and found none.

We wanted the trail back so badly — the cold was deepening, and the wind had gone sharp enough to cut — that we would not be drawn off our course even by the gaunt prairie-wolf that showed itself more than once, watching us over the nearest rise as though it would like us to come and try it. The Frenchmen shouted and put spurs in the instant they saw it, and Kelda and Cora went for it, but the bourgeois called dogs and men back each time, and reminded them that our whole business was to keep moving. Just before sundown we got ourselves with some trouble across a muddy stream that had a thin line of trees along it, enough to make a tolerable camping-place, and we took it gladly, having no idea whether we were near what we were looking for or nowhere near it at all.

We had covered fifty miles at the least since leaving Morrison's, and yet I felt hardly any fatigue. What I did feel was a low, unsettled discomfort at being lost in that wild, cold country, a thing wholly new to me. But my husband said, in that encouraging way of his, "Cheer up, wifie - we will find the trail tomorrow," and it went away.

The men soon had the camp in decent order, though the ground was frozen too hard to take a tent-pin easily and there was no timber big enough for the sort of fire we wanted. It was a stony side-hill, which is what they would call it in New England, where they have plenty of them. We were in no position to be fastidious. We ate our salt ham and toasted our bread and listened with pleasure to the Frenchmen talking, who could not say enough about the heroism of "Madame John" under the vicissitudes that kept befalling her.

By bedtime the wind was high enough to be a nuisance, and it kept rising through the night. Then it began to snow hard. We lay there expecting at any minute to have the tent taken off us by the wind. That was not how it went. The poles snapped instead, and the whole of it came down on us at once with all the snow it had been carrying.

Mr. Kinzie got his men up and took them off into the nearest wood to cut a new set of poles. That left me holding the entire weight of it on my shoulders, because the only shelter I had from the storm was to stay where I was, snug underneath the canvas.

They contrived some kind of support for the covering at last, and it held well enough until daylight. By then we were so damp and so wretched that we were glad to be up and standing over the fire until breakfast was got through and the horses were saddled again.

What we saw when we looked out was not encouraging. Snow all round us, unbroken, every direction the same. We had no compass, and the driving sleet thickened the air so that half the time there was no telling where the sun stood in it. I tied my husband's silk pocket-handkerchief over my veil to keep the wind and the flying ice off my face — they cut like a razor — and even shielded every way that could be managed out there, I suffered from the cold more than I had known was possible.

On we went, mile after mile, turning into every point of woods we came to on the chance of finding an Indian wigwam where we might at least ask a question. Every one of them was empty. Deserted, and with no sign that anybody had lit a fire there lately to encourage us that a living soul was within miles of where we stood.

Then the man at the head of the party shouted, and every heart in the line jumped.

"Une clôture! une clôture!" (A fence! a fence!)

It was almost like life coming back into the dead.

We spurred forward, and there, sure enough, were a few straggling rails along the crown of a rise not far ahead of us.

And at that moment a cock crowed somewhere beyond it, and there has never been music in this world half so sweet.
