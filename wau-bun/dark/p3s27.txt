A few weeks after we got home, my husband took his mother down to Prairie du Chien to be seen by Dr. Beaumont, of the U.S. Army, for the benefit of his medical advice.

They went in a large open boat down the Wisconsin, and since the boat had to make the trip anyway it was decided to bring back a good supply of corn, enough to carry the men and the cattle through the winter.

The ice caught them. It formed in the river so early that he had barely started up with his load before he had to turn and take the whole of it back to the Prairie, and sit there, and wait — wait until the winter's ice lay thick enough to hold sleighs, and then make the journey a second time and bring the corn up over the top of the river he could not travel on. That is what it cost, in time and labor and exposure, to move the plain necessities of life from one point to another in that wild and desolate country.

My brother Arthur came up from Kentucky by way of the Mississippi in the latter part of April, and he brought the news with him. There was new trouble with the Sauks and Foxes. Black Hawk had recrossed the Mississippi with the flower of his nation, to take back the old homes and the old corn-fields. Our own Indians began coming in almost at once to confirm it and to tell us, over and over, that they meant to stay faithful friends to the Americans. Then came word that the Illinois Rangers were in the Rock River country, and that the regular force under General Atkinson was in pursuit of the hostile Indians — who, by every report that reached us, were never where the pursuit was. It is not their custom to stand and give battle. The Sauks simply scattered themselves through the country and waited on a lucky accident to let them fall upon their enemies unexpectedly. Such accidents came, alas, only too often.

So the army's experience, for the most part, was this: a long, toilsome, punishing march to the place where the Sauks were certainly waiting to receive them, and then the discovery that the rogues had scampered off to some entirely different part of the country.

Wherever they did go, they left the most atrocious barbarities behind them, though the worst of those had not reached our ears yet. All we were told, at the time, was that they were down in the neighborhood of the Rock River and the Kishwaukee, and that they let no opportunity pass of falling upon defenceless inhabitants and murdering them cruelly.

Once it was clear the Sauks and Foxes would not do what they had done the year before — retreat peaceably across the Mississippi — Mr. Kinzie resolved to hold a council with every principal chief of the Winnebagoes he could reach. He knew the Sauks would spend every effort trying to bring their neighbors in with them, and he knew, too, that in the breasts of too many of the young men there was a real hunger to distinguish themselves by taking some white scalps. They did not love the Americans. Why should they? By the Americans they had been dispossessed, piece by piece, of the broad and beautiful country of their forefathers, and hunted from place to place, and all they had received in exchange was a few thousands a year in silver and presents, together with the pernicious example, the debasing influence, and the outright ill treatment of far too many of the new settlers on their land.
