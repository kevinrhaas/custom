For a while the passage over Thunder Bay was delightful. It was not destined, in our favor, to belie its name. A storm came on fast and furious, and worse than its fury was its duration. The pitching and rolling of the little boat, the closeness below, even the sea-sickness — we bore all of it as became us. They were what we had expected and had prepared for. Then a new feature of our discomfort appeared, and it almost upset our philosophy.

The rain fell in torrents, and it found its way through every seam and pore of deck and moulding. It came down the stairway. It came through the joints and the crevices. It saturated the carpet first, then the bedding, until at last we were driven, by stress of weather, into the Gentlemen's Cabin. Way was made for us very gallantly there. Every provision was resorted to for our comfort, and we were congratulating ourselves on having found a haven in our distress when the seams above us opened, and such a flood came down upon our devoted heads that even umbrellas were no protection at all. Nothing was left for the ladies and the children but the berths, which in that apartment had fortunately stayed dry, and there we stayed ensconced the whole day long. Our dinner was served up to us on our pillows. The gentlemen chose the driest spots they could find, raised their umbrellas, and sat under them telling amusing anecdotes and saying funny things to cheer us, hour after hour, until the rain ceased at last and at nine o'clock in the evening we were gladdened by the news that we had reached the pier at Mackinac.
