The crossing of Thunder Bay was lovely for a while. The bay did not intend to let its name go unearned on our account. The storm arrived all at once and came on hard, and worse, it did not pass. We took the pitching and rolling of that little boat as we ought to, and the closeness below, and the sea-sickness. All of that we had known was coming and had made up our minds to. What we had not counted on was a fresh kind of misery, and it nearly finished off our philosophy for us.

The rain came down in sheets and found every seam and every pore in the deck and the moulding overhead. It ran down the stair-way. It worked through the joints and the cracks. It soaked the carpet first and then the bedding, and at last, by stress of weather, we were driven right out of our own quarters and into the Gentlemen's Cabin. The gentlemen gave way to us very handsomely and did everything they could for our comfort, and we had just begun to congratulate ourselves on having found a harbor in the middle of our trouble when the seams over that cabin opened too, and the water came down on our heads in such quantity that umbrellas were no use at all. Nothing remained for the ladies and the children but the berths, which in that room had stayed dry, and there we lay the whole day long. Our dinner was handed to us on our pillows. The men picked out the driest patches of floor, put up their umbrellas, and sat under them telling us funny stories and saying cheerful things until the rain finally stopped, and at nine that evening word came down that we had made the pier at Mackinac.
