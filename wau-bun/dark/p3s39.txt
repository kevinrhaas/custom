Black Hawk got away northward with a handful of his warriors, and not long afterward the One-eyed Day-kau-ray and his party ran him down and delivered him as a prisoner to General Street at Prairie du Chien. The women and children of his band had already been dealt with another way — loaded into canoes and pushed down the Mississippi, on the hope that somebody would let them cross and rejoin their tribe.

Somebody had lashed the canoes together. A great many of them went over, and the children went into the river, and their mothers were too weak and worn out to reach them. Those who lived were taken prisoner and brought up to Prairie du Chien, starving and wretched. Our mother was at the Port when they came in. She had never seen people brought so low. One woman had a little Chippewa, and with it she laid out for our mother what the band had suffered and endured. It was appalling.

They had eaten the horses they could spare. After the horses it was acorns, and elm-bark, and at the end of it grass. Many died of starvation, and the whites coming up behind them found the bodies lying along the trail. The woman who spoke to our mother had lost her husband in the fighting and every one of her children in the canoe that went over, and the only thing she wanted now was to go where they had gone. Poor Indians! who can wonder that they don't love the whites?
