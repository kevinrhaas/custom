Black Hawk got away northward with a handful of his warriors. It bought him little time. The One-eyed Day-kau-ray and his party ran him down and delivered him as a prisoner to General Street at Prairie du Chien. The women and the children of his band had already been dealt with another way — loaded into canoes and pushed off down the Mississippi, on the hope that somebody would let them cross over and rejoin the rest of their tribe.

Somebody had lashed the canoes to one another. That was the detail that mattered. On the current they went over, one dragging the next, and the children went into the river, and their mothers were too starved and too spent to go in after them. Those who lived through it were taken prisoner and brought up to Prairie du Chien, hungry and past caring. Our mother was at the Port when they came in. She had never seen people brought so low. One of the women had a little Chippewa, and with it she laid out what the band had endured. There is no softening it. It was appalling.

They had eaten the horses they could spare. After the horses it was acorns, and elm-bark, and at the end of it grass. Some stopped and died where they stopped, and the whites coming on behind found the bodies lying along the trail. The woman who spoke to our mother had lost her husband in the fighting and every one of her children in the canoe that went over, and the only thing she wanted now was to go where they had gone. Poor Indians! who can wonder that they don't love the whites?
