There were two divisions of the Winnebago Indians. One was paid by the Agent, at the Portage; the other at Prairie du Chien, by General Street. The first of them numbered between four and five thousand people, and under the treaty stipulations they received fifteen thousand dollars a year, along with a considerable amount of presents, and a certain number of rations of bread and pork to be issued in times of emergency through the year.

The principal villages of this division stood at Lake Winnebago, at Green and Fox Lakes, at the Barribault, at Mud Lake, at the Four Lakes, at Kosh-ko-nong and at Turtle Creek. When the annuity-money came, or a little before it, messengers went out to every one of those villages to notify the heads of families and lodges to assemble at the Portage.

When they arrive, the masters of families, under their several chiefs, give in their names and the number of people in their lodges to be registered. Since a fixed sum is apportioned to each individual, it is of course an object to a head of a lodge to make his registered number as large as it will go. Each man brings his little bundle of sticks and presents it to the Agent for entry. Sometimes a dialogue like this one follows:

"How many have you in your lodge?"

The Indian counts his bundle of sticks over carefully, and with great ceremony. "Fifteen."

"How many men?"

"Two." The Agent lays two sticks aside. "How many women?"

"Three." Three more are separated out.

"How many children?"

"Eight." Eight sticks are added to the heap.

"What is the meaning of these two sticks that stay?"

The culprit, whose arithmetic has not carried his deception all the way through, disappears in the shouts and jeers of his companions, who are always well pleased at the detection of any roguery they have had no share in.

The young officers generally helped count out and hand over the money at these payments, and it was no unusual thing, when the last band came up, for the chiefs to take a quantity of silver out of the box and ask their Father to pay his friends for their trouble. They seemed really disturbed when he refused. In that, as in nearly everything, you see the native courtesy and politeness that they never lose sight of among themselves. If a party comes to their Father to beg for provisions and food is set before them, then however hungry they are, every one of them waits while one of the company divides the whole of it into equal shares; and then each takes his share and eats it quietly, with the greatest moderation. I never saw that rule broken but once.

Our friend Pawnee Blanc, the Old Dandy, came one day with a party of Indians and asked leave to dance for us in the open space before the door. It was a warm, dusty afternoon, and as our friends grew heated and tired with the violence and length of the exercise, a pitcher of raspberry negus was made and sent out to them. Pawnee took the pitcher and the tumbler. He poured the tumbler about half full and gave it to the first man of the circle, then filled it the same way for the next, and so on down the line — until it occurred to him to look into the pitcher. What he saw in there settled his course of action. He set the tumbler down on the ground, raised the pitcher in both hands to his lips, and took a long, hearty pull; after which he went on down the circle, giving less and less, until he was called in to have the pitcher replenished. Everyone present agreed it was the only instance any of them had ever witnessed of an Indian appearing afraid of getting less of a thing than his share.

During a payment a good many kegs of whiskey find their way into the lodges, whatever the watchfulness of the officers and the Agent. Where there is a demand there will always be a supply, let the legal prohibitions be what they may. The last day of the payment is, without exception, a day of general carousing.

When the men begin their frolic, the women go quietly around and gather up the guns, the knives, the tomahawks, every weapon of any description, and hide them all, so that as little mischief as possible can be done in the absence of restraint and reason. I am sorry to have to record that our little friend Pawnee Blanc was greatly addicted to the pleasures of the bottle.

Among the presents Shaw-nee-aw-kee had brought from the East for the chiefs were a trunk of blue cloth coats trimmed with broad gold lace, and a box of round black hats ornamented in the same way. Anyone who knows Indians of any tribe will have noticed that their first step toward civilization, in man or woman alike, is mounting a man's hat, decorated with tinsel or ribbons or feathers. Pawnee was among the happy number remembered in the distribution, and he put his new costume on at once, tied a few additional bunches of gay-colored ribbons to the long spear that was always his baton of ceremony, and came straight over, followed by an admiring train made up chiefly of women, to pay me a visit of state.

The solemn gravity of his face as he waved off anyone who came near enough to finger his newly received finery, and the dignity with which he strutted along, edging this way and that to avoid any possible contact with homely, every-day wardrobes, augured well for a long continuance of propriety and self-respect and a due regard for the good opinion of all around him. But alas for Pawnee. Late in the day we saw him coming back toward his lodge held up between two stout young Indians who had just pulled him out of a ditch — his fine coat covered with mud, his hat battered and bruised, his spear shorn of its gay streamers, and poor Pawnee himself weeping and uttering all the doleful lamentations of a tipsy Indian.
