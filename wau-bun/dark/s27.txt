The Winnebago were paid in two divisions. One of them came to the Agent at the Portage; the other went to General Street at Prairie du Chien. Ours numbered somewhere between four and five thousand people, and under the treaty stipulations they were owed fifteen thousand dollars a year, along with a considerable quantity of presents, and so many rations of bread and pork besides, to be given out at need across the rest of the year.

Their principal villages lay scattered wide: at Lake Winnebago; at Green and at Fox Lakes; at the Barribault; at Mud Lake; at the Four Lakes; at Kosh-ko-nong; at Turtle Creek. When the annuity-money was on its way, or had just come in, messengers rode out to all of them with word that the heads of families and lodges were to gather at "the Portage."

They came in by band, each master of a family under his own chief, and gave his name and the number of people in his lodge to be written down. Payment was reckoned by the head, so a man had a plain interest in making his lodge look as large as the register would allow. Each one brought a small bundle of sticks and handed it over to the Agent to be counted. What followed sometimes went like this:

"How many have you in your lodge?"

The man counts through his bundle, slowly, with a great deal of ceremony - "Fifteen"

"How many men?"

"Two." Two sticks are laid to one side "How many women?"

"Three." Three more are set apart.

"How many children?"

"Eight" Eight sticks join the heap.

"What is the meaning of these two sticks that stay?"

And the man whose arithmetic has not been equal to his ambition goes, off through a crowd of his own people hooting and laughing after him — nothing delights them more than a piece of roguery found out that they had no part in.

The young officers usually helped count the money out and hand it over, and it happened often enough that when the last band came forward the chiefs would take up a handful of the silver from the box and ask their Father to see his friends paid for the trouble they had taken; his refusing it genuinely distressed them. It was of a piece with everything else about them. That courtesy, that politeness, is never once let go of. Let a party come and beg provisions of their Father, and be offered food, and however hungry they may be, every one of them waits while a single man divides the whole into equal shares, and then takes his own portion and eats it quietly and with the greatest moderation. I saw that rule broken exactly once.

Our friend Pawnee Blanc — the Old Dandy — came one afternoon with a party who asked leave to dance for us on the open ground in front of the door. The day was warm and dusty, the dancing was violent and went on a long while, and when they were thoroughly heated and worn out with it a pitcher of raspberry negus was made up and carried out to them. Pawnee took charge of the pitcher and the tumbler. He filled the tumbler about halfway and handed it to the first man of the circle, filled it the same for the next, and went on down the line — and then he thought to look down into the pitcher. What he found in there settled his course of action. He set the tumbler on the ground, raised the pitcher in both hands to his lips, and took a long and hearty pull at it. Then he resumed, pouring less, and then less again, until somebody called for the pitcher to be replenished. Every person present agreed it was the only time any of them had seen an Indian look afraid of coming away with less than his share.

Whiskey gets into the lodges during a payment — a good many kegs of it — and neither the officers nor the Agent can watch closely enough to keep it out. Where there is a demand there will be a supply, and the legal prohibitions may say what they like. The last day of a payment is, without fail, given over to carousing of the general kind.

Once the men are well into their frolic, the women move through and collect every gun, knife, tomahawk and weapon of whatever description, and put them out of sight, so that as little mischief as possible gets done while restraint and reason are absent. I am sorry to have to set it down that our little friend Pawnee Blanc loved the bottle far too well.

Among the presents Shaw-nee-aw-kee had brought back from the East for the chiefs were a trunk of blue coats in cloth, laced broadly in gold, and a box of round black hats got up in the same manner. Anyone who knows Indians, of whatever tribe, has noticed that the first step toward civilization, in a man or in a woman, is mounting a man's hat and trimming it with tinsel or ribbon or feathers. The distribution remembered Pawnee among its happy number. He put the whole costume on at once, tied a few more bunches of gay-colored ribbon to the long spear he carried always as his baton of ceremony, and came straight over to pay me a visit of state, with an admiring train at his back, most of them women.

There was a wonderful solemn gravity on his face as he motioned away anyone who came close enough to finger the new finery, and he strutted, and he edged aside from this person and that so that no homely, every-day wardrobe should brush him in passing. All of it argued a long reign of propriety, of self-respect, of a due regard for the good opinion of everybody around him. But alas for Pawnee. Late in the day he came past us on the way to his lodge, held up between two stout young men who had just hauled him up out of a ditch. The fine coat was plastered with mud, the hat was battered and bruised, the spear had been shorn of every gay streamer, and Pawnee himself was weeping and uttering every doleful lamentation known to a tipsy Indian.
