It was nearly dark when we came up on Lawton's. The Aux Plaines was frozen over and the house stood on the other side of it. We shouted, and kept shouting, until a man came out of the building; then he set about cutting the ice and bringing a canoe across to us, and by the time he had done it we could no longer make out one object from another in the dark.

It was a very comfortable house, Lawton's, once we were finally in it — carpeted, with a warm stove, quite in civilized style. Mr. Weeks, the man who had brought us over the water, was the major-domo there during the temporary absence of Mr. Lawton.

Mrs. Lawton was a young woman, and not ill-looking. She complained bitterly of the loneliness of her condition, and of having been brought out there into the woods, which was a thing she hadn't expected when she came from the East. We did not ask her what expectations she had brought with her to a wild, unsettled country. We only tried to comfort her with the assurance that things would grow better in a few years. She said she didn't mean to wait for that. She should go back to her family in the East, if Mr. Lawton didn't invite some of her young friends to come and stay with her, and make it agreeable.
