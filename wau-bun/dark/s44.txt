The light was nearly gone by the time Lawton's came in sight. The Aux Plaines[20] had frozen over, and the house stood on the far bank with the ice between. We shouted until somebody came out of the building. He set to work cutting a channel and working a canoe through it toward us, and it took so long that by the time he reached us the shapes on the shore had blurred together.

It was worth the wait. Lawton's turned out to be a thoroughly comfortable house — carpets on the floor, a stove going, everything quite in civilized style. Mr. Weeks, the man who had brought us across the water, was running the place as major-domo while Mr. Lawton was away.

Mrs. Lawton was young, and not ill-looking. She was also bitterly unhappy, and she said so: the loneliness of her condition, and the fact of her having been "brought out there into the woods, which was a thing she hadn't expected, when she came from the East." We did not ask her what she had expected of a wild and unsettled country. We only said what people say — that in a few years things would be better. She answered that "She didn't mean to wait for that. She should go back to her family in the East, if Mr. Lawton didn't invite some of her young friends to come and stay with her, and make it agreeable."
