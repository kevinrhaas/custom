Morning came up hot and close. The rain had held the mosquitoes off all night; now they came back to collect what they were owed, and they tormented us without mercy.

Breakfast was a miserable business and barely worth eating. That left the question of what on earth to do with ourselves. Our boat was still under water where she had gone down, below the hill and half-way up the rapids. As for the Waubanakee houses, the nearest of them stood miles away, and nothing would carry us there but an Indian canoe, and only supposing one of our present friends and neighbors chose to be obliging enough to fetch one for our use. Even granting that, whether boats enough existed to move a party as numerous as ours back to Green Bay was another matter entirely.

We were still turning it over when a whoop came from the far side of the hill, where the ground falls away northward at the top of the rapids.

"There is John! that is certainly his voice!" — the cry went up from several of the company at once.

And it was my husband. A moment later he was standing in the middle of us. No arrival was ever better timed, or more plainly providential.

He knew nothing of our plans; the unsettled state of the country had made it impossible to send him word. He had simply come with a boat, meaning to carry us home to Fort Winnebago.

That finished the drying we had begun again that morning. Everything was bundled up as fast as hands could move, and in an incredibly short time we had been shifted into his boat, which was waiting above the Chûte out of the current, and the oars were taking us up toward Winnebago Lake.

Our plan went no farther than the neighborhood of the lake, where a breeze would take the edge off the mosquitoes and we could make one more attempt on our clothing. So we put in at a fine high bank near the Little Butte and unpacked the trunks again, and before long there was something hanging from every bush and twig within reach — whatever the cruel waves had left us.

The last rag and ribbon had barely gone out when we heard horses coming, and after them shouting and cheering that rang in through the forest.

Twenty-five horsemen or so rode straight into the middle of us, banners up, veils streaming from their hats, arms flashing in the sun, and between the greetings and the roars of laughter they demanded to know the nature and the reasons of the singular confusion they had found us in.

Colonel Stambough and Alexander Irwin had come out of Green Bay at the head of a company of young volunteers, with a whooping band of Menomonees behind them, the whole party making for the seat of war. We consoled them with the news that every victory had been won by now and every scalp taken. They said they hoped a few laurels might be left to earn yet, bade us adieu, and pressed on at speed.

A clear summer moon lit Lake Winnebago for our crossing. The air was soft and just enough of it stirred to fill the sail, so the men were spared the oars.

None of that witchery was enough to make us give up our rest after a day of heat and torment, and we arranged ourselves early to be stowed away in the middle of the boat. Not one of the numerous company could afterward explain how the thing was managed. Had any accident disturbed the arrangement, putting us back would have been a Chinese puzzle. The men lying along the outside had far the better of it, and we envied those off watch, who could snore and shift about whenever the humor took them.

Powell's came up exactly in time for us to go ashore and cook breakfast, supposing we had owned anything to cook. We had counted on supplies there. Until then we had eaten our way through what was left of my husband's ample stock, and it had got so low that when the mess-basket could not be filled at that place, talk began of putting ourselves on allowance.

Plenty of the wet bread remained, and it did what wet bread does under a July sun, and fermented. The tea went the same road; we had spread it on newspapers and bits of board and dried it carefully, and it turned musty and useless regardless. That left salted meat and a few crackers — literally nothing else, and hardly enough of that for the day we were standing in.

So the men were pressed for every mile they had in them, the object being to make Gleason's, at Lake Puckaway, in good season the next day.

In the evening we took our tea ashore in a lovely little clearing among the trees, and there our old enemies were worse than we had yet had them. We sat in cloaks and gloves with our veils tied close about the throat, and got a cracker or a swallow of tea in under the veil by stealth when we could.

The men rowed well and had us at Gleason's by eleven the next day. La Grosse Américaine, my old friend, who had moved there from Bellefontaine, greeted us with the most enthusiastic demonstrations.

"Oh, Mrs. Armstrong," cried we, "get us some breakfast - we are famishing!"

And then who should drive up but our faithful Mâtâ, in the old calèche that used to carry us on our little excursions about the Port. He had come over on the chance of meeting us, supposing some of the party would rather finish the journey home on wheels.

He had thought ahead as well — tea, roasted coffee, fresh butter, eggs and the rest of it, in case such luxuries had run out on us so far along in the trip.

That "Good-morning, Madame Johns! How do you dos?" of his fell on us like music.

We could not wait for breakfast to be cooked. We crowded round her like so many children while she cut bread and spread the butter on it for us.

Once the regular meal was over, we settled it that sister Margaret would take Josette and go back with Mâtâ to open the house and put it in order for the rest of us. It had served the whole time we were gone as head-quarters for militia, Indians and stragglers of every description, and it was easy enough to imagine that some degree of "misrule and unreason" had held sway there for the duration.

Seventy-two miles still lay ahead of us, counting every bend the river takes — first across the beautiful water of Lac de Boeuf, then in among the low marshy country that runs out toward the Portage. One of the gentlemen decided to work up a small fright among the ladies as we came near the place where people had supposed the Sauks might cross on their way into the Chippewa country.

"Who knows," he said, gravely, "but they may be lurking in this neighborhood yet? If so, we shall probably have some signal. We must be on the alert!"

One or two of the ladies lost their color and began looking about them. Then came a stretch of perfect silence, and out of it a low, drawn-out whistle. There was so much agitation, and in one or two cases such genuine terror, that the man behind the mischief had to own up on the spot and take a hearty scolding for the fright he had given.

The second day out from Gleason's brought us to our own door just before sunset. The place shone with neatness and good order. Our good Manaigre and his wife had given efficient help: whitewash from the roof down to the door-sill, a scrubbing and cleansing right through, the carpets out of their wrappings and laid on the floors, the furniture back where it belonged, and — last, and by no means least — a noble supper standing on the board and steaming by the time we had got ourselves into a civilized toilet again.

A good many friends from the Fort had come to meet us, and I doubt a happier or more thankful party has often been gathered under one roof.
