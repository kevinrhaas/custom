Whatever the tribe, they are hungry for stories — to tell them and to sit and hear them told, history or invention, it makes no difference which. They keep professed storytellers, as the nations of the East do, and the man goes from village to village and gathers a crowd around him that listens hard and admires everything, though there is not a word in the recitation that any of them is hearing for the first time.

That is how the traditions come down whole, generation after generation, with nothing worn off them. Their knowledge of the geography of their own country is exact in a way that would shame a surveyor. I have watched a man sit in his lodge and draw the Northwestern States in the ashes with one finger — not the country as the statistics have it, but the shape of the land itself, the lakes, the rivers, the high ground — and get it right, and give the relative distances in days' journeys without once stopping to think, and then carry the drawing further south and keep talking, down as far as Kentucky and Tennessee.

Their biography works the same way. They keep not only the leading events of a man's life but his features, his appearance, his bearing, his manners, and whatever small peculiarity it was that made him himself and nobody else.

The women prefer fiction, and some of their stories mix humor and grief in a way I have met nowhere else. I set down two of them here as specimens. The Indian names in them are Ottawa — Courte-Oreilles — but the same tales are current in every tongue and dialect on this frontier.

A great many peculiarities are attributed to this animal. They say he is like the jackal, that he will trouble the graves of the dead. They dread hearing his bark at night, because it forebodes calamity, and a death with it. And they say he was not always as he is now. He was one uniform color once, a plain reddish brown, all of him, legs and all. The legs went black in the way the story tells.

There was a chief of a certain village who had a beautiful daughter. He decided one time to make a feast and to invite all the animals. When the invitation was carried to the red fox, the fox asked, "What are you going to have for supper?"

"Mee-dau-mee-nau-bo," the messenger said. That is a porridge made of parched corn, slightly cracked.

The fox turned up his little sharp nose. "No, I thank you," he said. "I can get plenty of that at home."

The messenger went back to the chief and reported the contemptuous refusal of the fox.

"Go back to him," said the chief, "and tell him we are going to have a nice fresh body, and we will have it cooked in the most delicate manner possible."

The prospect of such a treat pleased the fox very much, and he gave a hearty assent to the second invitation.

The hour came and he set off for the lodge of the chief to attend the feast. The company were all prepared for him. They had made common cause with their friend who had been insulted. As the fox came in at the door, the guest nearest it rose with great courtesy and begged the newcomer to take his seat. Right away the next man rose too and insisted that the fox should have his place instead, as it was nearer the fire — the post of honor. Then the third pressed him, with many expressions of civility, to exchange with him. And so, with a great many ceremonious flourishes, he was passed along the circle, always drawing nearer to the fire, where a huge cauldron stood with the good cheer still cooking in it. The fox was by no means unwilling to occupy the highest place in the assembly. He was also anxious to get a look into the kettle, because he had begun to suspect that he might be disappointed of the delicacies he had been promised.

So by degrees he was ushered nearer and nearer the great blazing fire, until with one dexterous push and shove he was hoisted into the seething kettle.

His feet were dreadfully scalded. He leaped out of it and ran home to his own lodge, howling and crying with the pain. His grandmother, with whom he lived, according to the custom of animals, demanded an account of the whole affair. He told her every circumstance faithfully — unlike the civilized animals, it never occurred to him to tell his grandmother a story — and when he had finished she reproved him very strongly.

"You have committed two great faults," she said. "In the first place you were very rude to the chief, who was kind enough to invite you, and by returning insult for civility you made yourself enemies who were determined to punish you. In the second place it was very unbecoming in you to be so forward to take the place of honor. Had you been content to keep your seat modestly near the door, you would have escaped the misfortune that has befallen you."

None of this was very consolatory to the poor fox, who went on whining and crying most piteously while his grandmother, her lecture finished, went to bind up his wounds. It is thought that great virtue is added to any medicine or application by a little dancing. So the dressing was put on, and then the grandmother fell to dancing with all her might, round and round the lodge.

When she was nearly exhausted the fox said, "Grandmother, take off the bandages and see if my legs are healed."

She did as he asked. The burns were still fresh. She danced and danced again. Now and then, as he grew impatient, she would take off the coverings to observe the effect of the remedies. Toward morning at last she looked, and sure enough, the burns were quite healed. "But, oh!" she cried, "your legs are as black as a coal! They were so badly burned that they will never return to their color!"

The poor fox, who like many another brave was vain of his legs, fell into a transport of lamentation.

"Oh! my legs! My pretty red legs! What shall I do? The young girls will all despise me. I shall never dare to show myself among them again!"

He cried and sobbed until his grandmother, worn out with her exercise, fell asleep. By that time he had decided upon his plan of revenge.

He got up and stole softly out of the lodge and made his way quickly toward the village of the chief. There he turned his face in the direction of the principal lodge, and barked. The people inside heard that sound come out of the stillness of the night, and their hearts trembled in them. They knew what it foreboded: sorrow and trouble to some one of their number.

Very little time passed before the beautiful daughter of the chief fell sick. She grew worse and worse, in spite of the medicines and the charms and the dances. In the end she died. The fox had not intended to bring the misfortune down on the village in that particular shape, for he loved the beautiful daughter of the chief, and he kept close in his lodge and mourned and fretted over her death.

They made preparations for a magnificent funeral, but the friends of the dead girl were in great perplexity. "If we bury her in the earth," they said, "the fox will come and disturb her remains. He has barked her to death, and he will be glad enough to come and finish his work of revenge."

They took counsel together and determined to hang her body high in a tree for a place of sepulture. Their reasoning was that the fox would go groping about in the earth for her, and would never lift up his eyes to the branches over his head.

But the grandmother had been at the funeral. She came home and told the fox everything that had been done.

"Now, my son," she said, "listen to me. Don't meddle with the remains of the chief's daughter. You have done mischief enough already. Leave her in peace."

As soon as the grandmother was asleep that night, the fox went out. He found the place he was looking for without much trouble, and he came and sat down under the tree where the young girl had been put. He gazed and gazed at her all the livelong night, and she looked as beautiful as she had in life. But when the day came on, and the light let him see more clearly, he saw that decay was doing its work — that what hung above him was not beautiful at all now, but loathsome.

He went home sad and afflicted and passed the whole day mourning in his lodge.

"Have you disturbed the remains of the chief's beautiful daughter?" was his parent's anxious question.

"No, grandmother." And he uttered not another word.

So it went on for many days and many nights. The fox always took care to leave his watch at the first dawn of day, because he knew that her friends would suspect him, and would come early to see whether all was as they had left it.

And in time he began to notice that she looked less and less hideous in the morning light — that by degrees she was resuming the appearance she had worn in life, until at last her beauty and her look of health had quite come back to her.

One day he said, "Grandmother, give me my pipe, that I may take a smoke."

"Ah!" she cried, "you begin to be comforted. You have never smoked since the death of the chief's beautiful daughter. Have you heard some good news?"

"Never you mind," he said. "Bring the pipe."

He sat down and smoked, and smoked. After a while he said, "Grandmother, sweep your lodge and put it all in order, for this day you will receive a visit from your daughter-in-law."

The grandmother did as she was told. She swept the lodge and arranged it with all the taste she possessed, and then the two of them sat down to wait for the visit.

"When you hear a sound at the door," the fox said, "you must give the salutation, and say, Come in."

They had been sitting that way for some time when the grandmother heard a faint rustling sound. She looked toward the door. The mat that usually hung there as a curtain had been rolled up, and the door stood open.

"Peen-tee-geen n'dau-nis!" she cried. Come in, my daughter.

Something like a faint, faint shadow seemed to glide in. It took on a more distinct outline by degrees. As she looked and looked at it she began to make out the form and features of the chief's beautiful daughter; but it was a long while before she appeared like a reality, and took her place in the lodge like a thing of flesh and blood.

They kept the matter hidden very close, for they would not for the world have had the father or the friends of the bride learn what had happened. Before long, though, it began to be rumored about that the chief's beautiful daughter had returned to life and was living in the Red Fox's lodge. How it ever became known was a mystery, for the grandmother, of course, never spoke of it.

However it got out, the news made a great excitement in the village. "This must never be," they all said. "He barked her to death once, and who knows what he may do the next time?"

The father took a decided part at once. "The Red Fox isn't worthy of my daughter," he said. "I had promised her to the Hart, the finest and most elegant among the animals. Now that she has returned to life, I shall keep my word."

So the friends all went in a body to the lodge of the Red Fox. The bridegroom and the bride and the grandmother made all the resistance they could, but they were overpowered by numbers, and the Hart, who had waited conveniently outside where there was no danger, had the beautiful daughter of the chief put up on his back, and he coursed away through the forest to carry her home. When he reached the door of his own lodge he turned his head, and there was no bride in the place where he expected to see her. He had thought his burden very light from the beginning, but that had seemed to him natural enough in a spirit returned from the dead. It never entered his mind that she had glided from her seat at the very outset, and had slipped back in the middle of the tumult, unobserved, to her chosen husband.

One or two more attempts were made after that by her friends to repossess themselves of the young creature, and all of them failed. Then they said, "Let her stay where she is. It is true the Red Fox occasioned her death, but by his watchfulness and his care he caressed her into life again, so she rightfully belongs to him." And the Red Fox and his beautiful bride lived long together in great peace and happiness.
