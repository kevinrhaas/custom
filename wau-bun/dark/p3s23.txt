Whatever the tribe, the Indians love a story — telling one, or sitting still to hear one, and it makes no difference whether the thing is history or invention. Like the nations of the East they keep professional storytellers, men who travel from village to village and draw an audience that listens hard and admires, though what they recite is old and every soul in the lodge has heard it before.

That is how the traditions come down whole, one generation handing them to the next with nothing worn off them. Their grasp of the geography of their own country is astonishing in its exactness. I have watched a man sit in his lodge and draw the Northwestern States in the ashes with a finger — not the statistics of them but the shape of them, the lakes, the rivers, the mountains — setting them down accurately, naming the distances from one to the next in days of travel without once stopping to think, and carrying his drawing and his account of it southward as far as Kentucky and Tennessee.

They do the same with a life. They keep not only the great events of it but the man's face, his build, the way he carried himself, his manners, and every small habit or oddity that made him himself and nobody else.

The women prefer fiction, and some of their stories mingle the comic and the pitiable in a way I have met nowhere else. I set down two of them here as specimens. The Indian names in them come from the Ottawa — the "Courte-Oreilles" — though every tongue and dialect has its own telling of the same tales.

A great many peculiarities are laid on this animal. He is said to share the jackal's habit of disturbing the dead in their graves, and to hear him bark at night fills the Indians with a superstitious dread, since they hold that his voice foretells calamity and death. They say too that his coat was once a single even reddish-brown all over, and that his legs turned black in the way the story tells.

A certain village had a chief, and the chief had a beautiful daughter. It came into his head one day to give a feast, and every animal was to be invited to it. The messenger carried word to the red fox, who wanted to know first, "What are you going to have for supper?"

"Mee-dau-mee-nau-bo," the messenger told him. It is a porridge of parched corn, cracked a little.

The fox's little sharp nose went up. "No, I thank you," he said, "I can get plenty of that at home."

Back went the messenger to tell the chief how contemptuously the fox had refused him.

"Go back to him," said the chief, "and tell him we are going to have a nice fresh body, and we will have it cooked in the most delicate manner possible."

That was a prospect the fox liked very much, and he accepted the second invitation with a will.

When the hour came he set out for the chief's lodge. The company were ready for him; they had made common cause with the friend he had insulted. As he came in, the guest nearest the door rose with great courtesy and pressed his own seat on the new-comer. The next man was up immediately after and would hear of nothing but the fox taking his place, which stood nearer the fire and was therefore the post of honor. Then the third urged the same exchange on him with every expression of civility, and so, one ceremonious flourish after another, he was handed along the circle and always closer to the fire, where a huge cauldron stood with the good cheer still cooking in it. Occupying the highest place in the assembly suited the fox exactly. He also wanted a look into that kettle, a suspicion having grown on him that the delicacies he had come for might not be there at all.

So by degrees they ushered him nearer and nearer the great blazing fire, and then one dexterous push and shove hoisted him up and into the seething kettle.

It scalded his feet dreadfully. He got himself out and went home to his own lodge at a run, howling the whole way with the pain of it. His grandmother, who lived with him, as is the custom among the animals, demanded an account of the affair. He told her every circumstance faithfully — unlike the civilized animals, it never once occurred to him to tell his grandmother a story — and she reproved him very strongly.

"You have committed two great faults," she said. "Originally, you were very rude to the chief who was so kind as to invite you, and by returning insult for civility you made yourself enemies who were determined to punish you. In the next place, it was very unbecoming in you to be so forward to take the place of honor. Had you been contented modestly to keep your seat near the door, you would have escaped the misfortune that has befallen you."

None of which was much consolation to the poor fox, who went on whining and crying most piteously while his grandmother, having finished her lecture, set about binding up his wounds. A little dancing is held to add great virtue to any medical prescription or application, so, the dressing once in place, she began to dance — hard, and round and round the lodge.

She had nearly danced herself out when the fox said, "Grandmother, take off the bandages and see if my legs are healed."

She took them off. The burns were as raw as before. So she danced, and danced again, stopping when his impatience got the better of him to lift the coverings and observe what the remedies had done. Toward morning she looked once more and found the burns healed clean away. "But, oh!" cried she, "your legs are as black as a coal! They were so badly burned that they will never return to their color!"

Like many another brave, the fox was vain about his legs, and he broke into a transport of lamentation.

"Oh! my legs! My pretty red legs! What shall I do? The young girls will all despise me. I shall never dare to show myself among them again!"

He was sobbing still when his grandmother, worn out with her exercise, dropped off to sleep. By then he had settled his plan of revenge.

He got up and stole out of the lodge and went quickly toward the chief's village. There he faced the principal lodge and barked. The people inside heard it come through the stillness of the night, and their hearts trembled in them. They knew what it meant: sorrow and trouble on its way to some one of their number.

It was only a few days before the chief's beautiful daughter took sick. She grew rapidly worse, and neither medicines nor charms nor dances turned it, and in the end she died. This was not the shape in which the fox had wanted his misfortune to fall on the village, for he loved the chief's beautiful daughter, and he kept to his lodge and mourned and fretted over what he had done.

A magnificent funeral was prepared. But her friends were in great perplexity. "If we bury her in the earth," said they, "the fox will come and disturb her remains. He has barked her to death, and he will be glad to come and finish his work of revenge."

They talked it over among themselves and settled on hanging her body high up in a tree for its place of sepulture. The fox, they reckoned, would grope about in the ground and never think to raise his eyes into the branches over his head.

The grandmother, though, had been at the funeral, and she came home and told the fox every part of it.

"Now, my son," she said, "listen to me. Don't meddle with the remains of the chief's daughter. You have done mischief enough already. Leave her in peace."

That night, as soon as his grandmother slept, the fox rambled forth. He had no trouble finding the place, and he settled himself under the tree they had put the young girl in. All the livelong night he gazed at her, and she was as beautiful as she had been in life. Then day broke, and with light enough to see her properly he found decay at its work: nothing beautiful left to her, only something loathsome.

He went home sad and afflicted and spent the whole of the day in his lodge, mourning.

"Have you disturbed the remains of the chief's beautiful daughter?" his parent asked him anxiously.

"No, grandmother," - and nothing more than that would he say.

So it ran on, night after night and day after day. He was careful always to leave his watch at the first dawn, knowing her friends would suspect him and would come betimes to see that nothing had been touched.

And by degrees he perceived that the morning light showed her less hideous than it had, and that little by little she was taking back the appearance she had presented in life, until in process of time her beauty and her look of health had come back to her whole.

One day he asked for his pipe. "Grandmother, give me my pipe, that I may take a smoke."

"Ah!" said his grandmother, "you begin to be comforted. You have never smoked since the death of the chief's beautiful daughter. Have you heard some good news?"

"Never you mind," he told her, "bring the pipe."

He sat and smoked a long while. Then he said, "Grandmother, sweep your lodge and put it all in order, for this day you will receive a visit from your daughter-in-law."

She did as she was told, sweeping the lodge out and arranging it with all the taste she had in her, and then the two of them sat down to wait.

"When you hear a sound at the door," said the fox, "you must give the salutation, and say, Come in."

They had been sitting a while when a faint rustle reached the grandmother. She turned toward the doorway. The mat that generally hung there for a curtain had been rolled up, and the door stood open, which surprised her.

"Peen-tee-geen n'dau-nis!" she called out.

What came in was hardly more than a shadow, and a very faint one. Its outline sharpened as it came. She looked and kept looking, and out of it the form and the features of the chief's beautiful daughter gathered themselves; but a long time went by before the girl seemed like a reality at all, or sat down in the lodge like a creature of flesh and blood.

The two of them kept it strictly to themselves, for nothing on earth would have induced them to let the bride's father or her friends learn what had happened. All the same, word got about before long that the chief's beautiful daughter was alive again and was living in the lodge of the Red Fox. Nobody could say how it ever got out; the grandmother certainly never breathed a word of it.

However it traveled, the news set the village in an uproar. "This must never be," said they all. "He barked her to death once, and who knows what he may do next time?"

Her father came down on the question at once. "The Red Fox isn't worthy of my daughter," he said. "I had promised her to the Hart, the finest and most elegant among the animals. Now that she has returned to life, I shall keep my word."

So the whole body of them marched on the Red Fox's lodge. Bridegroom, bride and grandmother resisted with everything they had, and numbers beat them; and the Hart, who had conveniently kept himself outside, well clear of any danger, took the chief's beautiful daughter up on his back and coursed off through the forest for his own home. But at his own door he turned his head, and the place where he had expected a bride was empty. His burden had felt very light to him from the start, and he had thought nothing of it, supposing lightness natural in a spirit come back from the dead. It never entered his head that she had glided from her seat at the very outset and slipped away, unobserved, in the middle of the tumult, back to the husband she had chosen.

One or two more attempts were made by her friends to repossess themselves of the young creature, and not one of them succeeded. Then they said, "Let her stay where she is. It is true the Red Fox occasioned her death, but by his watchfulness and care he caressed her into life again, so she rightfully belongs to him." So the Red Fox kept his beautiful bride, and the two of them lived on a long while together in great peace and happiness.
