There is a mound at Butte des Morts, and the English of it is the Hillock of the Dead. A battle was fought on that ground long ago between the French and the Mis-qua-kees, whom everyone here calls the Foxes, and the killing ran so far past the ordinary that the rise of earth where the bodies were put down took its name from them and has carried it ever since. Until that day the Foxes had lived along the banks of the river that still goes by their name. They came out of that fight overwhelmed and beaten, and they left the river behind. They went down toward the Mississippi and asked shelter of their allies the Saukies — the Sauks, as they are called now — and year by year the two peoples grew into one another, until the joined tribes came to be known, as they are today, by the single name "Sauks and Foxes."

The French of the upper country keep a private name for every tribe, each one fastened to some quirk of habit or of body. The Chippewas are light on their feet, so they are "Sauteurs," which is to say Jumpers. The Ottawas are the "Courtes-oreilles," Short-ears. The Menomonees live where the wild rice stands thick, and so they are "Folles Avoines." The Winnebagoes bind the fur of a polecat about their legs when they go out equipped for war, and for that they are "les Puans." The Pottowattamies keep themselves poorly, and are therefore "les Poux." The Foxes are "les Renards." The list runs on in that fashion.

The consequence is that no French or half-breed resident of the country will name an Indian to you in any other way. "Such a person is a 'Court-oreille.'" "Is that woman a 'Winnebago'?" "No, she is a 'Folle Avoine.'" A newcomer spends his first weeks a good deal puzzled about how to sort the acquaintances he is making.

Every one of the native friends gathered around us in this place belonged to "les Puans" — though the word they use for themselves is far better sounding: the "Ho-tshung-rahs."

Saying adieu to Judge Doty went hard with me. His company had been the best part of the journey, and his example had taught us a thing worth carrying away, which is to take matters as you find them. We got our leave-taking over not long after breakfast, thanked our kind hosts, and set forward.

Butte des Morts to the Portage is about seventy miles if you go by land. By water it is not less than a hundred and thirty. The river refuses to run straight — it doubles and coils through the low swampy prairies that lie over so great a part of that country.
