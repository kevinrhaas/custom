There was one drawback to the little prisoner's happiness, apart from her longing after her own dear home: the wife of the Big White Man hated her. She had hated her from the day the child arrived and was taken into the family as a sister, and at first she had the prudence to keep it out of her husband's sight.

There was nothing strange in a wife feeling some jealousy at watching her own place in her husband's heart taken by the child of their enemy, the American. It might have stopped there. It did not, because the woman had a bad and vindictive temper to feed it, and because her husband met her complaints and her murmurings with indifference.

She had no children of her own to take up her attention. So her mind closed on the wrongs she believed had been done her, and then on what might be done about them, and it worked at that day after day. She did not have to wait long for her chance.

The Big White Man went off with a party — a war-party or a hunting-excursion, it made no difference — and while he was gone his little sister came down with fever and ague. The Old Queen nursed her with the utmost tenderness. So, to all appearances, did the chief's wife, who was tireless in her attentions to the little favorite, and who understood that this was the surest way to keep anyone from looking at her twice.

One afternoon, while the Old Queen was out of the lodge, her daughter-in-law came in carrying a bowl of something she had made. She crouched down beside the mat where the child was lying and spoke to her in an affectionate voice. "Drink, my sister. I have brought you that which will drive this fever far from you."

The little girl lifted her head to answer, and as she did she saw, through a crevice in the wall of the lodge, a pair of eyes. They were fixed on her, and there was something in them, some meaning being pushed at her very hard. She had a quick natural understanding, sharpened by her time among these people, and she used it. "Set it down, my sister," she said faintly. "When this fit of the fever has passed, I will drink your medicine."

The squaw was too careful to press her. She moved about the lodge a while longer, doing nothing in particular, and then went out to another lodge close by. The bright eyes stayed at the crevice. They followed the woman all the way out of sight. Then a low voice spoke — a young voice, a friend, one of the children she played with.

"Don't drink that which your brother's wife has brought you. She hates you, and is only waiting an opportunity to rid herself of you. I have watched her all the morning, and have seen her gathering the most deadly roots and herbs. I knew for whom they were intended, and came here to warn you."

"Take the bowl," said the little invalid, "and carry it to my mother's lodge."

It was carried there, and it was examined. What was in it was chiefly a decoction of the root of the May-apple, which is the deadliest poison known among the Indians.

There are no words for what went through that small community when the thing became known. The squaws ran to and fro, the way they do when they are roused, each of them trying to outdo the last in what she called down on the culprit's head. No punishment beyond that was inflicted on her for the present. When the first burst of rage had spent itself, she was met with a silent abhorrence instead.

The little patient was moved into the lodge of the Old Queen and kept under guard. Her enemy was left to walk by herself through the fields and the woods, in silence and solitude, waiting for her husband to come home and decide what would be done with her.

In a few days the excursion ended and the Big White Man came back to the village with his party. What he learned there was that someone had tried to kill his little sister. Contrary to the usual custom of savages, he took no summary vengeance in that first transport. He banished the woman from his lodge, never to return, and he condemned her to hoe corn in a far corner of the great field that served the whole community for a garden.

The vindictiveness in her never went out. Whenever the little girl and her companions strayed near that part of the field she would show it — a swing of the hoe at the child, or some other spiteful thing — but either she was watched too closely, or she stood too much in awe of the man who had been her husband, and she never made a second attempt on his sister's life.
