We must go back now to the place and the hour where this story began.

Late in the evening of that same day the father came home. The house was silent, and so was everything around it. Nothing living was left anywhere on the place, indoors or out. His nearest neighbors lived a long way off. He went to them at a run and demanded tidings of his family from people still climbing out of sleep.

One and another of them joined the search, and at the house of one of them the servant-maid was found. She had got away. Her first refuge, she said, had been a large brewing-tub in the outer kitchen, and she had got under it at the first alarm and stayed there. The Indians were plainly in a hurry. Their going gave her the chance to slip out and make for safety. Of her mistress and the children she could tell him nothing, except this: they had not been murdered where she could see it or hear it.

They quartered the neighborhood all night and turned up nothing. Then Mr. Lytle remembered an old settler who lived alone far up the valley, and they went straight there. The man had been at work in his field just before sunset, he said, and a party of strange Indians had passed close by his cabin. He had watched them wind along the brow of the hill. They had prisoners with them — a woman and a child. He knew the woman was white, he said, because she carried her infant in her arms, and not on her back, in the manner of the savages.

Day was breaking by then. The whole night had gone in searching and nothing had come of it. The father talked it over with the neighbors who had stayed with him, and when they offered to ride with him to Fort Pitt and lay the case before the commandant and the Indian Agent, he took the offer.

Going down the valley they came in sight of a hut they had searched in the dark and found empty. Two children were standing on the high bank in front of it, and the sight of them stopped the party where it stood. The father knew them at once for two of his missing flock. They could tell him nothing about their mother or the others. What they could tell him was simple, and they told it plainly.

They had been playing in the garden when they saw Indians come into the yard by the house. Nobody looked their way. The brother, who was six, got his little sister over the fence into a field grown thick with blackberry and wild raspberry, and they lay down in it. They stayed hidden there a while, and then, finding everything quiet, they began working toward the far side of the field, away from the house. The girl had pulled off her shoes and stockings in the garden, at play, and the briers went into her bare feet with every step. She was very close to crying out. Her brother took off his own stockings and put them on her, and then tried his shoes on her too, but they were too big and kept coming off, and she could not walk in them. They kept on. They believed they were escaping certain death, for, as I have said, the children of that country were raised on stories that taught them to see in every strange Indian a minister of torture, and of horrors worse than death. Then the little girl's strength ran out. Worn down by the pain and the walking, she said at last that she could go no farther.

"Then, Maggie," said her brother, "I must kill you, for I can't let you be killed by the Indians."

"Oh, no, Thomas!" she said. "Don't, pray don't kill me! I don't think the Indians will find us."

"Oh, yes, they will, Maggie, and I could kill you so much easier than they would."

He kept at it a long time, reasoning with her, and while he reasoned he was looking about him for a stick big enough for the purpose. Despair put some strength back into the little creature. She promised him she would not complain and would not falter, not once, if he would only help her get out of the field.

That a boy of six could believe he was saving his sister from savage barbarity by killing her himself is the whole measure of what the children of the early settlers were told, and grew up believing.

A few more efforts brought them out of the field and into an uninclosed pasture-ground, and there, to their great delight, were cows feeding. The children knew them at once. They belonged to Granny Myers, an old woman who lived at some little distance — though in which direction from where they were now standing, neither of them had the least idea.

The boy worked it out with a sense beyond his years. "Let us hide ourselves till sunset," he said, "when the cows will go home, and we will follow them."

They did. The cows took them home at sunset, and to their dismay the house stood deserted. Granny Myers had been called down the valley on some business and did not come back that night.

They were too tired and too hungry to go anywhere else. They tried to get milk out of the cows and got almost none, and then they lay down to sleep under an old bedstead standing behind the house. In the night their father and his party gave them one more terror. Men were shouting and calling, and the children, who could not tell friends from foes, took the noise for the whoop of Indians and pressed themselves together as far out of sight as they could get. The shouts had only been meant to rouse the people of the house. In the morning, when he found them, they were debating between themselves what course to take next for safety.
