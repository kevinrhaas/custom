Christmas and New Year brought our Ho-chunk friends back to the Portage. They had learned something of the holidays from their French neighbors, and I had been warned in advance that I would see the women kissing every white man they met. I did not entirely believe that. I did believe the rest of it — that each of them would expect a present, a compliment of the season — so I laid in a supply of beads, ribbons, combs, and whatever other small bright things I had. They were fond of dainties, all of them. The day before, I had a great quantity of crullers and doughnuts fried, so there would be something ready in the house that they would actually want.

The frying had been left to Louisa. When the cakes came up to be put away in the "Davis," only a moderate share of them were there, which surprised and annoyed me.

"Where are the rest of the cakes, Louisa?"

"That great fellow, Hancock, came in with the fatigue-party to fill the water-barrels, and while I had just stepped into the store-room to get some more flour, he carried off all I had got cooked."

She made a face and whined it out, as if she had not herself treated every soldier who ever set foot on the premises.

Early the next morning I had quite a levee of Ho-tshung-rah matrons. They came in one after another and seated themselves in a circle on the floor, and I was sorry to see that soap and water had formed no part of their holiday preparation where their blankets were concerned. There was no one to interpret. I thought I would open the talk in a language they would understand, so I took a china dish from the sideboard, filled it with the brown crullers, grated a good quantity of white sugar over them as one does, and handed it to the first woman in the circle. She took the dish out of my hand, tipped every cake into the corner of her blanket, and gave the dish back to me empty. She must be a very hungry woman, I thought. I will manage better next time. I filled the dish again and went to the second, keeping fast hold of the rim so that she could take one modestly. She scooped the whole of it out with both hands and put the cakes away in her blanket like the first. My sense of politeness rebelled at doling them out one at a time the way you do with children, so I sat down to think, because the supply would clearly not stretch to that kind of demand, and there would be more visitors before long.

While I sat there at a loss, the ones who had the cakes began sharing them out, and the whole number was divided fairly around the circle. But they did not eat them. They ran their fingers over the grated sugar, looked into one another's faces, and muttered low. There was something here they did not understand. At last one, bolder than the rest, wet her finger, picked up a few grains, and put it carefully to her mouth.

"Tah-nee-zhoo-rah!" — Sugar! — she said, delighted, and the whole room broke into a hearty laugh. The cakes went after that as fast as good breeding allowed. They had never seen any sugar but the brown or yellow maple. They had taken the white for salt, and that was why not one of them would taste it.

They stayed on and on, until Shaw-nee-aw-kee at last made his appearance, and then, having been made happy by their various gifts, they all took their leave together.
