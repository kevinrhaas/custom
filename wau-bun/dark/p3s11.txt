Permission did come, in time, to build a house for the blacksmith — meaning the man the Government kept in pay at this station to mend the guns and traps of the Indians.

It happened to be the luckiest thing possible for us that Monsieur Isidore Morrin was a bachelor, and perfectly content to go on boarding with his friend Louis Frum, dit Manaigre. So once the new house was genuinely under way, we laid it out and drove it forward strictly on our own account.

It was no palace. A parlor and two bedrooms on the ground floor, two low chambers up under the roof, a kitchen at the back. Set beside the rambling stable of a building we were living in then, it might as well have been one.

Before it was finished, word reached Mr. Kinzie that the money for the annual Indian payment was waiting for him in Detroit — his to take charge of, his to see safely carried to the Portage — and he had to leave at once.

The workmen brought up from the Mississippi to put up the main building were good enough to go on without anyone standing over them. The kitchen, though, was the Frenchmen's job, and nobody could say how that would get done with the bourgeois gone.

"You will have to content yourselves in the old quarters until my return," said my husband, "and then we will soon have things in order."

His trip would be a long, slow business. The Government did not move by railroad and telegraph in those days.

After he was gone I said to the men, "Come, you have all your logs cut and hauled — the squaws have brought the bark for the roof — what is to prevent our finishing the house and getting all moved and settled to surprise Monsieur John on his return?"

"Ah! to be sure, Madame John," said Plante, who always did the talking, "provided the one who plants a green bough on the chimney-top is to have a treat."

"Certainly. All hands fall to work, and see who will win the treat."

On the strength of a prize for whoever laid the last stroke on the building, Plante, Pillon, and Manaigre — whom the waggish Plante insisted on calling "mon nègre," always at a moment when he was safely out of arm's reach — went at it hard.

Building a log house is a strange process to watch. The logs go up one on another, jointed at the corners, until the walls stand as high as they are meant to stand. The chimney starts as four poles of the right length, laced together with a wicker-work of small branches. Someone digs a pit close by and works clay and water into a kind of mortar. Big wisps of hay are loaded with the thick stuff and shaped by hand into what are called, in the trade, clay cats, and these get packed into the frame of the chimney until there is not a chink left anywhere in it. Then the whole thing is smoothed over with a coat of wet clay, and that is what they call plastering.

Between the logs of the walls, small bits of wood are driven in close together — that is chinking — and once it is done the clay cats go in after them and are smoothed down with the plaster. When it has all dried, the walls and the chimney are whitewashed, and the result looks tidy and comfortable, and nothing at all like the pit it came out of.

The roof is thick sheets of bark laid over the transverse logs. Around the chimney, where rain does its worst, we were careful to have a few layers of palisade put down — the ones left over when Mr. Peach, an odd little itinerant genius, had fenced in our garden, which was the pride and the wonder of every settlement and wigwam for miles around.
