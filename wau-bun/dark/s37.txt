We followed the line of the inclosure down the far slope and found a cluster of log cabins at the bottom of it — low, shabby, nothing about them to raise anybody's hopes. They were also shelter, and the storm was coming down hard on us.

"Whose cabins are these?" Mr. Kinzie asked a man who was splitting wood at one of the doors.

"Hamilton's," the man said, and he came straight over to help us down, hospitality being a thing nobody in that wild country stops to consider.

We were put into the best-looking of the buildings. A big fire was going in the clay chimney and the room held a genial warmth, though there were gaps of several inches beside the doors and the windows. A woman got up from a seat beside a sort of bread-trough that was doing duty as a cradle for a fine, fat baby. She wore a tidy calico dress and a shabby black silk cap trimmed with lace shabbier than the cap. She made us room at the fire. She did not offer to take my wrappings and defences off me, heavy as they now were with snow — whether she was too timid for it or simply did not know, I could not tell.

I contrived, with my husband's help, to get free of them, and when he had seen me comfortably settled and in a fair way to thaw out after that freezing ride, he left me and went to see after his men and his horses.

He was gone a long while. I assumed he would bring our host back with him; instead he came back alone, and laughing. Mr. Hamilton, it seemed, could not bring himself to appear before me. He did not want somebody who had known his family in the East to see the life he was living now. Whatever the feeling was, it passed, because he came in before dinner and was introduced to me, and he was every bit as agreeable and as polite as a son of Alexander Hamilton naturally would be.

The housekeeper — she was married to one of the miners — got us a dinner, plain and comfortable, and a table was laid out as long as the cabin's dimensions would allow. The end of it nearest the fire had the nicer furniture on it and the more delicate fare. The rest of it did not.

A horn was blown. That brought in miners, ten or twelve of them, and they took the places below ours. I have never in my life seen a rougher-looking set of men, and they spoke as uncouthly as they looked. Deer-skin, all of it — hunting-shirts, trowsers, moccasins — the shirts fringed along the seams with more of the same, and every man wearing a colored belt at the waist with a large hunting-knife stuck through it. Each of them had the look of a brigand.

They called Mr. Hamilton, so far their superior as he was, "Uncle Billy" — all of them, uniformly. I could not help fancying there was something desperate in them that had to be propitiated by that kind of familiarity. What one of them said afterward did nothing to argue me out of it. He hung back when the rest of the gang had taken themselves off. He had heard we came from Fort Winnebago, and he told us "he was a discharged soldier, and would like to make some inquiries about his old station and comrades," and then sat himself down without being asked and started putting questions to us.

He spoke of his former officers with a bitterness that settled it for me: the man was a deserter. I rather suspected, too, that he had run from the service to escape a punishment. His face twisted out of shape when he came to Captain H., whose company he had belonged to. "There is a man in the mines," he said, "who has been in his hands, and if he ever gets a chance to come within shot of him, I guess the captain will remember it. He knows well enough he darsn't set his foot in the diggings. And there's T. isn't much better. Everybody thought it a great pity that fellow's gun snapped when he so nearly had him at Green Bay."

Having got all of that off his chest, he marched out, and I was more relieved than I can well express.

Mr. Hamilton spent most of the afternoon with us. The storm went on so violently outside that travelling any farther was out of the question. He told us a great many pleasant anecdotes and reminiscences — his early life in New York, and what had befallen him since he came out into the Western wilderness — and when business obliged him to leave us for a while he furnished us with books to pass the time. The most interesting of them was the biography of his father.

If that illustrious man could have foreseen the scene in which his book would one day be perused — the home of his own son — what would his sensations have been?

The most amusing part of it was still ahead of us. All through the late afternoon I had been turning over the question of where we were to be put for the night. Our landlady, pale and melancholy, and her fat baby were plainly the only specimens of the feminine gender anywhere about that establishment, so there was little reason to hope that any of the other cabins held the wherewithal for a comfortable lodging; and this one, so far as the eye could tell, offered nothing of the sort but two beds standing against the farther wall, neither of them curtained. In time the question answered itself. I watched the hostess run a cord between the two beds and hang petticoats and spare garments along it by way of a partition. Then she invited us to take one of them.

My whole preparation was to pull the cloak round me and lie down facing the wall. The good people were less ceremonious. At a distance of barely two feet there was no mistaking the sound their clothes made — not "laid aside," but sent flying over the partition-wall that stood between us.

And what we thought about, while we were still awake to think anything at all, was only how thankful we were for so comfortable a lodging after everything the day had put us through. We did not think it long. Our eyes closed and we slept.
