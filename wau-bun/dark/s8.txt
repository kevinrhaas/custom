We could not have picked a worse week to come into Green Bay. A treaty was being made between the United States Government and the Menomonees and Waubanakees, so the place held not only the commissioners and their clerks and their officials, but traders and claimants and travelers and more idlers than anyone could count. Nearly all of them had packed themselves into the one hotel the settlement could offer, a house of fair size standing near the river. We came in off the open boat cold and dripping and walked into the long dining-room, and the sight of what was in it entertained us enormously. People sat in knots wherever there were chairs. Others, newly arrived like ourselves, simply stood, because the seats had run out long before the guests did. The landlord sat through all of it with his hands in his pockets, calm, indifferent, phlegmatic in the way of a Pennsylvania Dutchman.

His wife made up for him. She was fat and capable and never still, trotting from one end of the room to the other, breaking off to scold somebody who, burn his skin, had not done what he was told, and breaking off again to laugh at some joke thrown her way until her whole frame shook with it.

Her welcome to us was warm enough. Her answer was not. We asked, "Can you accommodate us?" and she said, "Not I. I have got twice as many people now as I know what to do with. I have had to turn my own family out of their quarters, what with the commissioners and the lot of folks that has come in upon us."

"What are we to do, then? It is too late and stormy to go up to Shanty-town to seek for lodgings."

"Well, sit you down and take your supper, and we will see what we can do."

And she did. She turned up a little nook for us somewhere in that house, and we took it gladly, well away from all those people.

What separated us from the next room was a thin board partition, and the next room belonged to General Root, of New York, one of the commissioners of the treaty. Our steamer had carried the mail up with us — in those days a rare blessing for settlements that far out — and the General's share of it reached him around bedtime. Every dispatch had to be opened and read before he would consider going to bed. His eyes were weak, so his secretaries did the reading aloud. He was also somewhat deaf, and kept telling the man to raise his voice, and so, through that flimsy division, we heard the contents of the letters and the General's opinions of them whether we wished to or not.

"What is that, Walter? Read that over again."

We coughed. We hemmed. We knocked over several pieces of furniture. Nothing reached them; they were too far into their business to notice anything happening in the world outside it. Had we been politicians, every secret of the working-men's party was ours that night, and there was capital in it for anyone who wanted to make some.
