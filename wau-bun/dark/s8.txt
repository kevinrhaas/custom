We arrived at Green Bay at an unfortunate moment. A treaty was being made between the United States Government and the Menomonees and the Waubanakees, so that not only the commissioners with their clerks and officials, but traders, claimants, travelers, and idlers past counting were on the ground. Most of them were congregated in the only hotel the place afforded. It was a tolerably-sized house near the river-side, and when we came into the long dining-room, cold and dripping from the open boat, we were infinitely amused by the motley assemblage inside it. Groups were seated everywhere. New comers like ourselves stood here and there, because there were not seats enough for all who sought entertainment. The landlord sat calm and indifferent through all of it, his hands in his pockets, exhibiting the full phlegm of a Pennsylvania Dutchman.

His fat, notable spouse was trotting round the room — stopping to scold about somebody who, burn his skin, had fallen short in his duty, then laughing good-humoredly until her sides shook at some witticism addressed to her.

She welcomed us very cordially. But when we asked, "Can you accommodate us?" her reply was, "Not I. I have got twice as many people now as I know what to do with. I have had to turn my own family out of their quarters, what with the commissioners and the lot of folks that has come in upon us."

"What are we to do, then? It is too late and stormy to go up to Shanty-town to seek for lodgings."

"Well, sit you down and take your supper, and we will see what we can do."

And she did actually contrive to find a little nook, in which we were glad enough to take refuge from the multitudes around us.

A slight board partition was all that separated us from the apartment occupied by General Root of New York, one of the commissioners of the treaty. The steamer we had come on had brought the mail, in those days a rare blessing to the distant settlements. All the dispatches the General received had to be opened and read through before he could retire to rest, and he received them about bed-time. His eyes were weak, so his secretaries were employed to read the communications to him. He was also a little deaf. Through that slight division between the two apartments the contents of the letters and his comments on them were unpleasantly audible, as he kept admonishing his secretary to raise his voice.

"What is that, Walter? Read that over again."

We coughed. We hemmed. We knocked over sundry pieces of furniture. It was no use — they were too deeply interested to hear anything that passed around them, and if we had been politicians we should have had all the secrets of the working-men's party at our disposal, out of which to have made capital.
