Agathe's father went by the name Rascal Day-kau-ray. Whether the name was deserved is a thing the future will have to decide. He was a brother of the grand old chief who carried that name, and about as different from him as two men out of one blood can contrive to be.

The Day-kau-rays were a strikingly handsome family, and this daughter of theirs was the finest of them to look at — tall, strongly and well made, with a round sweet face and the low, musical voice the women of her people have. A stranger would have turned to look at her. What held you, though, was the sadness that sat in her face, which made you want, against your will, to know how it had come there. Mrs. Paquette was her friend, and it was from Mrs. Paquette that I learned it, and what she told me was a touching thing indeed.

One of the young officers at the Fort had noticed her, and had fixed on her — I will not say his heart, since there is real doubt he owned one — but his mind. He went to Paquette and asked him to arrange what he was pleased to call a marriage. It shames me to write that Paquette agreed to it. He understood precisely what it meant to carry lies to Agathe's family. He understood precisely what he was about to do to her.

She was already promised. There was a young man of her own people, and, as almost always happens, the feeling ran deep on both sides. These are people whose thoughts do not range far past the business of the day, and so what they love and what they hate become the very threads their character is woven from. Their feelings are fierce because they have so few things to spend them on. Family is the whole of it, and for the women it is everything they have.

Marriage among them is sacred and cannot be undone. I have read, in a report made not long ago to the Historical Society of Wisconsin, that in earlier days a temporary marriage between a white man and a Menomonee woman was common enough and carried no disgrace with it. My fear is that if anyone went back and examined those cases closely, he would find deceit and misrepresentation laid on top of every other sin in the business, and would find the woman to have been a victim of the arrangement and not a willing party to it.

Whatever was true elsewhere, nothing of the kind exists among the Winnebagoes. The strictness with which their women hold to propriety is one of the marked things about them. A woman who breaks it is spoken of as having "forgotten herself," and her friends will cast her off and "forget" her in turn.

So when the marriage was proposed between the young officer and Day-kau-ray's daughter, it was understood by everyone concerned to be a true one and a permanent one. The father would never have laid himself open to the scorn of his entire nation by selling his child to be some man's mistress. The Day-kau-rays, as I have mentioned before, took no small pride in the thread of French blood that ran, far back, into the native stream in their veins; and I imagine that when Agathe's father consented, the honor he believed he was gaining weighed with him quite as much as the valuable presents that came with the offer.

However that may be, the girl was taken away from the man she loved, out of her father's lodge, and installed in the young officer's quarters.

There were no ladies at the garrison in those days. Had there been even one, I doubt the thing would have been attempted. Set a man down far out in the wilderness, with nothing around him of religion or of decent society to hold him to his shape, and it is no great wonder if his sense of right goes dark in him, and he makes the old choice: "Evil, be thou my good!"

Then came the second act, worse than the first for sheer cruelty. The officer left the post on furlough — that was his word for it — and he did not come back. In time the news arrived that he had married, and when he rejoined his regiment he did it somewhere else.

The "woe pronounced against him" came from more than one tongue, and there was nothing strange in the strength of it. "He will never," my informant said, "dare show himself in this country again! Not an Indian who knows the Day-kau-rays but would take his life if he should meet him!"
