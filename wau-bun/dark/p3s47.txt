Agathe was the daughter of an Indian who went by the name of Rascal Day-kau-ray. Whether he had earned the name is a thing the reader may judge for himself before the end of this. He was brother to the grand old chief of that name, and as unlike him as two men of one blood can be.

The Day-kau-rays were a very handsome family, and this daughter was remarkable even among them. She was tall, and well made, with a round, sweet face and the peculiarly soft, melodious voice that belongs to the women of her people — enough by itself to make a stranger look twice. But it was the pensive set of her face that drew you, and made you want to know what had put it there. I had the history from her friend Mrs. Paquette, and it was a touching one.

A young officer at the Fort had seen her, and had set — I will not say his heart, it may be doubted whether he had one — but his mind upon her. He went to Paquette and asked him to negotiate what he was pleased to call a marriage with her. I am sorry to write that Paquette allowed himself to be brought into the scheme. He knew exactly what he was doing when he carried false representations to Agathe's family. He knew exactly what he was bringing down on her.

The girl was already betrothed, to a young man of her own people, and, as is generally the way, the attachment on both sides was very strong. Among these simple people, who have few subjects of thought or speculation outside the interests of their daily life, their affections and their animosities make up the warp and woof of what they are. All their feelings run intense because they are concentrated on so few objects. With the women particularly, the ties of family take up the whole amount of their sensibilities.

The marriage connection among them is sacred and cannot be dissolved. I have read, in a recent report to the Historical Society of Wisconsin, that in former times a temporary marriage between a white man and a Menomonee woman was no uncommon thing, and that such an arrangement brought no scandal with it. I am afraid that if such cases were looked into, a good deal of deceit and misrepresentation would be found added to the other sins of the transaction, and that the woman would turn out to have been a victim in it and not a willing party.

In any case, no system of the kind exists among the Winnebagoes. The strictest sense of female propriety is a distinguishing trait among them. A woman who transgresses it is said to have "forgotten herself," and she is certain to be cast off and forgotten by her friends.

The marriage proposed between the young officer and the daughter of Day-kau-ray was understood on their side to be a true one and a lasting one. The father would never have laid himself open to the contempt of his whole nation by selling his daughter to be any man's mistress. The Day-kau-rays, as I have said elsewhere, were not a little proud of the remote cross of French blood that ran mixed with the aboriginal stream in their veins, and probably, in acceding to the connection, Agathe's father was moved quite as much by the honor he believed it carried as by the amount of valuable presents that came with the overtures made to him.

Be that as it may. The girl was torn from the man she loved and handed over from her father's lodge to the young officer's quarters.

There were no ladies in the garrison at that time. Had there been, the step would hardly have been ventured. Far off in the wilderness, shut away from the wholesome influences of religion and society, what wonder that the moral sense sometimes goes blind, and that a man makes the choice, "Evil, be thou my good!"

The first wrong was followed by a second, worse in its cruelty. The young officer left the post on furlough, as he said. He never came back. In time the news arrived that he was married, and when he rejoined his regiment it was at another post.

There was a natural feeling behind the strength of the woe pronounced against him, and by more tongues than one. "He will never," said my informant, "dare show himself in this country again! Not an Indian who knows the Day-kau-rays but would take his life if he should meet him!"
