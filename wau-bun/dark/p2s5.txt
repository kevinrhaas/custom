The people who lived outside the walls — a few discharged soldiers, some families of half-breeds — moved into the Agency House and dug themselves in. It stood west of the fort, between the pickets and the river, about twenty rods off.

It was an old-fashioned log building with a hall running straight through the center and one large room on either side. Piazzas ran the whole length of it, front and rear. These they planked up for greater security, cut port-holes through the planking, and posted sentinels there at night.

The enemy was believed to be lurking still in the neighborhood, and if not, then likely to come back at any moment, having found the work easy the first time. So an order went out. No soldier and no citizen was to leave the vicinity of the garrison without a guard.

One night a sergeant and a private walking patrol came around into the pasture adjoining the esplanade and found themselves among a party of Indians. The sergeant fired his piece and both men ran for the fort. They had not reached it when one of the Indians threw his tomahawk. It missed the sergeant and struck a wagon standing near. The sentinel in the block-house fired at once, and to effect, and the two men got safely in. In the morning they read what had happened in the grass: blood, a long way out into the prairie, and a place where the stalks were pressed flat and a body had been laid.

Another time they came into the esplanade after horses. The stable had no horses in it. Rather than go away with nothing, they stabbed every sheep inside and turned them loose. The animals bolted toward the fort, and that is what raised the alarm. The garrison was aroused, parties were sent out after them, and the marauders got clean away, unmolested.

After that the fort was left alone for many weeks.
