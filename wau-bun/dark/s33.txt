Early in January the snow came down and did not stop. We had an unusual quantity of it at the Portage. In the diggings, as the lead-mining country was called, it lay at a depth nobody had heard of before — five or six feet on the level.

The officers had sent an express to Chicago to carry our letters down and bring the mail back. The messenger was a soldier named Sulky, a tough, hardy man who had always made light of the distance and of the pack on his shoulders, and who went the whole way on foot. Sulky had now been gone some weeks. We had given him up altogether, and settled it privately among ourselves that he had died somewhere out there of cold and hunger.

Then, at last, he came in. He was nearly blind from traveling in the snow. He had lain by three weeks in an Indian lodge because the snow lay too deep to let him move a step, and the account he gave of the country between here and there finished the hopes I had been quietly building of getting down to see our friends at Chicago that winter.

We had been laying plans for it before the last heavy fall. Captain Harney had kindly begun putting together some trains — boxes set on sledges — which lined with buffalo-skins would have made a very comfortable sort of vehicle, and I was still inclined to think that a good deep bed of snow over the whole country was no great obstacle to a sleigh-ride. The commanding officer ended the discussion. He had been violently against the scheme from the first, and he stated plainly that he would order the sentinels to fire on us if we tried to leave the fort. The majority was against us. We gave it up.

Sweet, lovely little Lizzie Twiggs arrived before January was quite out, and there was light and joy in two houses at once. She seemed to belong to all of us. As she grew in size and beauty it was hard to say which of us was proudest of her. Whatever slow hours we might have had before, we had none now — she was the pet, the darling, the joint property of both households.

Any regret I had felt about leaving my friend for the three weeks we meant to allow ourselves in Chicago went away with that. She would hardly miss me now. We could hold ourselves ready to move on the first break in the weather and carry out the plan at last.

Through the last part of February the cold slackened. The snow melted off, and by the beginning of March the weather had turned so warm and genial that we were confident of making the trip on horseback without any serious trouble.

Once the thing was settled, the first question was warm clothing. A riding-habit of stout broadcloth was pronounced indispensable to my equipment. I did not own one. Nothing in my wedding traveling gear came anywhere near answering the purpose. The cloth itself could be had in abundance at the sutler's store — the shantee, as everyone called it — but that left the question of who was to turn it into a garment.

The regimental tailor was sent for. He was also cook to one of the companies, and there was some doubt at first whether he could be spared from the spit for the needle as long as I would need him. All his tailoring until then had been done at odd hours on a bench in the company kitchen, and he proposed to carry the riding-habit over there and work on it in the usual way. I pointed out that if I was to superintend the work I should then have to take up my abode in the barracks for the duration, which would be a decided inconvenience.

He got around the difficulty himself, and was in the end so happy as to find a soldier in Company D who consented to officiate in his place at the kitchen fire until his term of service to me should expire.

So picture a little solemn-looking man in his stocking-feet, sitting cross-legged on an Indian mat by my parlor window. He had arranged everything to suit himself, and I judged it wisest not to interfere. The cutting-out was the hard part, and since he had never made a lady's riding-habit in his life, the cutting-out fell to me. I was as complete a novice as he was. I will admit that this first effort of mine was open to criticism. The little tailor did not think so. He was in an ecstasy over our joint performance.

"Upon my word, madam," he would say, standing back to survey it with admiring eyes, "we shall have a very respectable garment!" I could not count how many times he said it in the three days the work took.

I do not believe he had perfect confidence in the culinary powers of his comrade in Company D. Every day, half an hour before the beat of the drum, the work was folded and set aside, the snips gathered up, everything restored to order. Then he would slip out, put on the shoes he had left outside the door in the Turkish manner, and hurry across to the barrack-kitchen to see how matters stood.

Downstairs, in the meantime, great preparations were going forward under our tidy, active little French servant, Mrs. Pillon, the wife of one of the engagés, who had taken the place of the irregular and unmanageable Louisa.

Biscuits were baked. A ham, some tongues, and several pieces of salt pork were boiled. Coffee was roasted and ground, sugar cracked, isinglass cut up into pieces of the size wanted for a pot of coffee. Cotton bags of different sizes had been made up beforehand to hold each of these things, and large sacks of skin, which the Canadians call porches, were provided for the bulkier provisions. It was going to be a long trip.

The distance from Fort Winnebago to Chicago is not formidable if you take the direct route. We knew the direct route was impossible at that season. The road by Kosh-ko-nong was out of the question, since all the Indians were away from their villages in the winter, and with the ice gone there would be no means of getting across the Rock River at that place.

That left one way only: south to Dixon — Ogie's Ferry, as it was called then — the sole certain crossing of that broad, fast water. The detour ran so far out of our true course that we could not hope to do it in under six days, and we laid in our provisions accordingly.

While the wardrobe and the food were being got ready, the other arrangements were settled — who would come with us and what we would ride.

Mr. Kinzie decided to take only two men, Plante and Pierre Roy. Plante was to act as guide, on his own assurance that he knew every mile of the way from the Portage to Ogie's Ferry, and from Ogie's Ferry to Chicago.

The merits of the different saddle-horses were argued over and the most eligible one picked out for my use. For a while we hesitated between Le Gris and Souris, two much-vaunted animals belonging to Paquette, the interpreter. In the end, being governed like most of my sex by a regard for exterior, I chose Le Gris. Souris was given to young Roy. My own little stumpy pony, Brunet, was pronounced just the thing for a pack-saddle. My husband rode his own bay horse, Tom, and Plante, the gayest and proudest of the party, was mounted on a fine large animal named Jerry that had lately been bought for me. With that our cortége was complete.
