My head hurt hard that morning — the boat's motion of the day before, still working in me long after I had stepped off it. I did not want to give in to it. So I let myself be walked past every part of the little settlement that could be shown in the short time we had, and then we went to Mr. Mitchell's.

Mrs. M. received us: very pretty, delicate, French on one side and Sioux on the other, and raised at Prairie du Chien on the Mississippi. At Fort Crawford she had been a great belle among the young officers there — so much so that when the post-surgeon killed himself, people laid it to an attachment he had formed for her and she had not returned. What held me was not that story. It was the softness of her manners, and the way her voice rose and fell as though it were half sung, a music I came to know afterward as the mark of every woman in whom French and native blood ran together.

Another woman was of the company, a lady then staying at the Mission. She would have me lie down on the sofa, and tended my aching head with great kindness. She sat beside me and talked of the new sphere opening in front of me, and then she asked:

"Do you not realize very strongly the entire deprivation of religious privileges you will be obliged to suffer in your distant home?"

"The deprivation," I said, "will Surely be great, but not entire, for I shall have my Prayer-Book, and, though destitute of a church, we need not be without a mode of worship."

In the years after, out in the lonely wilderness and comforted by that precious book, I thought back on that conversation more times than I can count, and blessed God that so long as I kept it I could never be without "religious privileges."

We had not yet left the dinner-table when the little steamer's bell went, calling us aboard. There was no time for a proper leave-taking. We said what we could to all those kind friends, and carried away with us their hearty wishes for a safe and prosperous voyage.

There is hardly a finer thing to look at than Mackinac from the water. The shore let go of us and the whole island arranged itself in one view: the beach sloping down, wigwams scattered along it, canoes drawn up here and there, the odd irregular houses, the white walls of the fort above them, and higher still a farther eminence carrying the remains of old Fort Holmes. Seen whole and complete, it made the outline that had given the place its first name — Mich-i-li-mack-i-nac, the Big Turtle.

And the water under us. Living water, so clear that the fish showed plainly in it, gliding and darting and turning back; drop a thing over the side and you can follow it down fifty or sixty feet to the bottom, a dollar lying on the green floor of the lake and looking no bigger than a half dime. I have never been able to think the enthusiastic lady foolish who cried, "Oh! I could wish to be drowned in these pure, beautiful waters!"

Off the extreme western point of the island my husband turned me toward the northwest and showed me a promontory a long way out. Point St. Ignace, he said, and told me what it was: one of the earliest white settlements anywhere on this continent. The Jesuit missionaries had a church and a school standing there in 1607, the same year that white men settled St. Augustine in Florida, and one year before anyone laid out Jamestown in Virginia.

Of everything those devoted men built, nothing is standing now. What is left of them is the memory of the work, and in most cases nothing but the names of the places where they spent their love on their savage brethren. One building survives: the little French church at Sandwich, across from Detroit, the single witness left of the zeal and self-sacrifice of those pioneers of Christianity.

We passed "Old Mackinac" on the main land, which makes the southern edge of the straits, and then the shore opened and let us out onto the broad water of Lake Michigan. Everyone who travels here knows what was done at that old fort, and so does everyone who has read our history: how in Pontiac's day a game of ball was got up outside the walls, friendly enough to watch, so that the officers and the soldiers came out to see it played; how the ball was tossed, very neatly, over the wall; how the players poured in after it as though that were all they were after, and took possession, and massacred the garrison.

Far to the south the little Indian village of L'Arbre Croche caught the last of the setting sun and glittered. Nothing else on that whole enormous wooded coast gave any sign that a person lived there. Gigantic forest-trees came down to the edge, and here and there a small glade of prairie opened out to the water — ground any farmer would have looked at and wanted, fat with promise — and not one footprint of a man anywhere upon it. We left it to its solitude and laid our course westward across the waters.
