The country gave us seventy miles and nothing at all to look at. The same low ground, the same reeds, the same sky sitting flat on top of it. What sharpened the tedium into something worse was knowing the shape of the alternative: with horses, or a carriage of any kind, we could have gone overland from Gleason's to the Portage through pleasant country in a little more than three hours. Even our one dependable comfort was taken from us. The voyageurs' songs need oars, and the river, deep as it is, runs so narrow that in many places there is no room for the regular play of the oars; and a Frenchman can never keep tune unless his oars can keep time. Lapierre tried. He worked away with a paddle — the little row, he called it — and pitched his voice against the silence, and it would not go. Then there was the wild rice. In places it grows so thick that it closes the water over entirely, and even a moderate-sized boat must be forced through the tangled masses stroke by stroke, the stalks hissing along the hull the whole way. Two days of that, and still the worst of the impatience was ahead of us. It did not arrive until we came in sight of the white walls of Fort Winnebago, looking down from a rising ground over the vast expanse of low land the river winds through.

The Indians have a tradition about this water. A great serpent once lived in the Mississippi, and took a freak to visit the Great Lakes, and dragged his length across the prairies going; and the trail he left behind collected the water off the meadows and the rain out of heaven as it fell, and in time it became the Fox River.

The little lakes strung along its course are probably the places where he thrashed about in his uneasy sleep at night. In the neighborhood of the Portage he must have played all the antics of a kitten. When the fort was first pointed out to me I cried out, delighted, "Oh, we shall be there in half an hour!"

"Not quite so soon," my husband said, smiling. "Wait and see." So we sat and watched. We came up on the very bank where we were to disembark — near enough to distinguish the officers standing on it, and a lady with them, waiting to receive us. Then the boat swung, and we had our backs to them, and we were shooting out into the prairie again. After a while another bank came around, with a range of comfortable-looking log houses on it. "That's the Agency," my husband said. "The largest house belongs to Paquette, the interpreter, and the others are the dwellings of our Frenchmen. The little building, just at the foot of the hill, is the blacksmith's shop, kept there by the Government, that the Indians may have their guns and traps mended free of expense."

"But are we going to stop there?"

"No — do you not see we are going back to the fort?"

And we were. Our course had turned again and we were setting off in our first direction. It went on that way, tacking right and left, putting backward and forward, the walls showing themselves and sliding out of view and showing themselves again, for the better part of two hours, until at last we came in to the little landing, where the assembled party stood ready to greet us.
