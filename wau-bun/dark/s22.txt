Major and Mrs. Twiggs came out to meet us, and with them a few of the younger officers — nearly all the older ones were away — and our brother Robert, who is known throughout the Indian tribes simply as "Bob." The warmth of that welcome is not a thing I can explain to anyone who has not done what we had just done: travelled a long way to a house set down alone in the wilderness. The Major would not hear of our going to "the Agency," which had been the plan. There were empty quarters inside the fort, and he wanted us in them that hour.

"No - we must be under the same roof with them. Mrs. Twiggs had been without a companion of her own sex for more than four months, and would certainly not hear of a separation now. But we must be their guests until the arrival of the boats containing our furniture," which was creeping up from Green Bay in the charge of our old acquaintance Hamilton Arndt.

Dinner was already waiting. That is what the crooked, doubling passage up the Fox River does for you: nobody ever takes their friends by surprise, and dinner can be got ready. The whole circle sat down together, and I do not remember a merrier table.

Afterward Mrs. Twiggs walked me across the wide hall to see the rooms that were to be ours — two large rooms on each of the building's three floors. The front room on the ground floor stood empty. The one behind it was plainly meant for sleeping, since it held a bedstead of such enormous and ungovernable size that Og, the King of Bashan, might have stretched out on it with Mrs. Og and all the little Ogs beside him. We laughed; we could not help it. And the bedstead was the lesser wonder of that room. Something else stood in the second corner.

It had been raised under the personal direction of one of our young lieutenants, and he and the soldiers who built it had emptied out every drop of architectural feeling they possessed. The timbers were grooved and carved. The pillars holding up the front bulged and narrowed and bulged again in the most imaginative way. The doors were not merely panelled; they radiated, in a manner calculated to stun any eye that had never seen better. One of these had been put up in every set of quarters, to make good the want of closets — a want that had not once crossed the minds of the bachelors who drew the plans until it was far too late to fix. Each structure had three compartments, intended, one supposes, for clothes-press, store-room, and china-closet; those, at least, were the uses Mrs. Twiggs had found for hers. The only flaw was in the last of the three, where the shelves were set so close together that a gravy-boat could not be got in between them, though what they lacked in space they certainly made up in number. In honor of the man who conceived it we named the whole production a "Davis," and so laid the first laurel on a brow that would later distinguish itself in Cabinet making of an altogether different sort.

Fort Winnebago stood on a bold shoulder of ground. On one side it looked down over the long reach of prairie and the Fox River; on the other the land ran back into a heavily wooded ridge going off toward Belle Fontaine and Lake Puckaway.

Out in front was meadow, and cut across the meadow ran the Portage road, some two miles of it, joining the Fox and Wisconsin Rivers. The Government kept teams of oxen at the Agency, and a driver with them, for hauling the Indians' canoes over that ground — which through much of the year was soaked and miry and very nearly impossible to cross at all.
