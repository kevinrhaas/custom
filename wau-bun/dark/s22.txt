Major and Mrs. Twiggs came down to meet us with a few of the younger officers — nearly all the older ones were away — and with our brother Robert, who is called Bob through every Indian tribe on the river and by no other name. The welcome was a warm one. How warm, only those can know who have come, as we had, to a remote and isolated home in the wilderness, and found faces at the landing. The Major would not hear of our going to the Agency, as had been proposed. There were vacant quarters in the fort, and he meant us to take possession of them at once.

No, he said, we must be under the same roof with them. Mrs. Twiggs had been more than four months without a companion of her own sex, and she would certainly not hear of a separation now. But we were to be their guests until the boats came up with our furniture, which was working its way slowly from Green Bay under the care of our old acquaintance Hamilton Arndt.

A dinner had been made ready for us. That is one of the advantages of the zigzag approach by the Fox River: a traveler cannot take his friends by surprise if he tried, since they have two hours to watch him come. When the whole circle sat down to the hospitable board, we were as merry a company as I have ever belonged to.

After dinner Mrs. Twiggs took me across the spacious hall and showed me the quarters assigned to us — two large rooms on each of the building's three floors. The front room on the ground floor was empty. The one behind it was plainly meant for the sleeping-apartment, because of the bedstead. It was huge, and unwieldy, and of proportions amply sufficient to have accommodated Og, the King of Bashan, with Mrs. Og and the children into the bargain. We could not repress our laughter. And the bedstead was nothing at all beside the structure standing in the second corner of the room.

That edifice had been raised under the immediate superintendence of one of our young lieutenants, and it was plain to see that he and the soldiers who fabricated it had exhausted every particle of architectural skill between them. The timbers were grooved and carved. The pillars supporting the front swelled in and out in the most fanciful manner. The doors were not merely panelled, they radiated, in a way to excite the admiration of all unsophisticated eyes. A similar piece of workmanship had been erected in every set of quarters, to supply the want of closets — an inconvenience which had never once occurred to the bachelors who planned the building, until it was much too late. The three compartments of each were unquestionably intended for clothes-press, store-room, and china-closet; those, at any rate, were the uses to which Mrs. Twiggs had put hers. There was one slight difficulty. In the last of them the shelves were set so close together that a gravy-boat could not be stood upright between them. What they wanted in space they made up in number. We christened the whole affair, in honor of its projector, a "Davis" — laying the first laurel on the brow of one who was afterward to signalize himself in Cabinet making of quite a different character.

The promontory Fort Winnebago stands on is a bold one. On one side it looks down over the extended prairie and the Fox River. On the other it runs away into the thickly wooded ridge that leads off to Belle Fontaine and Lake Puckaway.

In front lies an open stretch of meadow, and across it the Portage road, about two miles long, running between the Fox and the Wisconsin. The Government keeps teams of oxen and a driver at the Agency to haul the canoes of the Indians over that ground, which at many seasons is wet, and miry, and very nearly impassable.
