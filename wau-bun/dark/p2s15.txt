"He carried me toward the lake, struggling and resisting the whole way. Fast as I was being hurried along, I saw what we passed. The surgeon lay dead on the ground, on the exact spot where my eyes had last found him living, and some tomahawk had put him there.

"Then I was in the lake. A hand held me fast there, and I struggled against it, and could not move it. What I understood after a moment was that the hand was not trying to drown me. It had me fixed in a particular way, and the way it had me kept my head clear of the water. That steadied me enough to look at the man who held me, and once I looked I knew him, paint and all: The Black Partridge.

"The firing thinned out, and then he lifted me from the water and walked me up the sand-banks. It was August, and the morning was already burning. Wet through as I was, that climb across loose sand was more painful and more exhausting than I can put into words. My shoes had packed nearly solid with sand, and I bent down and pulled them off to empty them, and a squaw came and took them out of my hands and carried them away, and I went on barefoot.

"On the prairie my father met me and told me that my husband was alive and only slightly hurt. They brought me back gently toward the Chicago River. The Pottowattamie camp lay along its southern bank. Somebody put me on a horse with no saddle, but the motion of it was more than I could bear, and I threw myself off. So I went on foot instead, held up on one side by my kind conductor, Black Partridge, and on the other by an Indian named Pee-so-tum, who carried something swinging from his hand. It was a scalp. I knew whose it was by the black ribbon tied around the queue: Captain Wells. Fainting, I dragged myself as far as one of the wigwams.

"There was a woman standing near it, the wife of Wau-bee-nee-mah, a chief who had come from the Illinois River. She saw the state I was in. She took up a kettle and went to the stream that ran close by and dipped water, and dropped maple-sugar into it, and stirred it around with her hand, and put it into mine to drink. With everything that was happening around us, that small kindness went straight through me. But I could not hold on to it. There were other things to see.

"Whoever had stayed behind when the troops marched out had turned the fort over to plunder. The cattle, loose and running, had been shot down where they ran, and they lay dead and dying all about. That killing had started just as we were marching out. I remembered then what Ensign Ronan had said while the firing was going on. He turned to me. 'Such,' he said, 'is to be our fate - to be shot down like brutes!'

"The commanding officer heard him. 'Well, sir,' he said, 'are you afraid?'

"'No,' replied the high-spirited young man, 'I can march up to the enemy where you dare not show your face.' What he did afterward proved it was no boast.

"The shooting slackened away to nothing, and the stragglers of the winning side came drifting into camp, and from them I had confirmed what my father had told me in those few hurried words on the lake shore. The whites had surrendered. About two-thirds of their number were gone. The terms, made through the interpreter, Peresh Leclerc, were that the survivors' lives would be spared, and the lives of the women and children who remained, and that they would be delivered at one or another of the British posts unless traders in the Indian country ransomed them first. The wounded, it seems, were held to fall outside that agreement. When they were brought into camp, what followed was horrible.

"There was an old squaw there who had lost friends, or who had simply been worked past herself by the slaughter all around her, and she was possessed by a ferocity I can only call demoniac. She took up a stable-fork and set on one of them where he lay groaning and twisting with his wounds, the sun beating down on him and making them worse. Then Wau-bee-nee-mah did a thing I would not have expected from anyone in such a place. He stretched a mat between two poles and stood it between me and what was happening, so that I was spared most of the sight of it. I could not shut out the sound of the man. In the night that followed, five more wounded prisoners were tomahawked."
