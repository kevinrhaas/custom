"He carried me toward the lake, struggling and resisting the whole way. Fast as I was hurried along, I saw as I passed them the lifeless remains of the unfortunate surgeon. Some tomahawk had stretched him out on the very spot where I had last seen him standing.

"Then I was in the water, plunged in and held under a strong hand, and I fought it. But I soon understood that my captor did not mean to drown me. He was holding me firmly, and holding me in such a way that my head stayed above the water. That steadied me, and I looked at him properly, and under the paint he had disguised himself with I recognized The Black Partridge.

"When the firing had nearly stopped, my preserver lifted me out of the water and took me up the sand-banks. It was a burning August morning and walking through sand in soaked clothes was painful and fatiguing beyond describing. I stopped and took off my shoes to empty out the sand that had nearly filled them, and a squaw seized them and carried them off, and I went on without them.

"When we had gained the prairie my father met me and told me my husband was safe and only slightly wounded. They led me gently back toward the Chicago River, along the southern bank of which was the Pottowattamie encampment. At one point they put me on a horse without a saddle, but the motion was insupportable and I sprang off. So I dragged my fainting steps to one of the wigwams, held up partly by my kind conductor, Black Partridge, and partly by another Indian, Pee-so-tum, who carried a scalp dangling in his hand. I knew it by the black ribbon around the queue. It was Captain Wells's.

"The wife of Wau-bee-nee-mah, a chief from the Illinois River, was standing near, and seeing the state I was in she took up a kettle, dipped some water out of a stream that ran close by, threw in some maple-sugar, stirred it with her hand, and gave it to me to drink. That one act of kindness, in the middle of so many horrors, touched me more than I can say. But my attention was drawn off soon enough to other things.

"The fort had become a scene of plunder for everyone who had stayed behind when the troops marched out. The cattle had been shot down as they ran loose and lay dead and dying all around. That butchery had started just as we were leaving the fort. I remembered very clearly what Ensign Ronan said as the firing of it went on. He turned to me and said, 'Such is to be our fate — to be shot down like brutes!'

"'Well, sir,' said the commanding officer, who had overheard him, 'are you afraid?'

"'No,' the high-spirited young man replied. 'I can march up to the enemy where you dare not show your face.' What he did afterward proved it was no idle boast.

"As the firing died away and the stragglers of the victorious party came dropping in, I had confirmation of what my father had told me hurriedly when we met on the lake shore: the whites had surrendered, after losing about two-thirds of their number. Through the interpreter, Peresh Leclerc, they had stipulated for their own lives and the lives of the remaining women and children, and for delivery at some British post, unless they were ransomed by traders in the Indian country. It appears the wounded prisoners were not considered to be included in that stipulation. A horrid scene followed when they were brought into camp.

"An old squaw, maddened by the loss of friends, or worked up by the sanguinary scenes around her, seemed possessed by a demoniac ferocity. She took a stable-fork and set upon one miserable man who lay groaning and writhing in the agony of his wounds, with the sun beating down on him and making it worse. With a delicacy of feeling one would scarcely expect at such a moment, Wau-bee-nee-mah stretched a mat across two poles between me and it. That spared me some part of the sight of it. It did not shut out the sounds the man made. The following night five more of the wounded prisoners were tomahawked."
