There was a non-commissioned officer in Mr. Kinzie's party who had got there by the strangest road of any of them. When the troops were forming to leave the fort, somebody found that the surgeon's baggage-horses had wandered off. They could not simply be left. Their packs carried part of the surgeon's apparatus and the medicines for the march, so they had to be recovered. The quartermaster-sergeant, Griffith, was sent back to collect them and bring them on.

He had been a long time on the sick report. That was why he had the baggage instead of a place in the ranks. He could not find the horses. He was hurrying to catch up with his party, uneasy at signs of disorder among the Indians and at other signs that looked like hostility, when To-pee-nee-bee met him on the road and took him prisoner.

The chief stripped him of his arms and accoutrements, put him in a canoe, paddled him across the river, and told him to get into the woods and hide himself. He did. The next day, in the afternoon, watching from his hiding-place and seeing that everything had gone quiet, he crept into Ouilmette's garden and got down behind the currant-bushes, and stayed there.

Eventually he decided the house was safer than the garden. He climbed up through a small back window into the room where the family were — and his timing was very nearly the end of him, because it was the same hour the Wabash Indians were leaving Ouilmette's for Mr. Kinzie's. So they took the uniform off him. They dressed him in a suit of deer-skin with a belt, moccasins and a pipe, like a French engagé. His dark complexion and heavy black whiskers did the rest. Everyone in the house was ordered to speak to him only in French, and though he did not know one word of the language, he contrived to pass for a Weem-tee-gosh, and went on in that character with Mr. Kinzie and his family, under his enemies' eyes the whole way, until they were safe.
