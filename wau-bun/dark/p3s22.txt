A death in the village is the signal for a general mourning, and the mourning is loud. It is also — and I am sorry to have to set this down — sometimes the excuse for a general carouse, wherever the means and the appliances for one can be found.

The relatives weep and howl for grief. The friends and acquaintances keep them company out of sympathy, and howl with them. Then a few of their number are appointed to go and wait upon their Father, to tell him what has happened, and to ask him for presents — to help them, as they put it, dry up their tears.

We had such a visit one morning, not long after the payment was concluded.

The dead man was a small drunken Indian whom the French people around had named "Old Boilvin," after an Indian Agent of that name at Prairie du Chien, because they thought he resembled him. He had been fishing, they said, on the shore of one of the little lakes near the Portage. He had taken a little too much whiskee. He had gone into the water and had not come out again. Nothing of him had been found but his blanket, lying on the bank where he had left it. There could be no funeral ceremonies without a body. But his friends were fully prepared to make a great lamentation over him.

Their Father weighed the case and gave them tobacco, knives, calico, and looking-glasses, in about the proportion he judged their grief could reasonably run at the loss of so worthless a vagabond. Then they went away.

A keg of whiskey was got, of course. The prohibitions on that subject were stringent, and it made no difference at all; some of the traders were still lingering about the Portage, and they were obliging. Armed with the keg and with their other treasures, the mourners gathered at an appointed spot, not far from the scene of the catastrophe, and sat down in a ring with the keg in the middle of them, and began their affliction. The more they drank, the more clamorous the grief became. The tears came faster and faster.

In the middle of these demonstrations a small figure came toward them out of the dusk, bent and staggering, covered over with mud, everything about him in disorder, his face full of wonder and sympathy. "Why? what? what?" he said. "Who's dead?"

"Who's dead?" they repeated, looking up at him in astonishment. "Why, you're dead! You were drowned in Swan Lake! Didn't we find your blanket there? Come, sit down and help us mourn."

The old man did not wait to be asked a second time. He took his place among them and cried and drank with the rest, weeping and lamenting as bitterly as any of them; and the strange scene went on as long as they had power to articulate, or any portion of the whiskey was left.
