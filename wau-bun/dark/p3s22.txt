When one of their number dies, the word goes round and the whole camp takes up the mourning and the lamentation. It is also, in some cases — and I am sorry to have to set this down — the excuse for a general carouse, wherever the means and appliances for one can be laid hold of.

The relatives weep and howl in their grief, and the friends and acquaintances sit with them and keep them company out of sympathy. A few of them are then chosen to go to their Father, carry him the news, and ask him for some presents "to help them," as they put it, "dry up their tears."

One morning, not long after the payment was concluded, that delegation came to us.

The dead man was a drunken little Indian whom the French people around the place called "Old Boilvin," because he looked like the Indian Agent of that name down at Prairie du Chien, and it was his death the application rested on. "He had been fishing," they said, "on the shores of one of the little lakes near the Portage, and, having taken a little too much ' whiskee,' had fallen into the water and been drowned." Nothing of him had turned up but the blanket left on the bank, which ruled out any funeral ceremonies; it did not, however, rule out a great lamentation, and his friends were ready with one.

Their Father handed over tobacco, knives, calico, and looking-glasses, measured against what he judged a reasonable quantity of grief for the loss of so worthless a vagabond, and they went away.

Whiskey was forbidden in the most stringent terms, and a keg of it was still no trouble at all to come by from one or another of the traders who had not yet gone. With the keg, and with the rest of their treasures, they gathered at an agreed spot not far from where the catastrophe had happened, set the keg down in the middle of them, and began being afflicted. The deeper they went into the keg, the louder their grief became, and the faster the tears ran.

In the thick of all this a small figure came toward them, bent over and staggering, caked with mud, his clothes every which way, his face full of wonder and sympathy. He began, — "Why? what? what? Who's dead?"

"Who's dead?" repeated they, looking up in astonishment. "Why, you're dead! you were drowned in Swan Lake! Didn't we find your blanket there? Come, sit down and help us mourn."

He did not need asking twice. He took a seat among them and cried and drank as the others were doing, mourning himself as bitterly as anyone there, and that strange scene ran on for precisely as long as there was whiskey left in the keg and breath enough in them to shape words.
