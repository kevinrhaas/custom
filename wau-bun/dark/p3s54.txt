Then, at last, word came that "the boats were in sight," and it was a thrilling thing to hear, and a joyful one.

Hundreds of them stood along the bank, waiting for the boats. And how slowly they came — up the bends of that river, crawling across the width of the open prairie, while the people watched. Our own impatience was as bad as theirs, or nearly. Then the first boat touched the shore, and we could hardly keep from laughing out loud, because old Wild-Cat — a good deal less enormous than he used to be — caught up "the Washington Woman" in his arms and hugged her and danced her round in the pure ecstasy of the thing.

Their Father gave them the sign, and every hatchet they had been holding ready so long came down at once. In almost no time at all the barrels were split open, one after another, and emptied of their corn. Even the smallest children filled pans and kettles with it and ran off to the fires that were burning all round us, to parch and cook what they had got.

There was no more destitution after that day. The abundance in front of us was followed at once by the supplies coming in for the Commissary's Department, and our poor children, fed now and with the strength back in them, went off again to their own villages to get their crops ready for the season ahead.
