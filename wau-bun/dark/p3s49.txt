In the first days of February, 1833, my husband and Lieutenant Hunter, with one or two others along, started out for Chicago. The place had grown enough to call itself a town — there may have been fifty people in it — and that meant the owners of "Kinzie's Addition" could no longer put off laying out their lots and cutting streets through the property. The whole of that work was finished on this visit.

They were out on the ground with a surveyor when my husband noticed a boy: a quick, bright-looking child in Indian dress, hopping along at the elbow of the assistant who carried the chain and calling out after him, in perfect imitation, "Stick!" "stuck!" every time the man did. He asked who the boy belonged to, and the answer took him aback. This was the younger brother of Victoire, Genevieve, and Baptiste, the servants who had been with the family for years. His name was Tomah. He had never in his life worn white clothing — a blanket and leggings were what he had — and he had never lived anywhere but in a wigwam. My husband asked whether he would care to come back with him to Fort Winnebago and learn to be a white boy. Tomah liked the sound of it very much, and his mother agreed to it, and so he was stowed into the wagon along with the two gentlemen and all their traveling gear for the journey home.

He had been fitted out by then with a jacket and trousers and whatever else his new station and character required. They were coming up on the Aux Plaines, near the house of Glode (Claude) Laframboise, and Tomah knew there would be people there who knew him. He asked to be let down to walk a little way. The next the gentlemen saw of him he was a Pottowattamie again, dressed head to foot in the old way — bitter as the weather was, he had put that uncomfortable clothing back on rather than let his old friends see what had been made of him.
