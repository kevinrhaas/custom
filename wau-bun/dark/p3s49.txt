Early in February, 1833, my husband and Lieutenant Hunter, in company with one or two others, set off on a trip to Chicago. The place had grown so much of a town — it may have held fifty inhabitants — that the proprietors of "Kinzie's Addition" found it necessary to lay out lots and open streets through their property. It was all accomplished during that visit.

While they were on the ground with a surveyor, my husband's attention was caught by a very bright-looking boy in Indian dress, who went hopping along beside the assistant carrying the chain and mimicking him as he called out, "Stick!" "stuck!" He asked who the lad was, and learned, to his surprise, that he was the brother of our old family servants Victoire, Genevieve, and Baptiste. Tomah — that was his name — had never worn civilized clothing in his life. He was in blanket and leggings, and had always lived in a wigwam. My husband asked him whether he would like to come to Fort Winnebago and learn to be a white boy. The idea pleased him greatly, and his mother giving her consent to the arrangement, he was packed into a wagon with the two gentlemen and their traveling gear when they set out on the return trip.

Tomah had been fitted out with a jacket and trousers and the other articles of clothing his new sphere and character required. They were coming up on the Aux Plaines, approaching the residence of Glode (Claude) Laframboise, where Tomah knew he would meet acquaintances of his own. He asked leave to get down out of the wagon and walk a little way. When the gentlemen next laid eyes on him he was in full Pottowattamie costume. It was bitter winter weather, and he had put his uncomfortable native garb back on rather than let his old friends see him in a state of transformation.
