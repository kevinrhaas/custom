Michilimackinac — the gem of the Lakes. How bright it looked, and how beautiful, when we walked out the next morning. The rain was gone, and it had left the whole world glittering, with the sun coming up out of the water of Lake Huron far off to the east. The bay lay in front of us, still not quite settled after the storm, and already there were canoes on it, and the fishermen's boats putting out their nets for the trout and the whitefish that are the treasure of that deep. Along the beach stood the wigwams of the Ottawas, who had come over to the island to trade. Their people came out of the lodges to have a look at us. Then somebody knew Shaw-nee-aw-kee, and a shout of welcome went up — he had lived among them seven years, and there was not one of them he was a stranger to.

A hand taken, and an emphatic "Bon-jour - bon-jour": that is the customary greeting between an Indian and a white man.

"Do the Indians speak French?" I asked of my husband.

"No, this is a fashion they have learned of the French traders during many years of intercourse."

The Canadian engagés were every bit as warm. Each of them came trotting up in turn to pay his respects to "Monsieur John" and to deliver a long stream of felicitations in a patois I could make nothing whatever of. I had to take the good wishes rained down upon "Madame John" entirely on faith. All I could catch out of any of it was the hope that I would be happy and contented in my "vie sauvage."
