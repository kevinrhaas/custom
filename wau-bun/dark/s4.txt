Michilimackinac. That gem of the Lakes. How bright and how beautiful it looked when we walked out the following morning. The rain had passed off and left everything glittering in the light of the sun as it came up over the waters of Lake Huron, far away to the east. The lovely bay lay in front of us, not quite tranquil yet after the storm, dotted over with canoes and with the boats of the fishermen, who were already out getting their nets down for the trout and the whitefish, those treasures of the deep. Along the beach were scattered the wigwams, the lodges of the Ottawas who had come to the island to trade. The people inside them came out to look at us. Then a shout of welcome went up, because they had recognized Shaw-nee-aw-kee, who from a seven years' residence among them was well known to every single one of them.

A shake of the hand, and an emphatic "Bon-jour — bon-jour." That is the customary salutation between the Indian and the white man.

"Do the Indians speak French?" I asked my husband.

"No, this is a fashion they have learned of the French traders during many years of intercourse."

The greeting of every Canadian engagé was just as hearty. Each one trotted forward in his turn to pay his respects to Monsieur John and to deliver a long string of felicitations in a patois I could make nothing of. I was obliged to take on faith all the good wishes being showered on Madame John, of which I understood nothing at all except the hope that I should be happy and contented in my vie sauvage.
