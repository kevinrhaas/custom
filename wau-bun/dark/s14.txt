By evening of the first day we reached the Kakalin, where another branch of the Grignon family lived, and they took us in and made much of us. I would rather have had the tent pitched on the bank, with the river running past in the dark and nothing at all between me and the beginning of my forest life. I was impatient to set down the indulgences of civilization all at once, the way you set down a coat you have outgrown. But to refuse a house is a slight, and a slight can turn into an affront, so we did the better thing instead. We ate hot venison steaks and crêpes. We drank that cup of coffee no one on earth prepares like a Frenchwoman, the kind that goes all the way through you after a day in the open air.

The Kakalin is a rapid of the Fox River. It is serious enough that a boat's heavy lading has to be carried around it by land while the boat itself is poled, or dragged on cords, up against the current. It is only the first of a series of rapids and chûtes — falls — strung out between that point and Lake Winnebago, twenty miles above.

The next morning, after breakfast, we took leave of our hosts and got ready to go on. The bourgeois had been up since an early hour, standing over his men while they worked the boat and its loading across the Kakalin. The late rains had left the paths through the woods and along the banks muddy and poor for walking, so I was put into an ox-cart. The road was unequal the whole way. We saluted, impartially, every stump and every stone that lay in it. The only relief from that came whenever the little thick-headed Frenchman conducting us bethought himself to guide his cattle suddenly into a projecting tree or a thorn-bush, to the great detriment of my straw bonnet, and very nearly of my eyes.

But we got through at last, and at the head of the rapids I found the boat lying there, everything stowed and ready for us to re-embark.

Our Menomonee guide came forward to be presented to me. Wish-tay-yun: a fine, stalwart man with an open, good-humored face — roguish, almost, if you watched it long enough.

"Bon-jour, bon-jour, maman," was his laughing salutation. The French no longer surprised me; I had grown accustomed to that. What surprised me was the respectable title he had decided to bestow on me.

"Yes," said my husband, "you must make up your mind to receive a very numerous and well-grown family, consisting of all the Winnebagoes, Pottowattamies, Chippewas, and Ottawas, together with such Sioux, Sacs and Foxes, and Iowas, as have any point to gain in applying to me. By the first-named tribe in virtue of my office, and by the others as a matter of courtesy, I am always addressed as 'father' — you, of course, will be their 'mother.'"

Wish-tay-yun and I were soon good friends, my husband turning the Chippewa he spoke into English for me as it came. The morning was already far advanced and we were impatient to be off. Everything was in readiness. The word was given.

"Pousse au large, mes gens!" (Push out, my men.)

At that moment a boat was seen leaving the opposite bank of the river and making toward us. White men in it. They showed by signs that they wanted us to hold where we were until they could come up. They drew near, and we found them to be Mr. Marsh, a missionary among the Waubanakees, or the New York Indians, lately brought into this country, and the Rev. Eleazar Williams, who was living at that time among his red brethren on the right bank of the Fox River.

To people situated as they were, even more emphatically than to people in the settlements, the arrival of visitors from the "east countrie" is a godsend. We had to give up everything we carried — the news political, the news ecclesiastical, the news social — and then a tolerably detailed account of what we proposed to do, or rather what we hoped we might be able to do, among our native children at the Portage.

For my own part I was obliged to confess that I was almost entirely a stranger to Indian character and habits, and was going among them with no settled plans of any kind. General good-will, and a hope of making them my friends: those were the only principles I could lay claim to at present. In what way the principle could be carried out for their greatest good, I would have to leave to time and a better acquaintance to show me.

Mr. Williams was a dark-complexioned, good-looking man. His relations in Connecticut had always spoken of him as "our Indian cousin," so it never occurred to me to doubt that he belonged to that race, although I think now that if I had met him anywhere else I should have taken him for a Spaniard, or a Mexican. His complexion had decidedly more of the olive in it than the copper. His face was grave, almost melancholy. He was very silent the whole of that interview, asking few questions, and offering no observation of any kind except in reply to something put directly to him.
