The first day's travel put us at the Kakalin by evening, where another branch of the Grignon family had their place. They made us very welcome. I was so impatient to begin my life in the forest that I would sooner have had the tent set up on the river bank and done with the comforts of civilization then and there — but to say so would have been a slight, and perhaps worse than a slight, so we behaved better than that. We sat down to hot venison steaks and crêpes and a cup of that coffee no one on earth makes like a Frenchwoman, which after a whole day out of doors is the most reviving thing there is.

The Kakalin is a rapid in the Fox River, and a serious enough one that the heavy lading has to come out of a boat and go round by land, while the boat itself is poled up, or hauled up on cords, against the water. It is only one of a string of rapids and chûtes, or falls, that lie between that spot and Lake Winnebago, twenty miles further on.

We breakfasted the next morning, took leave of our hosts, and made ready to go on. The bourgeois had been up since an early hour standing over his men while they worked the boat and the cargo past the Kakalin. The rains had left the paths through the woods and along the bank soft and miserable for walking, so I was loaded into an ox-cart instead, and jolted along a road that had never been leveled, meeting every stump and every stone in it without favor. The only relief from that came when the little thick-headed Frenchman driving us took it into his head to swing his cattle hard into an overhanging limb or a thorn-bush, which cost my straw bonnet dearly and very nearly cost me my eyes.

We came through it, though. At the head of the rapids the boat was waiting, loaded and ready for us to get aboard again.

Our Menomonee guide came up to be presented to me — Wish-tay-yun, a fine, strongly built man with an open, good-humored face that had something not far from mischief in it.

"Bon-jour, bon-jour, maman," he said, laughing. The French no longer surprised me; I had grown used to that. What surprised me was the respectable title he had decided to give me.

"Yes," my husband said, "you must make up your mind to receive a very numerous and well-grown family, consisting of all the Winnebagoes, Pottowattamies, Chippewas, and Ottawas, together with such Sioux, Sacs and Foxes, and Iowas, as have any point to gain in applying to me. By the first-named tribe in virtue of my office, and by the others as a matter of courtesy, I am always addressed as 'father' - you, of course, will be their 'mother.'"

Wish-tay-yun and I were friends in no time, my husband turning his Chippewa into English for me as he spoke. The morning was well along by then and we were all impatient to be moving, and everything being in order, the word came:

"Pousse au large, mes gens!" (Push out, my men).

Just then a boat pulled off from the far bank and came at us across the water. There were white men in it, and they were making signs for us to hold where we were until they could reach us. When they came alongside we found it was Mr. Marsh, a missionary to the Waubanakees, or New York Indians, who had lately been brought into this country, and with him the Rev. Eleazar Williams, who was living at that time among his red brethren on the right bank of the Fox River.

For men placed as they were — even more than for people in the settlements — the appearance of anyone out of the "east countrie" is nothing short of a godsend. We had to hand over every scrap of news we had carried in with us, political, ecclesiastical, and social, and then give a fairly full account of what we meant to do, or rather what we hoped we might be able to do, among our native children at the Portage.

For my own part I had to admit that I knew next to nothing of the Indian character or the Indian way of living, and that I was going among them with no plan whatever. Good-will in general, and a hope that they would come to be my friends — that was the whole of what I could claim to be carrying. What shape that hope ought to take, and how it might be turned to their greatest good, I would have to let time and better acquaintance teach me.

Mr. Williams was a dark-complexioned man and a good-looking one. His relations in Connecticut had always spoken of him as "our Indian cousin," so it never entered my head to question that he belonged to that race — though I think now that if I had come across him anywhere else I should have supposed him a Spaniard or a Mexican. There was more of the olive in him than the copper, and his face was grave, very nearly a sorrowful face. He said hardly anything the whole time we talked. He asked few questions and put forward no remark of his own, except when someone had asked him something directly.
