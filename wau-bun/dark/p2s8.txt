The Indians grew more unruly by the day. They came into the fort past the sentinels as though the sentinels were not there, and walked into the officers' quarters without ceremony. One of them picked up a rifle and fired it in the parlor of the commanding officer, for no reason but to show that he could. Some thought the shot was meant as a signal to the young men to begin. The old chiefs moved back and forth among the gathered groups in what looked like the liveliest agitation, and the squaws went to and fro in great excitement, plainly prepared for some fearful scene.

Nothing came of it that day. The ill feeling was suppressed for the present. And Captain Heald — strange as it is to write — went on holding the conviction that he had created so amicable a disposition among the Indians as would carry his command safely to Fort Wayne.

So the days went by until the 12th of August. What the people inside the fort felt in that time is better imagined than described. Every dawn seemed to bring them one day closer to the same appalling end, butchery by a savage foe, and at night they hardly dared let themselves sleep, in case they woke to the war-whoop and the tomahawk. Gloom and mistrust settled over everything. And because the officers were not of one mind, they were denied even the consolation of sympathizing with one another.

The Indians came in from the neighboring villages, and a council was held with them on the afternoon of the 12th. Captain Heald attended it alone on the part of the military. He had asked his officers to come with him and they had declined. They had been told, secretly, that the young chiefs meant to fall on the officers and massacre them in the middle of the council, and they could not persuade Captain Heald that the information was true. So they waited only until he had walked out of the garrison with Mr. Kinzie beside him. Then they took command of the block-houses that overlooked the esplanade where the council was being held, opened the port-holes, and laid the cannon so that they covered the whole assembly, every man in it. That is probably the reason the whites who sat in that council are alive to be spoken of.

In council, the commanding officer told the Indians that the next day he would distribute among them not only the goods lodged in the United States' factory, but the ammunition and the provisions too, of which the garrison had plenty. Then he asked the Pottowattamies for an escort to Fort Wayne, promising them a liberal reward on arrival, over and above the presents they were about to receive. With many professions of friendship and good will, they assented to everything he proposed, and promised everything he required.
