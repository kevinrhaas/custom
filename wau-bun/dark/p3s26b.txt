The next day's stage ran from the Kakalin to the Butte des Morts, where a man named Knaggs lived. It was rough, wild ground, not unlike what we had crossed the spring before between Hamilton's diggings and Kellogg's Grove, but this time we had Wish-tay-yun leading us instead of Uncle Billy, and that meant we could take it at a moderate pace.

Forty miles was all it came to, and still I got down cold and worn out, so that Mrs. Knaggs's snug little room and the fire burning in it were a very welcome sight. We had put nothing in our mouths since morning except a few crackers carried loose in our pockets. I will admit that we fell on her coffee and her cakes without any ceremony whatever, and on the venison-steaks after them, and on the bear's meat, which I had never in my life tasted before.

Supper finished, we began to consider where we were going to lie down. The room we had eaten in was small — a bed, a table shoved against the wall, and the handful of chairs we were sitting on, and that was the whole of it. There was not floor enough left for a shakedown of any description.

"Where can you put us for the night?" asked my husband of Mr. Knaggs, when he made his appearance.

"Why, there is no place that I know of, unless you can camp down in the old building outside."

We walked out to see it. One room, bare, filthy. A great chimney took up most of one side with a few brands still smoking in it. A bunk of sorts, rickety, had been built against another wall, and that was every stick of furniture the place contained. The floor lay thick with mud and dirt, and sitting on it near the fire was an old Indian with a pan of boiled corn in his lap, scooping the corn up in both hands and eating it as fast as he could get it down.

It took us a moment to understand that he was blind. Hearing feet and voices come in at the door, he pulled the pan in tight against himself and started up a low, sour muttering. Then he was told that the man speaking to him was Shaw-nee-aw-kee, and everything in his face let go. He even put the dish out toward us and asked us to eat with him.

"But are we to stay here?" I asked. "Can we not sleep out-of-doors?"

"We have no tent," replied my husband, "and the weather is too cold to risk the exposure without one."

"I could sit in a chair all night, by the fire."

"Then you wouldn't be able to ride to Bellefontaine tomorrow."

So that was decided. All the bedding Mr. Knaggs had to offer was one small bear-skin. The boards of the bunk were a shade less foul than the floor, so we laid the skin over them, and our saddle-blankets over the skin, and set a pair of saddle-bags at the head for a bolster. I rolled myself in my cloak and gave myself up to it.

Lying flat after a day in the saddle rested me a little, I suppose. Sleep was out of the question. It would have been out of the question on a far softer bed, because the old Indian snored, and so did the two or three others who had joined him, and every so often he would find himself crowded in the dark and cry out about it in that same querulous voice.

Getting up at first light and making ready to ride was a relief. I never learned where our fellow-travelers had stowed themselves, though it was plain they had done no better than we had. They were in high spirits regardless. We ate our breakfast in good humor and were ferried across the river, and picked up the trail on the far side toward Bellefontaine, twelve miles out from Fort Winnebago.
