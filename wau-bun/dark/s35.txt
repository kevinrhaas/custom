March 9th. - Our route that day took us by the first of the Four Lakes, and an encampment of Winnebagoes lay scattered along the bank of it. They set up a loud and joyful noise at the sight of their Father - "Bon-jour, bon-jour, Shaw-nee-aw-kee," "Hee-nee-kar-ray-kay-noo?" (how do you do?)

Then came the announcement we could always count upon, "Wys-kap-rah tshoonsh-koo-nee-noh!" (I have no bread.)

That is their way of asking. We were in no position to be generous. If our own stores gave out there was no saying where more would be found, and that uncertainty held us to the strictest economy.

But the encampment in the morning sun was a beautiful thing. The matted lodges, with blue smoke curling off the tops of them. The trees and the bushes powdered over by the light snow that had come down in the night. The lake almost at our feet, shining, the whole surface of it sparkling. Even the Indians, in their own peculiar costume, belonged to the picture and made it better.

I was sorry to leave, and we left in haste, because Souris, the pack-horse, had taken our talk with our red friends as his opportunity and decamped. He had at least had the sense to keep to the route in the right direction, so a short race brought us up with him. Young Roy, whose particular charge he was, gave him a great deal of scolding and a few blows besides, and then he was set in the middle of the cavalcade and made to ride there, disgraced, for having broken his duty.

Beyond the lake our road ran over a "rolling prairie," bare and desolate enough at that season. Snow lay in the hollows, thawed part of the way through, so that a horse could never be certain of his footing, and I could not help laughing along with our Frenchmen, whose laughter rang out, when Brunet and Souris, the two ponies, went floundering nearly to their bodies through the yielding mass. Even Plante, who was vainglorious about his horsemanship and prided himself on it, came once or twice within an inch of being unhorsed, having chosen his road badly. Here and there the rises carried a thicket or a copse, and out of these the dogs would generally start a deer, or more than one. That first bound - the "lope" - was the signal, and the chase was on. The horses caught the spirit of the thing as one "halloo" answered another, but we never had the good fortune of a shot. Once or twice the dogs came up with what they were after, and had not the strength to hold it. We came up to the Blue Mound somewhere near the middle of the afternoon. I rejoiced to have got even that far. I was fatigued past telling, and by then a single mile was doing the work of two. The miles in this country are unconscionably long. Then I was told that seven of them still lay between us and "Morrison's," where we meant to stop for the night, and I was nearly in despair. It was my first journey on horseback, and I was not yet inured to the exercise.

When we got to Morrison's my husband reached up to lift me down out of the saddle, and I was so far spent that I fell into his arms.

"This will never do," he said. "tomorrow we must turn our faces toward Fort Winnebago again."

Their door stood open to us in the kindest way. Two women received us there — one of them with a face as sweet and benignant as any I have seen, and beside her another, younger by some years. The first of them was Mrs. Morrison. The younger was Miss Elizabeth Dodge, whose father is General Dodge.

My husband carried me to a small bed that stood in the room where the two had been sitting over their work. They got off my bonnet and my riding-dress, chafed my hands, and made me warm wine and water, and I was soon revived. Half an hour of lying still refreshed me enough that I could talk with the ladies and set my husband's mind at rest on my account. Tea was announced not long afterward, and we crossed to an adjoining building to take it, for Morrison's, like every settler's establishment of that period, was a group of detached log houses, each holding one apartment, or at the most two.

The table groaned under the good cheer set out on it, and it called up for me certain tables I had once sat at among the old-fashioned Dutch residents along the Hudson.

My spirits had come back to me and we made a cheerful party. Mrs. Morrison told us that for her first eighteen months in this country not a single white woman had spoken with her; her husband and two black servant-women were the whole of her society.

A woman from Tennessee had come in just before tea, bringing her little son with her, and the two of them gave us a description to amuse Mr. Kinzie with. Courtesy was the whole of her errand. She was little and dumpy, her complexion burned by the sun to a perfect red, and her hair was an exact tow-color, braided into two tails — one taken up from the front of her forehead, one from the back of her neck — which met on the crown of her head and were held there by a small tin comb. Checkered homespun was her dress, and a "very tight fit" it was; there was neither ruff nor handkerchief at her throat, so that she looked like somebody being got ready for execution. Company overawed her, plainly. She was for departing again the moment she had come; but the boy did not intimidate so easily, and he took no meaning at all from her signs or her pinches until he had worked his way over beside Mrs. Morrison and, tugging that old hat of his down farther across his eyes, begged a whang from her — meaning by it a narrow strip of deer-skin. She very obligingly got a large smoked skin out of its receptacle and cut him one, and mother and son took themselves off, smiling, and looking rather scared.

After tea we went back into Mrs. Morrison's parlor, where nothing would content her but that I take the little bed again — to recruit me, as she put it, against the next day's traveling. My husband, meanwhile, had gone off to see how his men and his horses were to be accommodated.

In the talk that followed it came out that Mrs. Morrison had passed a good deal of time near my own recent home in Oneida County; that a number of people I had loved and valued there were her friends as well; and that when word reached her of my arrival at Fort Winnebago she had proposed to travel up and visit me, so as to begin an acquaintance which other means, none of them expected, had gone ahead and begun for us.

We talked on together, long and pleasantly, until the hour had got late, and there was satisfaction on both sides in bringing up old friends and by-gone events for review — all of it edifying to Miss Dodge, and to the gentlemen too, once they had come back and joined us.
