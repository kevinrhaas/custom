March 9th. — That day's ride took us past the first of the Four Lakes. There was an encampment of Winnebagoes scattered along the bank, and they greeted their Father with vociferous joy. "Bon-jour, bon-jour, Shaw-nee-aw-kee!" "Hee-nee-kar-ray-kay-noo?" How do you do?

Then came the usual announcement. "Wys-kap-rah tshoonsh-koo-nee-noh!" I have no bread.

That is their form of begging. We could not afford to be generous. There was no certainty of getting any more if our own gave out, and that obliged us to the strictest economy.

How beautiful the encampment looked in the morning sun. The matted lodges with the blue smoke curling off their tops, the trees and bushes powdered with the light snow that had come down in the night, the lake shining and sparkling almost at our feet — even the Indians in their own costume added to the picture.

I was sorry to leave it, and sorrier to leave it in a hurry, but Souris, the pack-horse, had taken it into his head to decamp while we stood talking with our red friends. He had at least been sensible enough to set off in the right direction, so we had the good fortune to overtake him after a short race. Young Roy, who had particular charge of him, gave him a great deal of scolding and some blows, and then he was put in the middle of the cavalcade as a mark of disgrace for his breach of duty.

After the lake our road ran over rolling prairie, bare and desolate enough now. The hollows were full of snow, and the snow was half thawed, so the footing was never certain, and I could not help joining in the ringing laughter of our Frenchmen when Brunet and Souris, the two ponies, went floundering through the yielding stuff almost up to their bodies. Even the vainglorious Plante, who piqued himself on his horsemanship, was nearly unhorsed once or twice, having chosen his road badly. Now and then the higher ground carried a thicket or a copse, and our dogs would put up a deer or two out of it. The first bound, the first long lope, was the signal for a chase. The horses seemed to catch the spirit of the thing as halloo answered halloo, but we were never fortunate enough to get a shot at one, and though the dogs came up with a deer once or twice they were not strong enough to hold it. It was about the middle of the afternoon when we reached the Blue Mound. I was very glad to have got that far, because I was sadly fatigued by then and every mile now felt like two to me. The miles are unconscionably long in that country. When I was told we still had seven of them to go to Morrison's, where we meant to stop for the night, I was almost in despair. It was my first trip on horseback and I was not yet inured to the exercise.

When we came to Morrison's I was so far spent that as my husband reached up to lift me from the saddle, I fell into his arms.

"This will never do," he said. "Tomorrow we must turn our faces toward Fort Winnebago again."

The door opened hospitably to take us in. We were welcomed by a lady with a most sweet, benignant face, and by a companion some years younger. The first was Mrs. Morrison. The other was Miss Elizabeth Dodge, daughter of General Dodge.

My husband laid me on a small bed in the room where the two ladies had been sitting at their work. They took off my bonnet and my riding-dress, chafed my hands, and made me some warm wine and water, and I soon came round. Half an hour of lying still refreshed me so much that I was able to talk with them and to relieve my husband's mind of all his anxiety on my account. Tea was announced soon after, and we went across to an adjoining building — Morrison's, like every settler's establishment of that period, was a group of detached log houses, each with one room in it, or at the most two.

The table groaned with good cheer, and put me in mind of tables I had seen among the old-fashioned Dutch families on the banks of the Hudson.

My spirits had come back and we made a cheerful party. Mrs. Morrison told us that in her first eighteen months in this country she did not speak with one white woman. Her husband and two black servant-women were the only society she had.

A Tennessee woman had called in with her little boy just before tea, and we amused Mr. Kinzie afterward with a description of the pair. The mother's visit was simply one of courtesy. She was a little dumpy woman, her complexion burned perfectly red by the sun, her hair an exact tow-color, braided up from the forehead in front and from the neck behind, the two tails meeting on the crown of her head and fastened there with a small tin comb. Her dress was checkered homespun and a very tight fit, and as she wore no ruff and no handkerchief at her throat, she looked as if she had just been made ready for execution. She was plainly awestruck at finding visitors in the house and looked ready to leave the moment she got in. The boy was not so easily intimidated. He would not take her signs or her pinches at all, until he had sidled up to Mrs. Morrison and, pulling his old hat down farther over his eyes, begged for a whang — meaning a narrow strip of deer-skin. The lady very obligingly cut him one from a large smoked skin that she brought out of its receptacle, and mother and son took their leave, smiling, and still looking rather scared.

After tea we went back to Mrs. Morrison's parlor, where she kindly insisted that I lie down again on the little bed and recruit myself, as she put it, for the next day's ride. My husband went out in the meantime to see his men and the horses settled.

In the talk that followed I learned that Mrs. Morrison had spent a good deal of time in the neighborhood of my own recent home in Oneida County, that a number of the friends I had loved and valued were her friends too, and that when she heard of my arrival at Fort Winnebago she had thought of coming up there to call on me — to begin an acquaintance which had now been brought about by other and quite unexpected means.

We talked long and pleasantly, and sat up late over it, going back through old friends and by-gone events with great satisfaction on both sides, much to the edification of Miss Dodge, and of the gentlemen when they came in and joined us again.
