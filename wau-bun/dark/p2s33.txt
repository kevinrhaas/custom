The march went on for days, and every day of it cost them something. At the end of it the party came into the Seneca village on the head-waters of the Alleghany, near the place now called Olean Point. The chief who had brought them there was known among his people as the Big White Man. He walked his prisoners up to the principal lodge, which belonged to his mother, widow of the head-chief of that band. They called her the Old Queen.

He brought the little girl forward into his mother's presence and presented her.

"My mother," he said, "I bring you a child to supply the place of my brother, who was killed by the Lenape six moons ago. She shall dwell in my lodge, and be to me a sister. Take the white woman and her children and treat them kindly — our father will give us many horses and guns to buy them back again."

By their father he meant the British Indian Agent of his tribe, Colonel Johnson, a good and benevolent gentleman who lived at Fort Niagara on the British side of the river of that name. The old queen did exactly as her son had told her. She took the prisoners in, and everything her simple and primitive way of living could provide for their comfort, she provided.
