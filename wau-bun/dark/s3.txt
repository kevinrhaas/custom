Mr. and Mrs. Robert Stuart received us with the most affectionate cordiality. We had been expected at their hospitable house for some days.

The repose and the comfort of an asylum like that one can only be truly appreciated by people who have reached it after a tossing and a drenching such as ours had been. There was a bright, warm fire. There were faces beaming with the kindest interest. Between the two of them, every sensation of fatigue and annoyance was gone.

After a season of pleasant talk the servants were assembled, the chapter of God's word was solemnly read, the hymn was chanted, the prayer of praise and thanksgiving was offered, and we were conducted to our place of repose.

It is not my purpose here to attempt a portrait of those noble friends whom I met then for the first time. The honor of writing the biography of Robert Stuart should be assigned to an abler pen than mine. Everyone who has enjoyed the happiness of his acquaintance, or better still a stay under his hospitable roof, will carry the impression of him to their latest hour — his noble bearing, his genial humor, his untiring benevolence, his upright and uncompromising adherence to principle, his ardent philanthropy, his noble disinterestedness. Irving in his Astoria, and Franchere in his Narrative, give many striking traits of his early character, along with events of his history of a thrilling and romantic interest. But both of them have left the most valuable portion unsaid: his after-life, that is, as a Christian gentleman.

Of his beloved partner, who survives him still, mourning on her bereaved and solitary pilgrimage, yet cheered by the memory of her long and useful course as a Mother in Israel, we will say no more than to offer the incense of loving hearts, and prayers for the best blessings from her Father in heaven.
