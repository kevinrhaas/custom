Mr. and Mrs. Robert Stuart took us in with the warmest affection, in a great hospitable house where we had been looked for several days already.

What such a shelter is worth can only be understood by people who reach one as we reached that one, tossed about and soaked through. There was a fire burning bright and hot, and there were faces turned toward us with nothing in them but the kindest concern, and between them every trace of the day's weariness and vexation went out of me.

We talked a good while, pleasantly, and then the servants were called in. The chapter of God's word was read out solemnly; the hymn sung; the prayer of praise and thanksgiving offered; and then we were shown to our beds.

I do not mean to draw those noble friends here, on the night I first met them. The life of Robert Stuart deserves a better pen than mine to write it. Anyone who has known him, and still more anyone who has stayed under that hospitable roof, will carry the impression of the man to their last hour — the bearing of him, the genial humor, the benevolence that never tired, the upright and uncompromising way he held to a principle, the ardent philanthropy, the noble disinterestedness. Irving, in his "Astoria," and Franchere, in his "Narrative," set down a good deal of his early character, along with passages of his history that are thrilling and romantic to read; but the most valuable part is in neither of them, which is what he became afterward, a Christian gentleman.

Of his beloved partner, who is living still, and mourning her way through a bereaved and solitary pilgrimage, cheered by the memory of her long, useful course as a "Mother in Israel," we will say nothing more than to offer her the incense of loving hearts, and our prayers for the best blessings her Father in heaven can send.
