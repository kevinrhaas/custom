Not long after, something happened that put our little community into a state of no small excitement. On a bitterly cold day Robineau came in to the Agency — the striker from the blacksmith establishment at Sugar Creek, over near the Four Lakes. He had come for a doctor. Mâtâ's eldest girl, Sophy, had been sliding on the lake, had gone down hard on the ice, and was badly hurt. Her father was not there to send for anyone himself; he had gone to Prairie du Chien to put his youngest daughter in school. Two or three days had already passed since the fall. A high fever had come up in her, and she was suffering a great deal, and so they had decided to send Robineau to us for advice and aid, and left Turcotte and a friendly Indian woman from a lodge nearby to sit with the girl.

The matter was laid before the commanding officer. He did not think it prudent to let the surgeon go out of the post, but he gave leave of absence very cheerfully to Currie, the hospital steward, a young man who knew something of medicine and surgery both.

Sophy would need experienced nursing, so we engaged Madame Bellaire, whose husband was the Frenchman generally employed as express to Chicago; and to help her and keep her company we added Agathe, a daughter of Day-kau-ray, who lived with Paquette's family.

Of Agathe I shall have more to say in the future.

The cold was severe when the four of them started out for Sugar Creek, some forty miles off. We had put up a good store of rice, crackers, tea and sugar for the sick girl, and this, along with what the party would need on the road, went packed on the horse Robineau had ridden in to the Portage. We looked for them to reach the place on the second day.

Imagine our faces, then, when on the fourth day after they left it was Turcotte who came walking up, wanting to know why Robineau had not yet come back with help for Sophy. There was only one way to read it. Robineau had steered them exactly as well as he had steered the boat at the Grande Chûte the summer before; he had no water to wreck them in this time, so he had simply lost them in the woods or out on the prairie. The one consolation was that starving seemed unlikely — the rice and crackers were several days' worth — and Agathe would be used to this kind of living, and sooner or later they must come across Indians who would bring them back to us.

The days went by and nothing came. Turcotte went back to Sugar Creek carrying comforts and prescriptions for Sophy, and Colonel Cutler sent out a party to search. Of the lost ones it was poor Currie we pitied most, his constitution being a delicate one.

The snow came on and the winds howled, and there was nothing any of us could do but go from one window to the next and back again, watching the distance in hopes that somebody would appear in it. There were no Indians at hand to send after them. By the tenth day we had very nearly given up.

That was when the word came, all at once: "They are found! They are at the Fort!" A party of soldiers out exploring had come upon them at Hastings's Woods, twelve miles from us, working their way home slowly and feebly. They had known they were on the right road at last. They had barely had the strength left to walk it.

Their food had run out two days before. Cold and hunger had brought them to the point of discussing, in earnest, whether to kill the horse and eat him. What stopped them was Currie: he could not go on foot, and none of them could face leaving him in the woods to die.

Agathe had known from the very beginning that the course they were on was wrong. Robineau, obstinate as ever, held to it anyway, and held to it until it brought them out on the Rock River, and then at last he admitted his mistake, and they turned and began working their way back.

Agathe had her hatchet with her, as the custom of her people is. Because of that they had a fire every night and boughs cut to break the storms off them. Without it they must certainly have died out there.

Two things about that journey stirred something in us stronger than sympathy. The first was what the miserable Robineau had done to Currie — demanded of him, first, all his money, and afterward his watch, as the price of leading the party back into the right path, which he swore he knew perfectly well.

The second was what Bellaire did to his kind, excellent wife when she got home. He gave her a hearty flogging "for going off," as he said, "on such a fool's errand."

Bellaire lay outside our jurisdiction. Mons. Robineau did not. He was discharged on the spot and told he might think himself a fortunate man to have escaped a legal process for swindling.

I am glad to be able to say that Sophy Mâtâ, for whose sake all this suffering was gone through, was quite well again by the time her father came back from the Prairie.
