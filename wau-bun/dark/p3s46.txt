About this time another thing happened that put our little community in no small excitement. Robineau, the striker from the blacksmith establishment at Sugar Creek, near the Four Lakes, came into the Agency on a very cold day. He had come for medical aid for Mâtâ's eldest daughter, Sophy, who had been sliding on the lake and had fallen on the ice and been badly hurt. Her father was away — he had gone to Prairie du Chien to put his youngest daughter in school. Two or three days had passed since the accident, a high fever had set in, and the girl was in great suffering, so it had been thought best to send Robineau to us for advice and aid and leave Turcotte and a friendly Indian woman from a neighboring lodge to take charge of her.

The commanding officer, when the case was laid before him, did not think it prudent to let the surgeon leave the post, but he very cheerfully granted leave of absence to Currie, the hospital steward, a young man with some knowledge of medicine and surgery.

Sophy would want an experienced nurse, so we secured Madame Bellaire, wife of the Frenchman generally employed as express to Chicago; and for help and company we added Agathe, a daughter of Day-kau-ray, who lived in Paquette's family.

Of Agathe I shall have more to say hereafter.

The cold was excessive the day Robineau, Currie, and the two women set out for Sugar Creek, about forty miles off. We had given them a good store of rice, crackers, tea, and sugar for the invalid, and all of it, with their own provisions for the road, was packed on the horse Robineau had ridden up to the Portage. They were expected to reach the place on the second day.

On the fourth day after they left, Turcotte walked in. He had come to ask why Robineau had not returned with help for poor Sophy. There was only one way to read it. Robineau had guided them as ill as he had guided the boat at the Grande Chûte the summer before, and though he could not shipwreck them out on the prairie, he had certainly lost them somewhere in the woods. The comfort was that they could not well starve. The rice and crackers were several days' provisions, and with Agathe along, who must be used to this kind of life, they could hardly fail to come upon Indians in time and be brought back to the Portage.

Still, day followed day and no word came. Turcotte went back to Sugar Creek with comforts and prescriptions for Sophy, and Colonel Cutler sent out a party to look for the missing ones. Of them all it was Currie we pitied most, with his delicate constitution.

The snow fell, and the wind got up and howled, and we could settle to nothing but walking from window to window, watching to see whether anybody would come into sight out of the distance. There were no Indians near enough to send out after them. By the tenth day we had almost given them up.

That was when the joyful news was suddenly brought in to us. They are found. They are at the Fort. A party of soldiers who had been out exploring out searching had come on them at Hastings's Woods, twelve miles off, working their way back toward the Portage slowly and feebly. They knew they had the right track under them by then. They had barely the strength to keep to it.

They were worn out with cold and hunger. Their provisions had given out two days before, and they had talked seriously of killing the horse and eating him. The only thing that had stopped them was that Currie could not go on foot, and they dreaded having to leave him in the woods to perish.

Agathe had been sure from the first that they were on the wrong track, but Robineau, with his usual obstinacy, kept to it until it brought them out at the Rock River. Then he was obliged to admit his error, and they turned and began retracing their steps.

Agathe had carried her hatchet with her, as her people always do. So they had a fire every night, and boughs cut to shelter them from the storms. Without that they must certainly have perished.

Two things in the story stirred something in us stronger than sympathy. The first was that the miserable Robineau had demanded of Currie, first all his money, and then his watch, as the price of bringing the party back into the right path — which he claimed all along that he knew perfectly well.

The second was Bellaire, who gave his kind, excellent wife a hearty flogging for going off, as he put it, on such a fool's errand.

The latter culprit was out of our jurisdiction and stayed out of it. Monsieur Robineau was discharged on the spot, and warned that he might think himself a happy man to have escaped a legal process for swindling.

I am happy to say that Sophy Mâtâ, in whose behalf all this suffering had been undergone, was quite recovered by the time her father came back from the Prairie.
