All the while this work was going on, our Indian friends kept coming to see us. First among them, and foremost, was the young Dandy — Four-Legs.

He turned up one fine morning with two women, whom he presented as his wives. He had a little Chippewa, and on that thin bridge he and our mother contrived to hold something like a conversation. He had come in everything he owned: brooches, wampum, fan, looking-glass, the whole inventory. The paint on his face and across his chest had plainly taken him a long while to get right.

He took a chair, the way he had seen it done in Washington, and signed to his women to sit down on the floor.

Taking two wives is not a common thing among the Indians. They appear to have worked out that the fewer of them a man has to manage, the more peace there is in the wigwam.

Still, it happens now and then that a husband gets a foolish notion about a second woman, and then he has to spend every scrap of cunning and eloquence he owns bringing the first one around to the idea of another body in the lodge. It has to be handled delicately, or there will be no harmony in that house again.

"My dear, your health isn't very good, it is time you should have some rest. You have worked very hard, and it grieves me that you should have to labor any longer. Let me get you some nice young squaw to wait upon you, that you may live at ease all the rest of your life."

The first wife agrees. She has no choice about it. And if she happens to be a jealous woman with a long memory, the life the newcomer leads after that does not bear describing. The old wife keeps every right she has as dowager and duenna, and the husband's tenderness is thin payment for what the young rival is put through.

The Dandy came to us on a Sunday morning. We were all sitting quietly, reading. Four-Legs asked my mother why we were doing that, and why everything around us was so still.

My mother explained the day of rest to him — that we set it aside for worshipping and serving the Great Spirit, as he had commanded in his Holy Word.

Four-Legs nodded his approval. That was quite right, he said. He was glad to see us doing our duty. He was very religious himself, and he liked to see it in other people. He always saw to it that his squaws attended to their duties too — not reading, perhaps, but the sort of thing the Great Spirit liked, and that he considered proper and becoming.

He had no appetite at all for hearing where our beliefs and his parted company. The conviction among the Winnebagoes that if the Great Spirit had wanted them any different from what they are, he would have made them so, is too solid a thing for argument or persuasion to move.

Sometimes a man who wants to appear a little civilized will sit through everything you have to say about Christianity, and then remark pleasantly, "Yes, we believe that too," and turn the talk to something else.

As a rule they do not see what there is to be gained by taking up the religion and the habits of the whites. "Look at them," they say, "always toiling and striving — always wearing a brow of care — shut up in houses — afraid of the wind and the rain — suffering when they are deprived of the comforts of life! We, on the contrary, live a life of freedom and happiness. We hunt and fish, and pass our time pleasantly in the open woods and prairies. If we are hungry, we take some game, or, if we don't find that, we can go without. If our enemies trouble us, we can kill them, and there is no more said about it. What should we gain by changing ourselves into white men?"
