With the payment finished and the Indians dispersed, we set about settling into our little home for a quiet season. The quiet did not last. A new source of disturbance came up.

My husband, as Agent of the Winnebagoes, had sent his accounts of disbursements on to the Department at Washington, and the accounts had never arrived there. Word of that came back to him in due course — meaning by the roundabout, unreliable chain of hands that was all we had then for keeping any sort of contact with the Eastern world. The difficulty was plain enough. If the vouchers for what he had already spent could not be produced, and the recent payment of $15,000 in annuity money along with them, he would be standing in a very awkward position indeed. He decided to go straight to Washington and carry the duplicate accounts there himself.

"Should you like to go and see your father and mother," he said to me, one morning, "and show them how the West agrees with you?"

A year had passed since I had seen them. Nothing he could have said would have pleased me more, and within a few days everything was ready for us to start.

We had a guest with us just then — Miss Brush, of Detroit, who had come up from Green Bay in company with Mr. and Mrs. Whitney and Miss Frances Henshaw, the party being bound on an excursion to the Mississippi. Our little India-rubber house had stretched itself once again to hold all of them, and their visit was a very pleasant one.

Two young ladies arriving at once was, as anyone can imagine, an outright godsend to the unmarried lieutenants. So when Miss Brush — weary of traveling, or else discouraged by the eight inches of snow that fell on the 4th of October — gave up the remainder of the excursion and accepted our urging that she stay with us until her friends came back through, we were regarded around the garrison as public benefactors. She would now come with us as far as Green Bay, and perhaps the whole way to Detroit.

Nothing happened to us on the river going down. We came into Green Bay to find the entire place awake and restless, waiting on one of Mr. Newbery's schooners. From Fort Howard down to Dickenson's, there was no other subject. The vessel would bring friends to some and supplies to others, fashions for the ladies and news for the gentlemen, and it carried the mails besides — not merely for Green Bay, but for the whole of the upper country.

She came in a few days later, and she had a mail aboard for Fort Winnebago. Only in the winter months did our letters travel overland to us, by way of Niles's Settlement and Chicago.

It fell to my husband, as Postmaster, to open the bag, and he took out his own letters first. The first he read told him that the missing accounts had turned up at the Department and all was satisfactory there. The second was a sad disappointment. My parents had left for Kentucky for the winter — not for any city, not for anywhere a person could reach, but up the Sandy, and on over among the mountains of Virginia, chasing old land-claims that belonged to my grandfather's estate.

There was no following them. In the short time we could be away from home we could hardly hope to come up with them, and that was true even allowing for the directions an old settler had once supplied to my father while he was exploring that wild country.

"You must go up Tug," said the man, "and down Troublesome, and fall over on to Kingdom-come." An expedition of that description did not seem advisable to us. We turned around and made our way back to Fort Winnebago.
