The heroine of the story just told was fourteen years old when she married Colonel McKillip, an officer in the British service. He died in 1794, near the place afterward called Fort Defiance, at the Miami Rapids. A body of British troops had come down from Detroit to hold that post; General Wayne was in the field against the Indians at the time, and the British Government judged it proper to make a demonstration or two on behalf of its allies. The Colonel had ridden out with a party to reconnoiter. He was coming back in after dark when one of his own sentinels fired on him and killed him. Mrs. Helm was the child of that marriage.

Widowed, Mrs. McKillip went to live with her parents at Grosse Pointe, eight miles up the river from Detroit. It was while she was there that the thing happened which she told over so many times afterward, always with a particular attention, because of how melancholy and how unaccountable it was.

Of all that large family she loved her second brother, Thomas Lytle, best; he had the kindest and most affectionate nature of any of them. He was courting a young lady who lived at the river Trench,[43] which is what they called it then — it is the Thames now — a stream that runs into Lake St. Clair some twenty miles above Detroit. To go to her he would take his horse across the Detroit River on the ferry and then ride overland to the Trench, which at certain seasons could be forded.

Late in the spring, on a fine forenoon, he said good-bye to his mother and his sister and set off on one of these visits. They generally kept him away two or three days.

After dinner his sister was sitting with her work at an open window that looked into a small side inclosure planted with fruit-trees. Something came between her and the light. She looked up. Her brother Thomas was standing outside the window. His horse was not with him. He had his saddle up on his shoulders.

She had heard no gate. That puzzled her, and so did the sight of him carrying his gear that way, and she called out to him and asked what had happened and why he was back so soon. He did not answer her. He only looked hard into her face, and went on along the paved walk that runs down to the stables, slowly.

She sat where she was for a little while, waiting for him to come round and explain himself and his adventures. He did not come. At last she could not stand the waiting, and she laid her work aside and went through to the back of the house to look for him.

Her mother was the first person she met. "Have you seen Thomas?" she asked.

"Thomas! He has gone to the river Trench."

"No, he has returned - I saw him pass the window not fifteen minutes since."

"Then he will be in soon."

She could not wait for that. She went out to the stables and hunted everywhere, in every direction. There was no Thomas. There was no horse. There was no saddle. She asked the servants and not one of them had seen him. She went back into the house and told her mother what had happened.

"You must have fallen asleep and dreamed it," said her mother.

"No, indeed! I was wide awake - I spoke to him, and he gave me no answer, but such a look!"

The uneasiness stayed on her all afternoon, and no argument she made to herself would shift it.

In the morning a messenger came from the river Trench with dismal news.

They had found the young man and his horse a little way below the ford, both of them drowned.

It appeared that when he reached the bank he found the river running much higher than usual, swollen with the recent rains. He would have to swim it with the horse. So he stripped off his clothes, tied them into a bundle, and made the bundle fast across his shoulders. What they believed, from how the bodies lay and how they looked when found, was that the force of the current shifted that bundle on his back, and that the weight of it pulled his head down under the water and held it there, and that he could not lift it again.

He had been seen going past a house near the stream on his way to the ford, and the hour of that is known. Set it against the hour at the window, and there is no getting away from it: he died at the moment his sister saw, or believed she saw, him pass in front of her.

When these sad particulars were told to me, I could not keep myself from asking, - "Mother, is it not possible this might have been a dream?"

"A dream? No, indeed, my child. I was perfectly wide awake - as much so as I am at this moment. I am not superstitious. I have never believed in ghosts or witches, but nothing can ever persuade me that this wasn't a warning sent from God, to prepare me for my brother's death."

Anyone who knew her knew what she was: a woman of rational, level good sense, with no fancies and no fears in her, and a calm self-possession that never once left her in circumstances that would try anyone. Knowing that, you find yourself very nearly seeing the thing as she saw it.

The order had now come for the evacuation of Fort Dearborn and the removal of the troops to Fort Howard, at Green Bay. That meant the family circle would be broken up. Our mother, and our sister Mrs. Helm with her little son, were to come back with us to Fort Winnebago. The rest of the family, Robert excepted, would go with the command to Green Bay.

The schooner Napoleon was to come round from Detroit to carry the troops and all their goods and chattels to the post assigned them. Our own party would travel overland. We meant to pick a shorter and pleasanter road than the one that had brought us here. My husband had arrived with his Frenchmen, Petaille Grignon and Simon Lecuyer, and after that every pair of hands in the house was busy with the work of breaking up and getting away.
