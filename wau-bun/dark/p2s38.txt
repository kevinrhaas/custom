At fourteen the heroine of the foregoing story married Colonel McKillip, a British officer. He was killed in 1794 at the Miami Rapids, near the place afterward called Fort Defiance. A detachment of British troops had been sent down from Detroit to take possession of the post; General Wayne was then in the field against the Indians, and the British Government had thought it proper to make a few demonstrations on behalf of its allies. Colonel McKillip had gone out with a party to reconnoiter and was coming back in after dark. One of his own sentinels fired on him and killed him. Mrs. Helm was the daughter of that marriage.

Through her widowhood Mrs. McKillip lived with her parents at Grosse Pointe, eight miles above Detroit, and it was in those years that a thing happened to her which she never afterward let go of. She would come back to it all her life. It was melancholy and it was never explained, and she told it the same way every time.

Her second brother, Thomas Lytle, was the one she loved best out of that whole large family, because there was nothing in him that was not gentle. He was courting a young lady who lived on the river Trench, as it was called then — the Thames now — a stream that empties into Lake St. Clair some twenty miles above Detroit. His way there never varied. He took his horse across the Detroit River on the ferry, and from the far bank he went overland to the Trench, which at some seasons of the year could be forded.

On a fine forenoon late in the spring he said good-bye to his mother and his sister and set off on one of these visits. They generally kept him two or three days.

After dinner his sister sat at her work by an open window. The window looked out on a little side inclosure planted with fruit-trees, and it was the brightest thing in the room. Something came between her and that light. She looked up. Her brother Thomas was standing outside the window. His horse was not with him, and he had his saddle up on his shoulders.

She had not heard the gate. That was the first strange thing, and the saddle was the second, and she asked him what had happened and why he had come back so soon. He did not answer her. He looked into her face, steadily, and went on along the paved walk that led to the stables, walking slowly.

She sat a few moments longer, waiting for him to come round and tell her what had gone wrong. He did not come. She put down her work and went toward the back of the house to find him.

The first person she met was her mother. "Have you seen Thomas?" she asked.

"Thomas! He has gone to the river Trench."

"No, he has returned — I saw him pass the window not fifteen minutes since."

"Then he will be in soon."

His sister could not wait for that. She went out to the stables. She looked in every direction a man could have gone. There was no Thomas, no horse, no saddle. She asked the domestics, one after another, and not one of them had seen him. Then she went back into the house and told her mother what had happened.

"You must have fallen asleep and dreamed it," said her mother.

"No, indeed! I was wide awake — I spoke to him, and he gave me no answer, but such a look!"

She could not talk herself out of it. All that afternoon the uneasiness sat in her, and no reasoning would move it.

The next morning a messenger came from the river Trench.

They had found the young man and his horse in the water below the ford, both drowned.

He had come to the bank and found the river up, swollen past its usual depth by the recent rains, too deep to ford. He would have to swim it with the horse. So he took off his clothes and made them into a packet and lashed the packet to his shoulders. It was thought afterward that the force of the current had shifted the bundle, and that the bundle had held his head under and kept it there, and that he had not been able to get it up again. All of that was read off the position of the two bodies and how they looked when they were found.

Someone had seen him pass a house standing near the stream on his way to the ford, and from that hour it was plain enough. He must have met his fate at the very moment his sister saw, or thought she saw, him passing before her window.

When these sad particulars were told to me, I could not help asking the question. "Mother, is it not possible this might have been a dream?"

"A dream? No, indeed, my child. I was perfectly wide awake — as much so as I am at this moment. I am not superstitious. I have never believed in ghosts or witches, but nothing can ever persuade me that this wasn't a warning sent from God, to prepare me for my brother's death."

And anyone who knew her — her rational good sense, her freedom from fancies and fears, the calm self-possession that never once deserted her under the most trying circumstances a life can offer — would come very near to seeing it as she saw it.

The order had now come for the evacuation of Fort Dearborn and the removal of the troops to Fort Howard, at Green Bay. The family circle was to be broken up. Our mother, our sister Mrs. Helm, and her little son were to come back with us to Fort Winnebago; the rest of the family, all but Robert, were to go with the command to Green Bay.

The schooner Napoleon was to be sent from Detroit to carry the troops and their goods and chattels to their new post. Our own party would go by land, and we meant to take a shorter and pleasanter route than the one we had come in by. My husband had arrived with his Frenchmen, Petaille Grignon and Simon Lecuyer, and every hand in the house was now busy with the work of breaking up and moving out.
