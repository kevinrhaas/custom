They did not stop and they did not rest until the current carried them opposite Burns's, where, as has been told, they shouted across the water to tell the family what was loose in the country. Then they went on to the fort.

Only once they were behind the pickets, safe, did it come to them what they had left behind. The Burns family was out there in the open with a newborn in the house, and no one between them and whatever had done the work at Lee's Place. Somebody would have to go back up. The question was who would put his own life down on the table to do it. A young officer, Ensign Ronan, volunteered, and took five or six soldiers with him.

They worked a scow up the river. The mother had been delivered of a child scarcely a day before, so they lifted her into the boat on her bed, the infant with her, and brought her and the rest of the family down to the fort a foot at a time, carefully, the way you carry something that will break.

That same afternoon a corporal and six men had been given leave to go up the river to fish.

They had not come back when the survivors of Lee's Place reached the fort. Afraid the fishing party would walk straight into what was waiting up there, the commanding officer ordered a cannon fired, to warn them.

They were two miles above Lee's Place when the report rolled up the water to them. It was night by then. They took the hint at once, put out their torches, and let the boat drop back down toward the garrison as silently as they could manage. Nothing about the country had felt settled since the battle of Tippecanoe the previous November, and every man in it had learned to read the smallest noise the same way. Beware of the Indians.

Coming down past Lee's Place, they decided to put in and warn the people there to be on their guard, since the signal from the fort meant danger of some kind. The house was still. Still as death, and they all felt it. They groped their way forward. The corporal swung a leg over the low enclosure around the yard and came down with his hand on a dead man. He did not need a light. His fingers told him the head had been scalped, and told him the rest of it too. The murdered man's dog was standing over him, keeping watch on what was left of his master.

That was the whole tale, told in the dark by touch. The men retreated to their canoes and reached the fort unmolested about eleven o'clock at night. The next morning a party of citizens and soldiers volunteered to go up to Lee's Place and learn what had become of the others. They found Mr. White with two balls in him and eleven stab wounds in his breast. They found the Frenchman where he had fallen, the dog still beside him. Both bodies were carried down to the fort and buried close under its walls.

It came out later, from traders out in the Indian country, that the men who did it were a party of Winnebagoes who had come into this neighborhood to take some white scalps. Their plan had been to work down the river from Lee's Place and kill every white man outside the walls of the fort. Then the cannon spoke. They did not know what it meant, and not knowing, they decided that this one exploit was enough, and went home to Rock River.
