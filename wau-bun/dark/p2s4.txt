They did not stop and they did not rest until the current carried them opposite Burns's, where, as has been told, they shouted across the water to warn the family of the danger they were in. Then they went on to the fort.

Once their own safety was secured, it came to them that the Burns family stood at that moment in the most imminent peril. Somebody would have to go back up the river for them. The question was who would put his own life down on the table to do it. A gallant young officer, Ensign Ronan, volunteered, and took a party of five or six soldiers with him.

They worked a scow up the river. The mother had been delivered of a child scarcely a day before, so they lifted her into the boat on her bed, the infant with her, and carried her and the rest of the family carefully down to the fort.

That same afternoon a corporal and six men had been given leave to go up the river to fish.

They had not come back when the survivors of Lee's Place reached the fort. Since they might well fall in with the Indians up there, the officer in command had a cannon fired as a warning to them.

They were two miles above Lee's Place when the signal reached them. It was night by then. They took the hint at once, put out their torches, and let the boat drop back down toward the garrison as silently as they could manage. Nothing about the country had felt settled since the battle of Tippecanoe the previous November, and every man in it had learned to read the smallest noise the same way. Beware of the Indians.

Coming down past Lee's Place, they decided to put in and warn the people there to be on their guard, since the signal from the fort meant danger of some kind. Around the house everything was as still as death. They groped their way forward. The corporal swung a leg over the small enclosure and came down with his hand on the body of a dead man. By touch he learned that the head had been scalped, and that the body had been mutilated besides. The murdered man's dog was standing over him, keeping watch on what was left of his master.

That was the whole tale. The men retreated to their canoes and reached the fort unmolested about eleven o'clock at night. The next morning a party of citizens and soldiers volunteered to go up to Lee's Place and learn what had become of the others. They found Mr. White with two balls in him and eleven stab wounds in his breast. They found the Frenchman where he had fallen, the dog still beside him. Both bodies were carried down to the fort and buried close under its walls.

It came out later, from traders out in the Indian country, that the men who did it were a party of Winnebagoes who had come into this neighborhood to take some white scalps. Their plan had been to work down the river from Lee's Place and kill every white man outside the walls of the fort. Then the cannon spoke. They did not know what it meant, and not knowing, they decided that this one exploit was enough, and went home to Rock River.
