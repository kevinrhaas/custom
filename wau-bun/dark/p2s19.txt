At their own mansion Mr. Kinzie's family were closely guarded by their Indian friends, whose intention was to carry them to Detroit for safety. The rest of the prisoners stayed at the wigwams of the men who had taken them.

The next morning, the work of plunder being finished, the Indians set fire to the fort. The finery seemed to have been divided very equitably, and shawls and ribbons and feathers went fluttering about in every direction. One young fellow had got himself up in a muslin gown and one of the ladies' bonnets, and under any other circumstances the sight of him would have been funny.

Black Partridge, Wau-ban-see and Kee-po-tah, with two other Indians, posted themselves in the porch of the building as sentinels, to keep the family from whatever the young men might be worked up to do. For a short while after the fort burned, everything stayed quiet.

Then a party of Indians from the Wabash arrived. Of all the Pottowattamie bands these were decidedly the most hostile and the most implacable.

Living farther off, they had shared less than the others in the kindness of Mr. Kinzie and his family, and their regard for that family was correspondingly thinner.

Runners had been sent out to the villages to tell them the post was to be evacuated, and to tell them what the assembled Indians meant to do to the troops.

They had come at speed, thirsting to be part of it, and at the river Aux Plaines they met a party of their friends coming the other way with their chief Nee-scot-nee-meg, badly wounded, and learned that the battle was over, the spoils divided, and every scalp already taken. Their mortification was great.

Reaching Chicago, they blackened their faces and started for Mr. Kinzie's house.

Black Partridge had been watching them come from his station on the piazza, and what frightened him most was Mrs. Helm, Mr. Kinzie's step-daughter, who had come to the post only recently and was not personally known to the more remote Indians. On his advice she was dressed as an ordinary Frenchwoman of the country — a short gown and petticoat, a blue cotton handkerchief wrapped around her head. In this disguise Black Partridge himself walked her over to the house of Ouilmette, a Frenchman with a half-breed wife who belonged to Mr. Kinzie's establishment and lived close by.

As it happened, that was the first house the Indians came to, hunting prisoners. As they came up, the people inside were afraid her fair complexion and general look would give her away as an American. So they lifted a large feather bed and put her under the edge of it, on the bedstead, with her face to the wall. Then Mrs. Bisson, a half-breed, the sister of Ouilmette's wife, sat down on the front of the bed with her sewing.

It was a hot day in August. Under the weight of the bed she could barely draw a breath, and the feverish excitement of fear on top of it grew so intolerable that Mrs. Helm at last begged them to let her out and give her up to the Indians.

"I can but die," she said. "Let them put an end to my misery at once."

Mrs. Bisson answered, "Your death would be the destruction of us all, for Black Partridge has resolved that if one drop of the blood of your family is spilled, he will take the lives of all concerned in it, even his nearest friends, and if once the work of murder commences, there will be no end of it, so long as there remains one white person or half-breed in the country."

That expostulation gave Mrs. Helm fresh resolution, and she stayed where she was.

The Indians came in. From her hiding-place she could see them at moments, gliding about the room, stealthily going over every part of it without appearing to search anything at all. At last, apparently satisfied that no one was concealed there, they left the house.

The whole time, Mrs. Bisson kept her seat on the side of the bed, calmly sorting and arranging the patch-work of the quilt she was making, with the appearance of the utmost tranquillity — not knowing, from one moment to the next, whether she was about to receive a tomahawk in her brain. Her self-command unquestionably saved the lives of everyone in that room.
