Before the war of the Revolution the whole western portion of Pennsylvania belonged to the Indian tribes, and everybody knows it. Among them the Delawares were friends to the whites, and when the great struggle opened they took the part of the United States. The Iroquois were the friends and allies of the mother-country.

Very few white settlers had ventured past the Susquehanna. The country beyond was crossed by roving bands — Shawanoes, Nanticokes and others — who at times professed friendship with the Americans and acted in concert with the Delawares, or Lenape, as allies, and at other times let themselves be worked upon by their neighbors the Iroquois until they showed the most sanguinary spirit of hostility.

That was the arithmetic of life on the frontier: constant peril, constant alarm. Many a scene of dismal barbarity was acted out there, as the histories of the time will testify, and even the people who felt themselves in some measure protected by their immediate neighbors the Delawares never for a day forgot the caution their exposed situation demanded of them.

The military garrison at Pittsburg — Fort Pitt, as it was then called — gave some added security to those who had pushed farther west into the fertile valleys of the Alleghany and the Monongahela. Among these was the family of Mr. Lytle, who some years before the opening of our story had moved from Baltimore to Path Valley, near Carlisle, and afterward settled himself on the banks of Plum River, a tributary of the Alleghany. There he lived with his wife and five children, comfortable and secure, never once visited by an enemy, and troubled only by the occasional false alarm raised by his more timorous neighbors — people with longer experience of frontier life, who were prone to anticipate evil and to magnify every appearance of danger.

On a bright afternoon in the autumn of 1779, two of Mr. Lytle's children were playing in a little dingle behind their father's house — a girl of nine and her brother, two years younger. Some large trees had been felled there not long before and were still lying about untrimmed, their branches on them, and there were logs cut for fuel scattered over the ground. The children tired of their game and sat down on one of the logs, and to pass the time they got to talking about a thing that had been troubling them both.

A few hours earlier, playing in the same place, they thought they had seen an Indian crouched behind one of the fallen trees. The Indians of the neighborhood paid occasional visits to the family, and the children knew many of them well and were fond of them. This one they did not know. After the first look they ran for the house.

Their mother scolded them for the story they brought in, and set about proving it had nothing in it. "You know," she said, "you are always alarming us unnecessarily: the neighbors' children have frightened you to death. Go back to your play, and learn to be more courageous."

So they went back to their sports, not much persuaded by their mother's arguments. And while they were sitting there on the tree-trunk, something interrupted them. It sounded like a quail, not far off.

"Listen," said the boy. A second note answered the first. "Do you hear that?"

"Yes," she said. Then, after a moment: "Do you not hear a rustling among the branches of the tree yonder?"

"Maybe it is a squirrel — but look! what is that? Surely I saw something red among the branches. It looked like a fawn popping up its head."

They were staring so hard at the fallen tree that they had forgotten there was any other direction. Hands took them from behind. They were pinioned in a grip like iron, and they were in the arms of savages, whose faces and gestures told them plainly enough that these were enemies.

The men made signs that meant silence, on pain of death, and hurried them off, half dead with terror, on a course leading away from their father's house. They went a long way without a word spoken. As night came on the severity of their captors slackened a little, and the party halted and took the usual precautions against being surprised.

Torn out of their home, taken from their parents, and knowing every horror that the rumors of that time attached to a captivity among the Indians — a torturing death, perhaps, at the end of it — the children could hold it in no longer, and gave way to sobbing and lamentation.

Their distress seemed to touch one of the party, a man of mild aspect, who came over and tried to quiet them. He made them a couch out of the long grass that grew near the encamping-place. He gave them a share of his own stock of dried meat and parched corn. And he made them understand, by signs, that no further harm was intended them.
