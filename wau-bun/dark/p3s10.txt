The companies of the First Infantry that had been holding Fort Winnebago had their orders before we ever rode in. They were to move on to the Mississippi the moment a portion of the Fifth, then at Fort Howard, came up to relieve them.

A good many of the officers of the Fifth had wives, which meant every set of quarters in the post would be spoken for. So although Major Twiggs pressed us hard to come back inside the Fort and stay until he went on furlough, we decided it was better to set ourselves up at once at "the Agency."

It was a very grand name for a very small concern, and I could never say it without wanting to laugh. The heads of department at Washington had promised us a comfortable house as soon as Congress voted money enough to cover any extra expense in the Indian Department. Congress, it was clear, bore us some private grudge, because it had let two whole sessions go by without troubling itself about where we slept. There was nothing to be done about that. We would be comfortable on whatever we had.

What we had were the old log barracks, thrown up two years before for the officers and men when the post was first established. Our French engagés had taken them apart and stood them up again on the little hill across from the Fort. To these were now added a dairy, stables, a smoke-house and the rest, built of tamarack logs hauled out of the swamp nearby. The whole arrangement looked exactly as rough and as makeshift as it was.

The main building was a row of four rooms. No two of them opened into each other. Each had its own door onto the open air, and a small window cut straight through the logs front and back to let in light. An enormous clay chimney served every two rooms and took up one whole side of each. Overhead, where a ceiling should have been, there were a few rough boards laid across the transverse logs that carried the roof.

It was surprising how quickly those old broken-down rooms began to feel like a home — a few Indian mats down on the floor, the piano and the rest of the furniture set where furniture belongs, even some pictures hung against the logs. The pictures did not last. With the first hard rain the water found its way in through any number of crevices, and after that we made a habit of putting away anything that moisture could ruin. We laughed about it. We also packed the carpets and the extra furniture up onto the boards overhead, which we grandly called the attic, and settled down to wait for the day the Government would condescend to remember that we existed. The thing that wore on me most was the straw bonnet. I had to wear it all day long, crossing from bedroom to parlor and from parlor to kitchen, and I got so used to the weight of it that I would sometimes forget to take it off when I sat down to the table, or to some quiet piece of work with my mother and sister.
