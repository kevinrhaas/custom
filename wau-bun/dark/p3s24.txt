A young man called Shee-shee-banze — the Little Duck — was working his canoe along the shore of the lake.

A pair of girls came down to the water. The elder of them saw him and said to her sister, "Let us call to him to take us a sail."

It has to be said that whenever two or more sisters turn up in one of these Indian stories, the elder is without fail the foolish one, the ridiculous one, the one you are meant to find disgusting, and the younger is the wise and beautiful one.

The younger objected. "Oh, no," she said, "let us not do such a thing. What will he think of us?"

Her sister paid no attention to that and called out across the water, "Ho! come and take us into your canoe." He did as he was told. He brought the canoe in to the shore and took the two of them aboard.

"Who are you?" the elder asked.

"I am Way-gee-mar-kin," he answered, "the great chief."

Now, the true Way-gee-mar-kin had something of the fairy about him. When his followers were gathered round him and he had a mind to be generous, he would give one small cough, and out of his mouth would come silver brooches and ear-bobs and ornaments of every kind, and his people would go down on the ground after them, each one grabbing for more than his share, exactly as people do in places that consider themselves more civilized.

So the elder sister said, "If you are Way-gee-mar-kin, let us see you cough."

As it happened, Shee-shee-banze owned a few bits of silver himself, won in scrambles of that very sort, and he kept them packed away in the sides of his mouth against an emergency. He hacked and jerked and produced two or three, and the girl snatched them up as fast as they came.

They went on down the shore. After a while a fine, tall elk stepped out of the timber and came to the water to drink.

"What is that?" said the one who did all the talking. The younger had not spoken since they came aboard, and did not speak now.

"It is my dog that I hunt with."

"Call him to us, that I may see him."

Shee-shee-banze called. The elk turned and fled back into the trees.

"He doesn't seem to obey you, though."

"No, it is because you inspire him with disgust, and so he flies from you."

Not long after, a bear came out at the water's edge.

"What is that?"

"One of my servants."

Again she asked him to call it. Again the call was ignored, and again he gave the same reason for it.

The outing ended at last. There had been magic in it the whole time, because the girls had believed themselves to be sitting in a canoe and there was no canoe at all. They had only imagined the thing under them.

Shee-shee-banze lived in his grandmother's lodge, and that was where he took his two guests.

He left them standing outside and went in by himself.

"Grandmother," he said, "I have brought you two young girls, who will be your daughters-in-law. Invite them into your lodge."

The old woman called out, "Ho! come in." In they came, and they were made welcome, and given the best of everything the lodge had in it.

Meanwhile the real Way-gee-mar-kin was getting up a great feast. When he handed his messenger the invitations he told him, "Be very particular to bid Shee-shee-banze to the feast, for, as he is the smallest and meanest person in the tribe, you must use double ceremony with him, or he will be apt to think himself slighted."

Shee-shee-banze was at home with his new friends when the messenger came to the door.

"Ho! Shee-shee-banze," he called, "you are invited to a great feast that Way-gee-mar-kin is to give tonight, to all his subjects."

Shee-shee-banze behaved as though nothing at all had been said. He whistled a little and looked off in another direction. The messenger said the whole thing over again, got the same nothing for his trouble, and went away.

The girls looked at each other. At last the elder found her voice.

"What does this mean?" she said. "Why does he call you Shee-shee-banze, and invite you to visit Way-gee-mar-kin?"

"Oh," said Shee-shee-banze, "it is one of my followers that always likes to be a little impudent. I am obliged to put up with it sometimes, but you noticed that I treated him with silent contempt."

The messenger went back and reported to the chief exactly how the invitation had been received.

"Oh," said the chief, who was a good-natured man, "it is because he feels that he is poor and insignificant. Go back again - call him by my name, and make a flourishing speech to him."

The messenger went and did precisely as he had been told.

"Way-gee-mar-kin," he announced, with every ounce of pomp he could raise, "a great feast is to be given tonight, and I am sent most respectfully to solicit the honor of your company!"

"Did I not tell you?" said Shee-shee-banze to the two girls. Then he gave the small careless nod of a man used to such things and added, "Tell them I'll come."

When night came he painted himself with great care and put on his best feathers and every ornament he had. Before he left he drew his grandmother aside.

"Be sure," he said, "that you watch these young people closely until I come back. Shut up your lodge tight, tight. Let no one come in or go out, and, above all things, don't go to sleep."

Having given his orders, he went off.

The grandmother fully meant to obey them. But the night wore on and her eyes kept falling shut, and at last she took a strong cord and laced it back and forth across the mat that hung over the doorway, the way the Indians lace up the mouth of a bag. The girls lay quiet in their bed. Everything was fast. She lay down herself and was soon sleeping very comfortably.

The girls were not asleep at all. They were being eaten alive by curiosity about where Shee-shee-banze had gone, and the moment they were certain of the old woman they made ready to follow him and see for themselves. In case she woke and looked over, they took two logs of wood and laid them in the bed under the blanket, arranged so that anyone would have sworn two people were sleeping there quietly. Then they cut the cords at the door and went out, and the noise of the music and the dancing and the merry-making led them straight to the house of Way-gee-mar-kin.

Inside, the chief sat on a throne in a blaze of light and splendor. The whole place was pleasure and amusement. Courtiers filled the room, every one of them turned out in the most brilliant array. The two of them searched the room for their friend Shee-shee-banze. He was not in it.

Every so often the chief would cough, and silver and other precious things came showering out in every direction, and in the same instant the whole company was down on the floor scrambling to gather them up and keep them.

It was in one of those rushes that the brides-elect finally caught sight of their poor little friend. He was crowded into a corner, where nobody took the smallest notice of him except to push him out of the way or step on him when he was underfoot. Each time one or another handled him so, he let out a pitiful little squeak. He was far too busy saving himself to notice that the pair he had shut up safe at home were standing there, watching every bit of it.

At length the word came that the company should go. They all went, except the two young damsels. The chief had marked those two, and had already sent an assistant to them with great offers if they would stay and be his wives.

Poor Shee-shee-banze came home to his lodge, and found to his consternation that the door stood open.

"Ho! grandmother," he cried, "is this the way you keep watch?"

The old woman started up out of her sleep. "There are my daughters-in-law," she said, and pointed to the two logs. He flung himself down on the ground between them, and struck them so hard that his back was broken, but he never minded that. His whole mind was on revenge and on getting his sweethearts back.

He waited only long enough to fetch a powerful poison and make it ready. Then he stole softly back to Way-gee-mar-kin's wigwam. Nothing stirred anywhere. He got inside without making the least sound. The chief lay there, and a young girl slept against either side of him.

All three of them were deep asleep. The chief lay face up, his mouth open wide. The poison was down his throat before he knew anything of it, and Shee-shee-banze went quietly home.

In the morning it went round the village that Way-gee-mar-kin was dead — found so in his own bed. Everyone naturally supposed he had overindulged at his own feast. There was grief and lamentation everywhere. "Let us go and tell poor Shee-shee-banze," said one of them, "he was so fond of Way-gee-mar-kin."

They found him sitting on a bank with a fishing line. He had been up since the peep of day, making his preparations for receiving the news.

He had taken two or three fish already, cut the bladders out of them, run them full of blood and strapped them beneath his arm. The friends of the dead chief came in sight of him and shouted, "Oh! Shee-shee-banze - your friend, Way-gee-mar-kin, is dead!"

He flung up his hands in despair, drew his knife, and drove it in — not into his heart at all, but into the blood-filled bladders he had made ready. He dropped to the ground, to all appearances a dead man, and the messengers at once began reproaching themselves. "Oh! why did we tell him so suddenly? We might have known he wouldn't survive it. Poor Shee-shee-banze! he loved Way-gee-mar-kin so."

The day after the funeral, to the great surprise of everybody, Shee-shee-banze came walking toward the dead chief's wigwam. As he came along he was singing — chaunting, rather, on a single flat note,[50] this: - "Way-gee-mar-kin is dead, is dead, I know who killed him.

I guess it was I - I guess it was I."

The whole village came up at that. Every one of them went after the murderer, and he slipped through the lot of them and got away to a place where they could not touch him.

Before long he showed himself again, walking with little mincing steps and singing to the same tune as before, - "If you wish to take and punish me, Let the widows come and catch me."

That struck everybody as a sound idea, and the young women were advised to go out and lure the culprit back into the village, where the dead chief's friends could lay hands on him.

So out they went. He would let them get near him and then dance off a little way; would let them come nearer still, and then give ground again, singing the whole time, "Come, pretty widows, come and catch me."

That was how he drew them on, and he sweetened it with honeyed words and flattering speeches, until at last they agreed to go home with him and live in his lodge.

It shocked the dead chief's people that the widows could turn so quickly, and they settled it among themselves that all three should be punished the moment they could be caught.

They came round the lodge with shouting and threats. But he and his two brides had already slipped the watch and got down to his canoe, which lay in the river a few steps off.

They were barely aboard when the escape was discovered. The whole troop came after them, and some went into the stream and got hold of the canoe, and in the struggle it went over. The instant they touched the water — whether the canoe's own magic did it, or the grandmother's necromancy, nobody can say — the three of them were turned into ducks and flew off quacking.

Ever since that time, birds of that kind keep to parties of three — a male, and two females along with him.

The Canard de France, or Mallard, and the Brancheuse, or Wood Duck, order themselves otherwise, and go through the air two by two. Of the Wood Duck they say his faithfulness runs so deep that once his mate is taken from him he will pair with no other bird at all, and grieves for her as long as he lives.
