We still had the trail to guide us, and we kept following it until about nine o'clock, when we came out of a wood upon a broad and rapid river. A collection of Indian wigwams stood on the opposite bank. The trail led directly down to the water, so it was fair to infer that the stream was fordable. We had no opportunity of testing it. The banks were so lined with ice, piled up tier upon tier by the breaking-up of the previous week, that we tried in vain to find any path by which we could get down to the water at all. We rode along the top of it, looking, and the wigwams across the current stayed exactly as far away as they had been.

The men shouted, again and again, in hopes that some straggling inhabitant of the village might be within hearing with his canoe. No answer came back but the echoes. What was to be done? I looked at my husband and saw the care on his brow, though he still kept speaking cheerfully. "We will follow this cross-trail down the bank of the river," he said. "There must be Indians wintering near, in some of these points of wood."

I confess I felt somewhat dismayed at our prospects. I kept up a show of courage and did not let my despondency be seen. All the party were dull and gloomy enough without it.

We kept along the bank, which stood considerably above the water and was bordered a little way off by thick wood. All at once my horse, who was mortally afraid of Indians, began to jump and prance, snorting and pricking up his ears as though an enemy were at hand. I screamed with delight to my husband, at the head of the file, "Oh, John! John! there are Indians near — look at Jerry!"

At that instant a little Indian dog ran out from under the bushes at the roadside and began barking at us. Never were sounds more welcome. We rode straight into the thicket, and, descending into a little hollow, found two squaws crouched behind the bushes, trying to conceal themselves from our sight.

They appeared greatly relieved when Mr. Kinzie addressed them in the Pottowattamie language. "What are you doing here?"

"Digging Indian potatoes" — a species of artichoke.

"Where is your lodge?"

"On the other side of the river."

"Good — then you have a canoe here. Can you take us across?"

"Yes — the canoe is very small."

They conducted us down the bank to the water's edge, where the canoe was. It was indeed very small. It lay drawn up in the broken ice, and it did not look to me like a thing that would hold three. My husband explained to them that they must take me across first and then come back for the rest of the party.

"Will you trust yourself alone over the river?" he asked me. "You see that but one can cross at a time."

"Oh, yes." And I was soon laid flat in the bottom of the canoe, looking straight up at the sky, while the older squaw took the paddle in her hand and knelt at my head, and the younger, a girl of fourteen or fifteen, stationed herself at my feet. There was just room for me to lie in that position, with each of the others kneeling in the opposite ends of the canoe.

While these preparations were making, Mr. Kinzie questioned the women about our whereabouts. They knew no name for the river but Saumanong. That was not definite, being the generic term for any large stream. But he gathered that the village we had passed higher up, on the other side, was Wau-ban-see's, and from that he knew we were on the Fox River, and probably about fifty miles from Chicago.

The squaw, answering his inquiries, assured him that Chicago was close by.

"That means," he said, "that it isn't so far off as Canada. We must not be too sanguine."

The men set about unpacking the horses, and I was paddled across the river. The old woman turned right around and went back for the next load, leaving the younger one with me for company. I seated myself on the fallen trunk of a tree, in the middle of the snow, and looked across at the dark water. I am not ashamed to confess my weakness — for the first time on my trip, I shed tears. It was neither hunger nor fear nor cold that extorted them. It was the utter desolation of spirit, the sickness of heart that hope deferred always occasions, and which of all evils is the hardest to bear.

The poor little squaw looked into my face with a wondering, sympathizing expression. Probably she was speculating in her own mind about what a person who rode so fine a horse and wore so comfortable a broadcloth dress could possibly have to cry about. I pointed to a seat beside me on the log. She preferred to stand and go on gazing at me with the same pitying look. Presently a young companion joined her, and after a short chattering of which I was evidently the subject, the two of them trotted off into the woods and left me to my own solitary reflections.

"What would my friends at the East think," I said to myself, "if they could see me now? What would poor old Mrs. Welsh say? She who warned me that if I came away so far to the West, I should break my heart?

"Would she not rejoice to find how likely her prediction was to be fulfilled?"

These thoughts roused me. I dried up my tears, and by the time my husband and his party and all his horses and luggage were across, I had recovered my cheerfulness and was ready for fresh adventures.
