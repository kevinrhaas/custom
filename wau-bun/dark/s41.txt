The trail was still under us, and we held to it until about nine o'clock. Then the wood opened out and there was a river in front of us, broad and running fast, with a cluster of Indian wigwams standing on the far bank. The trail went straight on down into the water, which said plainly enough that the stream could be forded. We never got the chance to prove it. The break-up of the week before had stacked ice along the banks, tier upon tier, and we searched up and down for some place to bring ourselves down to the water and could not find one.

The men shouted, and shouted again, on the chance that some straggler from the village might be within hearing with his canoe. Nothing came back to us but our own voices off the far side. What were we to do? I looked at my husband. He was still speaking cheerfully, but the care had settled into his face. "We will follow this cross-trail down the bank of the river," he said. "There must be Indians wintering near, in some of these points of wood."

I will admit that our prospects dismayed me. I kept up a show of courage all the same and let nobody see how low I had sunk. There was no help in it either way — the whole party was dull and gloomy already.

So we went on along the bank, which stood well up above the water, with a thick wood set back a little from it. Then my horse, who held a mortal terror of Indians, started to prance and jump beneath me, snorting, ears pricked, certain that an enemy stood somewhere close. I screamed up the line to my husband, who rode at the head of it, and what I felt was pure delight. "Oh, John! John! there are Indians near - look at Jerry!"

At that instant a little Indian dog came out from under the bushes at the roadside and set up a barking at us. No sound was ever more welcome to me. We rode straight into the thicket, dropped down into a small hollow, and found two squaws crouched behind the brush, doing their best to keep themselves out of our sight.

The relief came over them when Mr. Kinzie spoke to them in the Pottowattamie language. "What are you doing here?"

"Digging Indian potatoes" - (a species of artichoke.)

"Where is your lodge?"

"On the other side of the river."

"Good - then you have a canoe here. Can you take us across?"

"Yes - the canoe is very small."

They brought us down the bank to the water's edge where the canoe was lying. It was very small indeed. My husband explained to them that they would have to carry me across first and then come back for the rest of the party.

"Will you trust yourself alone over the river?" asked he. "You see that but one can cross at a time."

"Oh, yes" - and in a moment I was laid flat in the bottom of the canoe with nothing above me but the sky. The older of the two took the paddle and knelt at my head; the younger, a girl of fourteen or fifteen, knelt down at my feet. There was exactly room for me to lie between them that way, the two of them at the opposite ends, and no room to spare.

While all this was being got ready, Mr. Kinzie was asking the women where we had come to. They had no name for the river except "Saumanong," which tells nothing, being the word they use for any large stream. But he worked out of them that the village we had passed higher up, on the other side of the water, was Wau-ban-see's, and with that he knew where we stood: on the Fox River, and probably some fifty miles from Chicago.

To all his questions the squaw gave the same assurance, that Chicago was "close by."

"That means," he said, "that it isn't so far off as Canada. We must not be too sanguine."

The men began stripping the packs off the horses, and while they worked I was paddled over. The old woman turned around and went straight back for the next load, leaving the girl behind with me for company. I found the trunk of a fallen tree and sat down on it in the snow and looked out across the dark water. I am not ashamed to own the weakness. For the first time in the whole journey, I cried. It was not hunger that brought it, nor fear, nor the cold. It was the plain desolation of spirit, the sickness of heart that comes of "hope deferred," and of all the evils I know of, that one is the hardest to bear.

The poor little squaw stood looking into my face, wondering at me and sorry for me at once. I suppose she was working out privately what a woman mounted on so fine a horse, in a broadcloth dress as comfortable as mine, could possibly find to cry about. I patted the log beside me to offer her a seat, but she would rather stand and go on watching me with that same pitying look. A young companion came out and joined her, and after a short burst of chattering in which I was plainly the subject, the two of them trotted off into the woods and left me alone with my own thoughts.

"What would my friends at the East think," I said to myself, "if they could see me now? What would poor old Mrs. Welsh say? She who warned me that if I came away so far to the West, I should break my heart?

Would she not rejoice to find how likely her prediction was to be fulfilled?"

That was enough to rouse me. I dried my face, and by the time my husband and his party and every horse and every article of luggage were over the water, I had my cheerfulness back again and was ready for whatever came next.
