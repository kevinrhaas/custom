Within a few days our friends woke up to the fact that something had to be done, and done at once. The first step was an order forbidding any Winnebago to come inside the garrison, in case they should find out what they had known just as well as we had for the past three months — how little there was in that place to resist anything. The next was to send fatigue-parties out into the woods under guard, to cut pickets to fence the garrison in.

There was every reason to think the enemy were not far off. The best guess about their purpose in coming north was that they meant to break a road through into the Chippewa country, where they would be safe among friends and allies. But the story our Indian runners brought in most often was a different one. It was that the Sauks had determined to come down on the whites at the Portage and the Fort and massacre every one of them, sparing only the families of the Agent and the Interpreter.

Plante and Pillon had taken their families out at the first word of danger. What was left to us was Manaigre, whose wife was half Winnebago, Isidore Morrin, and the two blacksmiths from Sugar Creek, Mâtâ and Turcotte.

At night we were armed, all of us, and each had a post. Every possible thing was done to make the house secure, and then the orders were given. If we were attacked, sister Margaret and I were to take the children up to the rooms above while my husband and his men held the house as long as they could hold it. I had lately shown my sportsmanship by bringing down a blackbird on the wing, entirely by accident, and on the strength of that I believed I could do some real execution with my little pistols. They were laid beside my pillow every night. I was fully resolved to use them. What I remember, and what I find I cannot explain away, is that the idea of taking the lives of two Sauks — which is exactly what I should have done — did not trouble me in the slightest. It explained to me something I had often wondered at: how a soldier on a field of battle can be indifferent to the destruction of human life. Had I ever actually been called on to use those weapons to any effect, I have no doubt at all that I would have looked back on it with horror.

We were ringed by Indian lodges, and a camp is never quite silent, and our nerves were in the state that a steady diet of such news puts them in. We slept badly. One night, long after we had settled ourselves as well as we could, there came a tap on the window at the head of our bed, and a voice calling: "Chon! Chon!" — John! John!
