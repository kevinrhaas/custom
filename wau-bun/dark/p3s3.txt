The horses picked up their pace as we came toward a wooded knoll, and that was how we knew where we would be sleeping. They always know. Some instinct in them reads the ground and tells them where a camp belongs, and this one was as lovely a spot as anything I could have imagined.

Down near the lake the cold, late season had kept the trees a pale yellow, the color of goslings not yet fledged. Here they were breaking into full leaf. The ground under them was carpeted so thickly with flowers that we could hardly stand to think of what we were about to do to it — fell a tree, pitch a tent, put our weight down in the middle of all that. The birds were giving out their sweetest notes in the warm, lingering light. The opening buds of the young hickory and the sassafras had filled all the air around us with perfume.

After a day's ride in the glare of the sun, nothing could have been better than this. The children lost their heads over it. They wanted to be useful and kept inventing ways to be — stacking the saddles, breaking boughs for the fire, running the little kettles down for water and carrying them back to Petaille and Lecuyer, the Frenchmen, who were getting our supper together.

What entertained them past all bearing was how ridiculously the horses moved once they had been spancelled. Everything was new to our little nephew Edwin. Josette had already made more than one horseback trip to St. Joseph, and she took on all the dignity of a seasoned traveler, explaining to him whatever struck him as strange or unaccountable.

They were not the last ones up when the call came the next morning — "how! how!"

The fire was built up again, breakfast was started, and the Frenchmen went off to bring in the horses so we could get an early start.

Harry and Josette did their parts, under our direction, and the meal was a simple one. We sat down around the table-mat, each of us with a cup and a knife. We ate. We finished. No men came back. No horses came back. When another half-hour had gone by, my husband took Harry and went out to look for them himself.

The day wore on. One after another they would come in to report progress, which meant no progress. Petaille and Lecuyer eventually turned up with two of the horses. The rest were not anywhere. Mr. Kinzie and Harry came back wet to the knees from the dew standing in the long prairie grass, with nothing to tell us. They ate, and went out again, and came back the same way.

The rest of us had spent the whole morning at the encampment turning it over. Could the Indians have taken them? It was hardly possible. These people almost never committed a robbery in time of peace, and never against our family, whom they counted among their best friends. The horses would be found. They had probably been carelessly fastened the evening before and had simply wandered farther than they usually did.

We held a council and decided to send Grignon back to Chicago to get fresh horses from Gholson Kercheval and return as fast as he could. If he found the encampment empty when he got back, he was to understand that we had recovered our own animals and gone on to Fox River, where he would certainly catch us.

He had been gone less than an hour when the missing horses came hopping slowly out of a point of woods to the north of us — a stand of trees that every one of the searchers swore he had gone over again and again — and made straight for the spot where we were sitting. They came up as demurely as though nothing at all had happened. They seemed genuinely surprised to be met with a hearty scolding instead of the usual patting and praise.

It took a short half-hour to strike and pack the tent, stow the mats and the kettles, saddle up, and get mounted for the road.
