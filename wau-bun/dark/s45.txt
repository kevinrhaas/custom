Rising the next morning, we could hardly take it in that only twelve miles of prairie now lay between us and Chicago le Désiré, as I could not help naming it.

We could look clear across the plain, and on its farthest verge stood two tall trees. My husband pointed them out to me: he had put them in the ground himself, as a boy. They had grown lofty enough to serve as landmarks, and they stayed in sight the whole way as we followed the beaten road. I kept saying it over to myself. There live the friends I am so longing to see. There will end all our trials and hardships.

A Mr. Wentworth fell in with us on the road, and we asked him after the welfare of the family, from whom we had received no intelligence in a long while. When we reached Chicago he took us to a little tavern at the forks of the river. That part of the place was then called Wolf Point, from an Indian who had lived there named Moaway, or the Wolf.

"Dear me," said the old landlady at the little tavern, "what dreadful cold weather you must have had to travel in! Why, two days ago the river was all open here, and now it's frozen hard enough for folks to cross a-horseback!"

For all that assurance, my husband did not like to venture the horses on it. He determined to leave them where they stood and go on foot to his mother's and sister's house, about half a mile off.

We set out walking, first over the ice, then down the northern bank of the river. As we came near the house we were spotted by Genevieve, a half-breed servant of the family. She did not wait to salute us. She flew into the house crying, "Oh! Madame Kinzie, who do you think has come? Monsieur John and Madame John, all the way from Fort Winnebago on foot!"

In another minute we were in the arms of our dear, kind friends. A messenger was sent off to the garrison for the rest of the family, and for that day at least I was the wonder and the admiration of the whole circle, for the dangers I had seen.
