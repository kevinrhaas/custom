Long before we had any business expecting my husband back, a message came down asking me to come up to the new house. We all went together. We had kept away on purpose for a few days, waiting for exactly that summons, and we knew what it meant.

Plante was up on the roof beside the kitchen chimney, sitting astride a small keg and enormously pleased with himself. On the very top of the chimney he had planted a green bough. He held on to it with one hand and waved the other at us and shouted, "Eh bien, Madame John! à cette heure, pour le régal!"

"Yes, Plante, you are entitled to a treat, and I hope you won't enjoy it the less that Pillon and Manaigre are to share it with you."

A suitable reward left all three of them perfectly satisfied with their bourgeoise — against whom Plante had been known to grumble, on the grounds, he said, that she had him called up too early in the morning. He might have added that she could not follow the philosophy by which he came in to work in his own garden on the plea that it was too rainy to work in Monsieur John's.

It was no ordinary feeling with which we walked out of the old log tenement and took possession of the new house, small and unimportant a thing as it was.

I was only too glad of the luxury of a real bedchamber after more than two months of sleeping on the parlor floor. Our cooking arrangements, I admit, had not risen much. The clay chimney was not strong enough to carry the trammel and pot-hooks, which in those days had not yet been shoved aside by the cooking-stove and the kitchen-range. We built the fire the old way — great logs at the back, smaller sticks in front laid across the andirons — and any pot that would not fit on the hearth was set up on those sticks. But let one treacherous stick burn through while nobody was watching it, and down came the whole arrangement: kettles, saucepans, burning brands and cinders in one mass you could hardly separate. How often that happened while Harry or little Josette had charge of it, and the mistress of the house was in the parlor playing lady to a visitor, it were vain to tell.

Then, for all of Monsieur Plante's palisades around the chimney, a hard shower would come pelting down, and the hearth sloped just enough the wrong way that the fire would drown. The bark on the roof did not always do its duty either, and now and then we were so thoroughly rained on indoors that there was nothing for it but to snatch up the breakfast or the dinner and tuck it away under the table until better times — that is, until it was fair again. In spite of every one of these small hostilities, we liked our new quarters enormously.

The garden was doing well and full of vegetables, and even the currant bushes we had carried up from Chicago, tied in a bundle on the back of the carriage, had given us a little fruit.

The Indian women came steadily, and they never came without presents. Sometimes venison, sometimes ducks or pigeons, whortleberries, wild plums or cranberries as the season turned, neat pretty mats for the floor or the table, wooden bowls and ladles, fancy work in deer-skin or porcupine-quills. They would carry these in and drop them at my feet. If I was distracted and failed to look pleased, and to lift the things up off the floor and put them carefully away, there would be a look of real hurt, and the remark, "Our mother hates our gifts," and I would know exactly how much I had wounded them. A present had to be received graciously, and it had to be returned with something worth twice as much.

Meanwhile the weeks went by, one after another, and still the master did not come.

Schooners reached Green Bay so seldom that no one could ever say how long a passage across the lakes and back would take, and it was the end of August before he came home. He had not expected to find us moved and settled in and doing nicely, and it astonished him. With him there to drive it, the troubles we had been complaining of were put right in short order.

At Fort Gratiot he had met my young brother Julian, and brought him along — my parents were sending him out, at our asking, to live with us. Edwin was overjoyed to have a companion again, having been very much alone until then. The boys had plenty to look at soon enough, because the summons had gone out to the villages, and the Indians began coming in for the payment.
