Most of the command had already gone ahead of us. A day or two before we were to leave, they had started overland for Green Bay by way of Fort Winnebago, driving the cattle that belonged to the officers and the soldiers. Lieutenant Foster stayed behind. He wanted the pleasure of traveling with our party and was willing to wait for it. We waited too — long enough to see the rest of our family safely aboard the Napoleon. After that there was nothing left to hold us. We turned our faces homeward again.

We took a little bound-girl with us. Josette, ten years old, quick and bright and pretty, the daughter of Ouilmette — a Frenchman who had been living here at the time of the Massacre — and of a Pottowattamie mother. She had been schooled at the St. Joseph's mission under Mr. McCoy. The idea that she was going all the way to the Portage with Monsieur and Madame John made her shine.

We had a negro boy as well. Harry. Mr. Kercheval had brought him up from Kentucky the year before, and in the crossing from a slave State to a free one, Harry's position quietly changed its shape: he could be nothing now but an indentured servant. He was about to enter Dr. Wolcott's household, and before that could happen he had to choose a guardian. They brought him into the parlor, where the family had gathered, and explained it to him. My husband was a young man then, home on a visit. "Now, Harry," they said to him, "you must choose your guardian." Every person in that room expected the boy to name whichever of them carried the most years and the most dignity. Harry rolled his great eyes around the circle of faces. He let his head hang over to one side. Then he said, "I'll have Master John for my guardian."

From that day forward Harry considered himself, in some measure, to belong to Master John — and when the family in Chicago broke up, he came over to our establishment, which surprised nobody.

There were three ladies in the traveling party: our mother, our sister Mrs. Helm, and myself. I had spent some of the last days of the visit thinking about the sun and the prairie wind and what they do to a face left out in them, and I had made each of us a mask out of brown linen, cut to fit — openings for the eyes, the nose, the mouth, shaped to our own features. Then, because there was no reason to stop at merely strange, I had worked eyebrows and eyelashes into them in black silk, and a black ring around the hole for the mouth. They gathered in plaits under the chin and tied above and below, and they shut out the sun and the wind completely. Nothing more frightful can be imagined than the three of us standing there fully equipped. We argued about it. The argument was over which of us should be called the ugliest.

We left in a wash of good wishes and laughter from the few acquaintances we had left. The wagon had a pair of excellent traveling horses; sister Margaret and I had the best pacers the country could produce; and we rode off toward the Aux Plaines in high spirits, with our old friend Billy Caldwell — the Sau-ga-nash — and our brother Robert and Gholson Kercheval riding out with us as far as that.
