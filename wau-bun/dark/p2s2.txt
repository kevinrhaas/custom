The officers at the fort just then were Captain Heald, who commanded; Lieutenant Helm, Mr. Kinzie's son-in-law; Ensign Ronan; and the surgeon, Dr. Van Voorhees. Helm and Ronan were both very young men.

The command came to about seventy-five men. Very few of them were effective.

Between the garrison and the Indians there had been steady, friendly dealing all along. Certain things might have been set against that. Every year the leading men of the Pottowattamie nation, like the leading men of most tribes, made the journey up to Fort Malden in Canada and came back loaded with presents; the British government had been buying their alliance that way for years. It was also well enough known that a good many Pottowattamies, and Winnebagoes with them, had fought beside the Ottawas and Shawnees at Tippecanoe the autumn before. But the head chiefs of every band in the neighborhood were on the friendliest possible terms with the Americans, and nobody looked for the peace to break.

It was after the 15th of August that people started to remember. There had been a whole run of circumstances that would have opened the settlers' eyes, if the settlers had not been so comfortably sure of themselves. One will do for an example.

In the spring before the fort was destroyed, two Indians of the Calumet band came in to pay a call on the commanding officer. Crossing the quarters, they passed Mrs. Heald and Mrs. Helm at a game of battledoor.

One of them, Nau-non-gee, turned to the interpreter. "The white chiefs' wives are amusing themselves very much, it won't be long before they are hoeing in our corn-fields!"

Nothing was made of it at the time. It was taken for an empty threat, or at worst a flash of resentment at how unlike the lives of their own women were to the lives of the "white chiefs' wives." Some months later they remembered it, and remembered it bitterly.
