The farm at Lee's Place was occupied by a Mr. White and three people he employed in the care of it.

On the afternoon of the day this narrative commences, a party of ten or twelve Indians, dressed and painted, reached the house, and, according to the custom among them, entered and seated themselves without ceremony.

Something in their appearance and their manner excited the suspicions of one of the family, a Frenchman, who said, "I don't like the appearance of these Indians — they are none of our folks. I know by their dress and paint that they aren't Pottowattamies."

Another of the family, a discharged soldier, then said to the boy who was present, "If that is the case, we had better get away from them if we can. Say nothing, but do as you see me do."

The afternoon was far advanced. The soldier walked, without any hurry in it, toward the canoes — there were two of them tied near the bank. Some of the Indians asked where he was going. He pointed across at the cattle standing among the haystacks on the opposite bank, and made signs that they must go and fodder them, and then they should return and get their supper.

He got into one canoe and the boy into the other. The stream was narrow and they were soon across. When they had gained the opposite side they pulled some hay for the cattle, made a show of collecting them, and worked around in a gradual circuit until their movements were concealed by the haystacks. Then they took to the woods, which were close at hand, and made for the fort.

They had run about a quarter of a mile when they heard the discharge of two guns, one after the other, back the way they had come. They thought both had been levelled at the companions they had left behind.
