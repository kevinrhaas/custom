The farm at Lee's Place was held by a Mr. White, who had three people working for him there to look after it.

On the afternoon our story begins, ten or twelve Indians came up to the house, dressed and painted. They came in and sat down where they pleased, without waiting to be asked, which was the custom.

Something about the look of them, and the way they carried themselves, put one of the household on his guard. He was a Frenchman. "I don't like the appearance of these Indians - they are none of our folks. I know by their dress and paint that they aren't Pottowattamies."

Another of the household was a discharged soldier, and he spoke to the boy who was standing there. "If that is the case, we had better get away from them if we can. Say nothing, but do as you see me do."

The afternoon was well along by then. The soldier walked down toward the bank without hurrying; two canoes were tied up there. Some of the Indians asked him where he was going. He pointed at the cattle, out among the haystacks on the far bank, and made signs that the beasts had to be foddered and that the two of them would be back for their supper afterward.

He took one canoe and the boy took the other. The stream is narrow there and they were over in no time. On the other side they pulled hay down for the cattle and made a show of gathering them in, working round by slow degrees until the haystacks stood between them and the house. Then they went into the woods, which began right at hand, and ran for the fort.

They had gone about a quarter of a mile when a gun went off behind them, and then a second one. They took it that both had been fired at the men they had left in the house.
