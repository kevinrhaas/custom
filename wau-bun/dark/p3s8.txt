The bob-o'-link runs off his rapid chattering flourish; the thrush lays a soft whistle over it; the wood-dove puts in her tender coo, and the grouse a deep warbling bass, and the partridge his drumming; then the lark's melodious trill, the robin's gay carol, and the friendly, familiar calling of the duck and the teal — and all of it coming at once out of the trees and the knolls and the low ground, until the simple half-breed says the only thing there is to say: "Voilà la forêt qui parle!" It seems as though a man could not help raising his own voice to take his part in the chorus — the mating song of praise.

Birds and flowers and the soft balmy air of the morning. Must it not have been in a scene like this one that Milton's Adam poured out that beautiful hymn of adoration — These are thy glorious works, Parent of Good?

Our hope for that day was to reach, early, the broad expanse of the Rock River that widens here into the Kosh-ko-nong. What that name means, in a language whose people's later fate makes it doubly affecting to say, is the lake we live on.

For the first part of the day the road ran through forest so thick and tangled that Grignon and Lecuyer had to go out ahead of us as pioneers with their axes, cutting away the shrubs and branches that shut the way. It was slow work and at times it was discouraging. We got through it at last, and came out into country of an entirely different kind — low prairie, cut across by deep narrow streams as straight as canals, and every one of them a delay for horses and a real difficulty for carriages.

Several times that forenoon the horses had to be taken out of the carriage and the carriage pulled and pushed over those deep narrow channels however it could be managed.

Nobody has ever been happier to see the wooded banks of the Kosh-ko-nong than we were when they finally came up in front of us. Five or six miles of riding through beautiful oak openings brought us to Man-Eater's village — a cluster of neat bark wigwams with wide fields on either side of it, corn and beans and squashes lately planted and already promising a fine crop. In front of it lay the broad blue lake. To the south its shores were open and marshy; near the village, and away north as far as we could see, they were lined with fine tall trees. The village stood a little below the point where the Rock River opens into the lake, and while our party talked with the Indians there, an arrangement was made for them to take us across at a place about half a mile above.

After a short halt we took up the line of march again through the woods, following the bank of the river.

A number of the Winnebagoes went by water toward the appointed place, paddling the largest and strongest of their canoes — we had been among our own people ever since we left Gros-pied Lake.

At the spot we dismounted and the men began unsaddling and unloading. We were put into the canoes first and paddled over to the far bank. Then the horses were swum across. Then the carriage. Two long wooden canoes were lashed firmly together side by side, wide enough between them for the carriage to stand upright, and the crossing began. Again and again those tottering hulls would lean, and a cry or a shout would go up from the party on shore as the whole mass looked to be going over sideways into the river — and then it would right itself, and after a good many departures from the perpendicular it came to shore safely.

Now we thought our troubles were finished and there was nothing left to do but mount up and trot for Fort Winnebago as fast as we could go. No. Half a mile on there was a swamp. It was not wide, that much is true. It was two to three feet deep in mud and water, and whether we could get the carriage across it at all was an open question. Several of the Indians came along with us to that place, partly to lend their help and advice, and partly for the pleasure of watching what happened.

When we got to it, we were inclined to laugh at all the formidable things that had been said about it. What we saw was a strip of somewhat low ground with tall dry rushes standing all over it.

The ground did look a little wet. There was nothing there to justify the alarm anyone had been raising. So it was a considerable surprise to me when my husband, who had been gone from us a few minutes, came back into the circle wearing his duck trousers, with no shoes and no stockings on.

"What are you going to do?" asked I.

"Carry you through the swamp on my shoulders. Come, Petaille, you are the strongest — you are to carry Madame Kinzie, and To-shun-nuck there," pointing to a tall, stout Winnebago, "he will take Madame Helm."

"Wait a moment," I said, and I sat down on the grass and took off my own boots and stockings without hurrying about it.

"What is that for?" they all asked.

"Because I don't wish to ride with wet feet all the rest of the day."

"No danger of that," they said, and not one of them did as I had done.

By the time they were out in the middle of it, they had reason to think better of my precaution. The water our bearers had to wade was deep enough that nothing the ladies could do kept their feet above the surface of it, and I had the satisfaction of knowing that my weight on my husband's shoulders was far less trouble to him than it might have been, since I could hold the position I had started in instead of shifting about constantly to stay clear of the water.

The laugh was entirely on my side when I put my boots and stockings back on and got up into my saddle dry-shod.

It will be seen that traveling in the woods is, to some extent, a deranger of ceremony and formality, and that it necessarily cuts into our conventionalities. The only remedy for it is to take a double share of them when we come back to the civilized walks of life.

By dint of a great deal of pulling and shouting and encouraging and threatening, the horses finally dragged the carriage through that ugly passage, and our red friends were free to go back to their village with what was certainly a very exaggerated and very amusing account of everything they had seen and had a hand in.

We had not forgotten our promise to Lieutenant Foster about a guide-board of some kind, to help him follow us. Several times we carried pieces out of the woods with us, three or four feet long, and planted them at certain points along the way, with a stick set crosswise through a cleft in the top to show which direction he and his party were to take.

We felt sure that, coming a few days behind us, he would find our trail and have the benefit of the same help we had had in getting through the hard places.
