The buildings of Fort Dearborn then were the buildings that stand there now. Better kept in those days, though a good deal of it was already run-down even then. They went up in 1816 with Captain Hezekiah Bradley overseeing the work, and there was a tale in circulation about him: that his regard for the Government's purse ran so high he made the soldiers whittle wooden pins to fasten the timbers rather than spend spikes and nails on them, and that he called the junior officers out to build alongside the enlisted men, whose work it properly was. If any of that is true, the captain was laboring under a delusion — a forgivable one in a man who had spent his life on the frontier — that the Government thanks its servants for an excess of economical zeal.

High pickets closed the place in, with bastions set at every other angle. Big gates faced north and south, and small posterns were cut here and there for the convenience of the people inside. The strip of river bank running west — the light-house buildings sit on it now, and docks close it in — held the garrison's root-houses. South of the pickets lay the parade-ground, and past that the company gardens, thick with currant-bushes and young fruit-trees.

You would take the fort to be standing at the river's mouth. It was not. The river in those days made a turn, ran around the promontory the fort was built on, bent south, and met the lake about half a mile lower down. The fort was on the right bank. The left was a long spit of land reaching out from the northern shore and forming part of it. In 1833 the United States Engineers, sent to build a harbor at that point, cut through that portion of the left bank and threw the piers out, and after that the water came over the whole tongue of land. It has been eating at the southern bank ever since and has taken many valuable acres off it, while the same motion of that vast body of water lays new ground down, constantly, on the north of the harbor.

Between the gardens and the river bank stood the house of Jean Baptiste Beaubien. Farther south there was a rickety tenement that Mr. John Dean, the sutler of the post, had put up many years before. Not long after Chicago began to grow, the lake worked in under its foundations until the whole thing went over backward down the bank, and there it lay for a long while afterward, a melancholy spectacle.

Straight across the water from the fort, on the north bank, was my husband's family mansion. It was long and low, a range of four or five rooms, with a piazza running the length of its front. A broad green space lay inclosed between it and the river, shaded by a row of Lombardy poplars. Behind the house stood two immense cottonwood-trees; one of them is standing yet, an ancient landmark. North of the house was a fine garden, well cultivated, and around it all the buildings the establishment required — dairy, bake-house, a lodging-house for the Frenchmen, and the stables.

Between the house and the lake ran a vast range of sand-hills, grown over with stunted cedars, pines and dwarf willows. The lake at that time was not more than thirty rods off.

Going on from there up the northern bank you came first to the Agency House. Cobweb Castle, it had been named during the long stretch when a bachelor lived in it, and the sobriquet stuck to it ever after. It stood where the southwest corner of Wolcott and N. Water Streets is now. Plenty of people still remember it: a substantial, compact little building of hewed and squared logs, a center with two wings, and — to be strictly accurate — two tails as well, because once the sides had no room left for additions, the additions went on behind, wherever a vacant spot could be found.

From the front none of that showed, and the whole thing looked symmetrical enough. Then the town improved itself, and a street was opened, maliciously, directly across the rear of the building, and after that the establishment with all its comical little adjuncts was a standing entertainment for anyone who passed by. No matter. A great many of Chicago's early settlers can testify to the pleasant, happy hours passed under that odd-shaped roof.

Log buildings stood grouped around the Agency House, quarters for the various men the Government employed there — blacksmith, striker, laborers. Canadians and half-breeds, most of them, with now and then a stray Yankee to set everything going by his activity and enterprise.

One more house stood on the north side of the river. A former resident named Miller had built it and then gone off to "Rivière du Chemin," or Trail Creek, which about that time people were beginning to call "Michigan City." His house, near the forks of the river, stood empty.

On the southern bank there was nothing at all between the fort and "The Point," which is what the forks of the river were called then. That ground was low wet prairie, hardly fit to walk on in the driest part of the summer and flatly impassable the rest of the year. A muddy little stream — a slew, in the language of this country — came winding around from about where the Tremont House stands now and fell into the river at the foot of State Street. At the Point, on the south side, Mark Beaubien had just finished a house: two stories, white, pretentious, with bright-blue wooden shutters, and the whole little circle at Wolf Point admired it. He kept a canoe ferry there to carry people across the south branch of the river.

Facing down the river from the west, the first thing was a small tavern kept by Mr. Wentworth, whom everyone knew as "Old Geese" — no reflection on his shrewdness, only a compliment to one of his own cant expressions. Close to him were two or three log cabins occupied by Robinson, the Pottowattamie chief, and some of his wife's connections. Billy Caldwell, the Sau-ga-nash, stayed here at times with his wife, who was a daughter of Nee-scot-nee-meg, one of the most famous chiefs of the nation. A little way off from these stood a small square log building meant originally for a school-house, though it served as a place of worship whenever some itinerant minister turned up.

The Clybourns had settled before this near where they live now, on the North Branch; they called their place New Virginia. Four miles up the South Branch stood an old building that had once drawn a great deal of interest, having been the theater of some stirring events in the troubles of 1812. It went by Lee's Place, or Hardscrabble. A settler named Heacock was living in it then.

The roads being bad the greater part of the year, anyone going between the fort and the Point generally went by water — a boat rowed up the river, or a canoe under some skilful hand. The same canoes kept up the intercourse between the people at the fort and those at the Agency House.

Two companies of soldiers held the garrison. Of the officers, Lieutenant Furman had died the autumn previous, and several more were away on furlough. Major Fowle and Captain Scott both being absent, the command devolved on Lieutenant Hunter. There were Lieutenants Engle and Foster besides him; Foster was unmarried. Dr. Finley, the post surgeon, was gone as well, and Dr. Harmon, a gentleman from Vermont, was supplying his place.

My husband's mother, his two sisters and his brother were living at the Agency House; the family residence near the lake had J.N. Bailey, the postmaster, in it.

The Dean House held a Mr. and Mrs. Forbes, who kept a school. Gholson Kercheval ran a small trading establishment out of one of the log buildings at Wolf Point, and John S.C. Hogan had charge of the sutler's store in the garrison.

There was also a Mr. See, lately come into the country, living at the Point, who would hold forth in the little school-house of a Sunday — less to the edification of the people listening than to the unmerciful slaughter of the "King's English."

That, I believe, is every white inhabitant of Chicago at a date less than half a century back. To a good many who read these pages the foregoing particulars will surely seem uninteresting. But for anyone who comes to see Chicago, and still more for anyone who comes to make it their home, there may be something in looking back at its first beginnings, in considering the almost magical change a handful of years has wrought, and in reading out of that past the marvelous prosperity still ahead of it.
