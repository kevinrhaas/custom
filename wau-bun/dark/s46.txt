Fort Dearborn in that day was the same collection of buildings that stands there now, in better preservation then, though even then considerably dilapidated. They had been erected in 1816 under the supervision of Captain Hezekiah Bradley, and there was a story about him that went round the post. His patriotic regard for the interests of the Government was such, people said, that he obliged the soldiers to fashion wooden pins instead of drawing spikes and nails out of stores, and to fasten the timbers of the buildings with those; and that he called on the junior officers to help in the construction alongside the men whose business it was. If it is true, the captain must have been laboring under the delusion — excusable in a man who has lived long on the frontier — that a Government thanks its servants for an excess of economical zeal.

The fort was inclosed by high pickets, with bastions at the alternate angles. Large gates opened to the north and the south, and there were small posterns here and there for the accommodation of the people inside. The bank of the river that stretches west, covered now by the light-house buildings and inclosed by docks, was then given over to the root-houses of the garrison. South of the pickets lay the parade-ground, and beyond that the company gardens, well filled with currant-bushes and young fruit-trees.

The fort stood at what anyone would naturally take for the mouth of the river. It was not. In those days the river came down, took a turn, and swept round the promontory the fort was built on, running south, and did not join the lake until half a mile below. The fort buildings stood on the right bank. The left was a long spit of land extending out from the northern shore, of which it formed a part. In 1833 the United States Engineers, employed to construct a harbor at this point, cut through that portion of the left bank and threw out the piers; after which the water overflowed the whole long tongue of land, and, encroaching steadily on the southern bank, robbed it of many valuable acres — while by the same action of that vast body of water an accretion was constantly taking place on the north of the harbor.

The residence of Jean Baptiste Beaubien stood in this period between the gardens and the river-bank. Farther south still was a rickety tenement, built many years before by Mr. John Dean, the sutler of the post. A short time after the growth of Chicago began, the lake worked in under its foundations by gradual encroachment, and the building tumbled backward down the bank and lay there a long while, a melancholy spectacle.

On the northern bank of the river, directly facing the fort, was the family mansion of my husband. It was a long, low building, a range of four or five rooms, with a piazza running the length of its front. Between it and the river lay a broad green space, inclosed, and shaded by a row of Lombardy poplars. Two immense cottonwood-trees stood in the rear of the building; one of them remains to this day, an ancient landmark. A fine, well-cultivated garden extended north from the house, and around it were grouped the various buildings belonging to the establishment — dairy, bake-house, lodging-house for the Frenchmen, and stables.

Between the house and the lake ran a vast range of sand-hills, grown over with stunted cedars, pines, and dwarf-willow-trees. The lake itself was at this time not more than thirty rods distant.

Proceeding from that point along the northern bank of the river, you came first to the Agency House — Cobweb Castle, as it had been denominated during the long years a bachelor lived in it, and the sobriquet adhered to it ever after. It stood at what is now the southwest corner of Wolcott and North Water Streets. Many will still remember it: a substantial, compact little building of logs hewed and squared, with a center, two wings, and, strictly speaking, two tails — because when there was found no more room for additions at the sides, they were put on in the rear, wherever a vacant spot could be found.

These appendages did nothing to mar the symmetry of the whole as viewed from the front. But when, in the process of the town's improvement, a street was maliciously opened directly in the rear of the building, the whole establishment with its comical little adjuncts became a constant source of amusement to everybody who passed. No matter. There were pleasant, happy hours passed under that odd-shaped roof, as a good many of Chicago's early settlers can testify.

Grouped around the Agency House was a collection of log buildings, the residences of the different people in the employ of the Government who belonged to that establishment — blacksmith, striker, and laborers. These were for the most part Canadians or half-breeds, with sometimes a stray Yankee among them, to set all things going by his activity and enterprise.

There was still another house on the north side of the river, built by a former resident by the name of Miller, but he had removed to the Rivière du Chemin, or Trail Creek, which about this time people were beginning to call Michigan City. His house stood near the forks of the river. It was standing empty.

On the southern bank, between the fort and the Point — the forks of the river went by that name then — there was no house at all. The land was a low wet prairie, scarcely affording good walking in the driest summer weather, and at other seasons absolutely impassable. A muddy streamlet, or, as it is called in this country, a slew, wound around from about the present site of the Tremont House and fell into the river at the foot of State Street. At the Point, on the south side, stood a house just completed by Mark Beaubien. It was a pretentious white two-story building with bright-blue wooden shutters, the admiration of all the little circle at Wolf Point. A canoe ferry was kept there to transport people across the south branch of the river.

Facing down the river from the west there was, first, a small tavern kept by Mr. Wentworth, familiarly known as Old Geese — not from any want of shrewdness on his part, but in compliment to one of his own cant expressions. Near him stood two or three log cabins occupied by Robinson, the Pottowattamie chief, and some of his wife's connections. Billy Caldwell, the Sau-ga-nash, lived there too at times, with his wife, who was a daughter of Nee-scot-nee-meg, one of the most famous chiefs of the nation. A little remote from these residences stood a small square log building, originally designed for a school-house, but used sometimes as a place of worship whenever any itinerant minister presented himself.

The family of Clybourn had before this time established themselves near their present residence on the North Branch; they called their place New Virginia. Four miles up the South Branch stood an old building that was at one time an object of great interest, having been the theater of some stirring events during the troubles of 1812. It was denominated Lee's Place, or Hardscrabble. A settler named Heacock was living in it at that time.

Because of the badness of the roads the greater part of the year, the usual mode of communication between the fort and the Point was by water — a boat rowed up the river, or a canoe paddled by some skilful hand. By the same means an intercourse was kept up between the residents of the fort and the Agency House.

There were at this time two companies of soldiers in the garrison, but the officers were thin on the ground. Lieutenant Furman had died the autumn previous. Several of the others were away on furlough. In the absence of Major Fowle and Captain Scott, the command devolved on Lieutenant Hunter. Besides him there were Lieutenants Engle and Foster, the latter unmarried. Dr. Finley, the post surgeon, was also absent, and his place was supplied by Dr. Harmon, a gentleman from Vermont.

My husband's mother, his two sisters and his brother lived at the Agency House. The family residence near the lake was occupied by J. N. Bailey, the postmaster.

In the Dean house lived a Mr. and Mrs. Forbes, who kept a school. Gholson Kercheval had a small trading establishment in one of the log buildings at Wolf Point, and John S. C. Hogan superintended the sutler's store in the garrison.

There was also a Mr. See, lately come into the country, living at the Point, who sometimes held forth in the little school-house on a Sunday — less to the edification of his hearers than to the unmerciful slaughter of the King's English.

That enumeration, I think, comprises all the white inhabitants of Chicago at a period less than half a century ago. To many who may read these pages the foregoing particulars will surely appear uninteresting. But to those who come to visit Chicago, and still more to those who come to make it their home, it may not be without interest to look back at its first beginnings, to contemplate the almost magical change a few years have wrought, and out of that past to augur the marvelous prosperity of the future.
