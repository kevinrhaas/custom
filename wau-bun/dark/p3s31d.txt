Somebody had to learn how badly the furs had suffered, so an early start was proposed. The people in the cottages, however, had grown weary in well-doing. They would not make us a breakfast, and it made no difference that we promised to pay them well for the trouble. We went down to the water with very slender prospects of a morning meal.

Then we saw the boat. Bedclothes, cloaks, trunks, the mess-basket, the packs of furs — every article of it carried the marks of a complete deluge, and the boat itself stood ankle-deep in water. There was not one place aboard where a person could sit or stand. We bailed for a while. We dragged out the packs of skins that had come off least badly and built them into a sort of divan amidships, and when that was done there was nothing left to do but climb in again and put up with what could not be helped.

It was not an enviable position. Set down a hand or a foot anywhere and the water came up around it with a soft bubbling noise. As for the bandboxes and the work-baskets, I will not describe them. Breakfast was out of the question: the mess-basket, when opened, held one indistinguishable mass, tea and pepper and salt and short-cake all afloat together, and there was no separating any of it.

And that was not the worst of it. The July sun climbed, fierce, and every object in the boat began to give off steam, until the air over us was nearly suffocating. The boat was old. The skins were old. Their company had been no pleasure on a dry day; now it was past bearing. There was nowhere on the river to go to get away from it, so we told the children to arm themselves with patience for the little of the voyage that was left.

We sat on that odoriferous couch under a single umbrella, which was all the shade our whole party had against the scorching sun, and slid down the current through the long tedious day. One by one the landmarks came and went — the Kakalin, the Rapids, Dickenson's, the Agency — and I looked at each roof we passed with a longing I am not ashamed of, thinking of the people under it with food in front of them, and counted over the hours that still lay between us and Fort Howard.

The Frenchmen gave us no songs that day. Music and fasting keep poor company. In time we came in to Shanty-town, where the boat was to be unloaded, and every hand fell to carrying the cargo up to the Fur Company's warehouse by the landing. The work went quickly because they all worked with a will. And the moment it was finished, every voyageur among them made ready to walk away. Mâtâ stormed and raved to no purpose. Arthur remonstrated to no purpose.

"No," they said, "they had brought the boat and cargo to the warehouse - that was all of their job." And they turned to go.

"Guardapié," I said, "do you intend to leave us here?"

"Bien, madame! it is the place we always stop at."

"Does Monsieur John pay you for bringing his family down?"

"Oh, yes, Monsieur John has given us an order on the sutler, at the Fort down below."

"To be paid when you deliver us safe at the Fort down below. It seems I shall be there before you, and I shall arrange that matter. Monsieur John never dreamed that this would be your conduct."

They put their heads together over that. What came of the conference was Guardapié and two others stepping back into the boat, taking up their oars, and rowing us the last two miles to Fort Howard, sulkily enough.
