We had to find out how far the water had got into the cargo of furs, so an early start was proposed. The people in the cottages, however, had grown weary of doing good. They declined to make us any breakfast, though we assured them they would be well paid for their trouble. So we saw ourselves compelled to leave with very slender prospects of a morning meal.

When we came down to the boat, we stopped where we stood. Bedclothes, cloaks, trunks, the mess-basket, the packs of furs — every one of them carried the mark of a complete deluge. The boat lay ankle-deep in water. There was literally no place on board where a person could either stand or sit. We bailed for a while. We dragged out the packs that had suffered least and arranged them across the center to make a sort of divan, and when that was done nothing better seemed to offer than to climb back in and endure what could not be cured.

Our position was not an enviable one. Wherever a foot or a hand went down, the water came up around it with a bubbling sound, and the state of the bandboxes and work-baskets does not bear describing. Breakfast there was none. When we opened the mess-basket, everything in it had been mingled into one undistinguishable mass — tea, pepper, salt, short-cake, all floating together. It was a hopeless case.

That was not the worst of it. As the fervid July sun climbed higher, the steam that came off every object on board was nearly suffocating. The boat was old. The packs of skins were old. Their company on a dry day had been anything but agreeable. Now it was intolerable, and there was no retreating from it, no corner of the boat where the air was any different. So we encouraged the children to arm themselves with patience for the short time that yet remained of our voyage.

Seated on our odoriferous couch, beneath the shade of a single umbrella that had to protect our whole party from the scorching sun, we glided wearily down the stream through that long, tedious day. We passed the Kakalin, then the Rapids, then Dickenson's, then the Agency. With what longing eyes we watched those human habitations go by, where other people were enjoying the shelter of a roof and the comforts of food — and how eagerly we counted the hours that must pass before we could reach Fort Howard.

There were no songs from the poor Frenchmen that day. Music and fasting do not go well together. In time we stopped at Shanty-town, where the boat was to be unloaded, and every hand fell to work transferring the cargo up to the warehouse of the Fur Company, which stood near the landing. It was not a long operation, for they all worked heartily. This being accomplished, the voyageurs, one and all, prepared to take their leave. In vain Mâtâ stormed and raved. In vain Arthur remonstrated.

"No," they said. They had brought the boat and cargo to the warehouse. That was all of their job. And they turned to go.

"Guardapié," I said, "do you intend to leave us here?"

"Bien, madame! It is the place we always stop at."

"Does Monsieur John pay you for bringing his family down?"

"Oh, yes. Monsieur John has given us an order on the sutler, at the Fort down below."

"To be paid when you deliver us safe at the Fort down below. It seems I shall be there before you, and I shall arrange that matter. Monsieur John never dreamed that this would be your conduct."

The Frenchmen drew together and consulted, and the result was that Guardapié and two others climbed into the boat, took up their oars, and rowed us, rather sulkily, the remaining two miles to Fort Howard.
