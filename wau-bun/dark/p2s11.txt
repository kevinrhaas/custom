There were chiefs among them who shared the general hostile feeling of their tribe toward the Americans and yet kept a personal regard for the troops at this post and for the few white citizens of the place. Those men spent everything they had of influence trying to cool the revengeful feelings of the young men and turn them off their sanguinary designs. It did no good at all.

On the evening after the council, Black Partridge, a conspicuous chief, came into the quarters of the commanding officer.

"Father," he said, "I come to deliver up to you the medal I wear. It was given me by the Americans, and I have long worn it in token of our mutual friendship. But our young men are resolved to imbrue their hands in the blood of the whites. I can't restrain them, and I won't wear a token of peace while I am compelled to act as an enemy."

If anyone in that devoted band had still needed proof that their melancholy anticipations were correct, they had it now. Even so, they went steadily on with the preparations that had to be made. And in the middle of all of it there were gallant hearts who worked at encouraging their desponding companions with hopes of escape they did not hold themselves.

Of ammunition, twenty-five rounds had been reserved, and one box of cartridges in the baggage-wagons. Against any real danger it would have been an inadequate supply. But they had a fatiguing march ahead of them in the state they were in, and could not afford to burden themselves with more.
