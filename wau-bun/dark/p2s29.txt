Chicago was not, when I first saw it, the cheerful and happy place it had been. Dr. Wolcott had died, and Lieutenant Furman, and a promising young son of Mr. Beaubien, the three of them within a few weeks of one another, and every branch of the little social circle lay under the gloom of it.

The season's weather was worse than anything anyone there could remember. In two months there were exactly two days on which the sun stayed out from morning to night. Even in the second week of April, when my husband set off to go back to Fort Winnebago, the storms were bad enough to drive him and his men into an Indian lodge, where they had to lie by for two or three days.

Robert Kinzie had gone off to the Calumet at that same hour to hunt, and Medard Beaubien and Billy Caldwell with him. Day after day passed with no word of them, and we made up our minds they had frozen to death. They came back at last, to our infinite joy. What had saved them was that Robert and Caldwell had each thought to take two blankets instead of one.

Riding was our one recreation, when the weather permitted it — through the woods that lie north of the river, or south across the prairie along the shore of the lake.

Going north, a little bridle-path took us along the line of what is now Rush Street. The boughs met thickly overhead the whole way, and often enough we had to break the shrubs back out of the path as we rode. We generally turned around at the little prairie that lies west of Wright's Woods.

Going south, we nearly always came on a new-comer, Dr. Harmon, standing over the building of a sod fence on a piece of ground he had chosen near the lake shore. As the season came on he took to planting fruit-stones of every description inside the inclosure, laying down a garden and an orchard against the years to come.

We would stop and talk a while. He had two subjects — horticulture, and the certainty that Chicago was going to matter. That it would one day be a great city he never doubted for a moment; and by that time, forest and prairie though the whole of it was, we had half come round to believing it ourselves.

When the afternoons turned pleasant, later in the season, we took up pistol-firing, which entertained us a good deal. It was done outside the pickets, with the mark set on a panel of one of the bastions. The gentlemen must forgive me for putting it on record, but the ladies in time got good enough to carry off the honors as a rule. One of them, Mrs. Hunter, was a great shot; she had brought down her grouse once on the wing, much to the delight of an officer named Captain Martin Scott, of raccoon celebrity.

Every so often the fort had a small sensation of its own. A settler would be found out selling the soldiers milk-punch under the name of milk, which interfered in no small degree with the regularity and the perfect discipline of the service. The first step was to drum the offender out with all the honors of war — a party-colored suit on his back and the Rogue's March playing behind him. The second was to shut up everyone he had imposed upon in the guard-house, where the lady of the commanding officer fed them coffee and hot cakes in quantity, so that their eyes might be opened to the enormity of what they had done. It is no wonder the officers complained now and then that the men competed to get into the guard-house rather than to stay out. They knew, the poor fellows, when they were well off.

We were rowed up to Wolf Point one Sunday to attend a religious service, held by a man everyone called Father See.

He was tall and thin, in a green frock-coat, and out of its sleeves hung a pair of hands that gave abundant evidence, as did the rest of his dress, that he had no great opinion of the axiom - "cleanliness is a part of holiness."

He stepped briskly up behind a table on a little platform and began. His subject was "The fear of God."

"There was a kind of fear," he told us, "that was very nearly a_lee_-a-nated to love: so nearly, that it wasn't worth while splitting hairs for the difference." Then he set about describing that kind of fear. The description tangled as he went, and tangled further, until he stopped altogether, thoroughly lost, and said, "Come, let's stop a little while, and clear away the brush." He picked apart the thread of his ideas as well as he could and started off again. Before long he had lost his way a second time and came to another halt. He mopped the perspiration off his forehead with a red cotton handkerchief that was many degrees from clean, and said, "Now, suppose we drive back a little piece." So he went back over what he wished to impress upon us: that we must cherish the fear that maketh wise unto salvation, "which fear," he said, "may we all enjoy, that together we may soar away, on the rolling clouds of aether, to a boundless and happy eternity, which is the wish of your humble servant." Then he flung his hands wide, made the best of dancing-school bows, and sat down.

We came away satisfied that our own religious exercises at home were the more edifying of the two, and we kept to them after that.

Our brother Robert Kinzie came back from Palestine — the seat of the Land Office, not the Holy Land — carrying the certificate of the family's title to the piece of Chicago that has since been called "Kinzie's Addition." We took it as the making of a home for us some day, if good Dr. Harmon and a few others should turn out to be right in their glorious dreams. One small incident will show how modest the expectations of most people were at that period.

The certificate stood in Robert's name, since it was he who had applied on the family's behalf. What it described was a fractional quarter-section — one hundred and two acres, not one hundred and sixty, because the river and Lake Michigan cut fifty-eight acres off the southern and the eastern lines of it. An applicant in that case was at liberty to take his missing fifty-eight acres wherever he liked, out of whatever land had not been appropriated already.

"Now, my son," said his mother to Robert, "lay your claim on the corn-field at Wolf Point. It is fine land, and will always be valuable for cultivation, besides, as it faces down the main river, the situation will always be a convenient one."

Robert laughed out loud at her. "Hear mother!" he said. "We have just got a hundred and two acres - more than we shall ever want, or know what to do with, and now she would have me go and claim fifty-eight acres more!"

"Take my advice, my boy," his mother said again, "or you may live one day to regret it."

"Well, I can't see how I can ever regret not getting more than we can possibly use." That closed the matter. Nobody ever claimed the fifty-eight acres, and I think there was a general feeling that to ask for what was ours by right would have had a grasping, covetous look about it. Five-and-twenty years have made us a great deal wiser.

I stayed two months at Chicago, and our mother spent many of those evenings telling me about her early life and the adventures in it. What follows is her account of her captivity among the Senecas. I have set it down in the form of a tale, but with not the slightest variation from the facts as she gave them to me, and from her sister — the little Maggie of the story — Mrs. William Forsyth, of Sandwich (C.W.).
