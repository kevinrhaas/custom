All of that had cost us so much of the day that our afternoon's ride came to very little. We got no farther than the shore of a beautiful sheet of water that people now call Crystal Lake. Its clear surface was covered over with loons, and with Poules d'Eau — a kind of rail that swarms in this region at certain seasons of the year.

The Indians have the genius of Aesop for the character of animals. There is not a peculiarity in the look or the habits or the disposition of any creature that they do not have a fable ready to account for.

The back of the little rail is deeply hollowed, scooped inward. The Indians say it came to be that way like this. Most of the Northwestern tribes hold that there is an invisible being among them, something on the order of the Genie in the stories of the East. Nan-nee-bo-zho is not exactly the father of evil. His office is to punish whatever is amiss, and he is described as forever busy trapping the animals that cross his path and making examples of them.

One fine evening, walking along the bank of a lake, he saw a flock of ducks out on the blue water, sailing about and enjoying themselves. He called to them.

"Ho! come with me into my lodge, and I will teach you to dance!" Some of the ducks said among themselves, "It is Nan-nee-bo-zho, let us not go." Others thought differently. His words were fair and his voice was easy to listen to, and a few of them turned their faces toward the land. The rest followed soon after, and the whole flock came trooping up the bank behind him with a good deal of pleasant quacking, and went into his lodge.

Once they were inside, the first thing he did was take up an Indian sack with a wide mouth and tie it by the strings around his neck, so that it hung down over his shoulders with the mouth of it left open. Then he stood himself in the center of the lodge and arranged the ducks in a circle around him.

"Now," he said, "you must all shut your eyes tight, whoever opens his eyes at all, something dreadful will happen to him. I will take my Indian flute and play upon it, and you will, at the word I shall give, open your eyes, and begin dancing, as you see me do."

The ducks did as they were told. They shut their eyes tight and kept the time by stepping from one foot to the other, all of them impatient for the dancing to start.

Then there came a sound like a quack with something over it. None of them dared open their eyes.

The flute kept breaking off. Each time it broke off there was that gurgling cry again — qu-a-a-ck. There was one little duck among them, much smaller than the others, and at about this point she could not stand it any longer and opened one eye, carefully. She saw Nan-nee-bo-zho playing his flute with one hand, and stooping down every so often to take hold of the duck nearest him, and throttle it, and push it into the bag on his shoulders. She edged herself a little way out of the circle, toward the door, which had been left partly open to let in the light, and then she called out — "Open your eyes — Nan-nee-bo-zho is choking you all and putting you into his bag!"

And with that she flew. Nan-nee-bo-zho came down on her. His hand closed over her back, and she tore herself out of it by main force and got into the open air. Her companions came screaming and quacking out after her. Some of them escaped. Some of them stayed with the sprite.

The little duck had saved her life and lost her beauty. She kept forever after the shape she had been forced into in that one moment — her back pressed down in the middle, her head and neck stretched out unnaturally into the air.
