One of the first women I got to know was the wife of Wau-kaun-zee-kah, the Yellow Thunder. She had gone along with her husband, who was one of the delegation that visited the President, and ever since she had been known as "the Washington woman." There was a pleasant, old-acquaintance air about the way she greeted me, as much as to say, "You and I have both seen something of the world." Not a word of surprise or admiration passed her lips while her companions exclaimed and clapped their hands with childlike, laughing simplicity at the various wonderful things I showed them. Her manner said plainly, "Yes, yes, my children, I have seen all these things before." It was not until I held to her ear a tropical shell out of the little cabinet of them I had, and she heard the murmuring sound inside, that she dropped her air of indifference. She poked her finger into the opening to get at the animal she supposed was in there, shook the shell violently, raised it to her ear again, and at last burst out laughing and put it down, admitting by her looks that this was past her understanding.

I had one shell of unusual beauty — my favorite in the whole collection — a small conch covered with rich dark veins. Every one of the visitors picked this shell up in turn and expressed her admiration in words and gestures, plainly showing she had an eye for beauty. This was on the occasion of my red daughters' farewell visit.

Not long after the payment was finished and the Indians had left, I found that my treasured shell was missing from the collection. Could one of the squaws have stolen it? It was possible; they would occasionally, though rarely, do such a thing under strong temptation. I tried to remember which of the party looked likeliest to be the culprit. It could not have been the Washington woman — she was partly civilized, and knew better.

A few weeks later Mrs. Yellow Thunder appeared again, and carefully unfolding a gay-colored chintz shawl that she carried rolled up in her hand, she produced the shell and laid it on the table in front of me. I could not decide whether to let my face show displeasure at the trick she had played on me or joy at getting my treasure back, but I concluded in the end that the best policy was to show no feeling at all.

She stayed on until my husband came home, and he then questioned her about it.

"She had taken the shell to her village, to show it to some of her people who had not come to the payment."

"Why had she not asked her mother's leave before carrying it off?"

"Because she saw that her mother liked the shell, and she was afraid her mother would say no."

This was not the first time Madame Washington had shown the shrewdness that was the leading trait in her character. During the Indians' visit to the Eastern cities they were taken to all sorts of exhibitions, museums, menageries, theatres, and the like. It did not escape their notice that some silver was always handed over before anyone was let in, and they asked why. It was explained to them. The woman brightened up, as if an idea had struck her.

"How much do you pay for each one?"

Her Father told her.

"How do you say that in English?"

"Two shillings."

"Two shinnin — humph." (Good.)

The next day, when visitors began as usual to crowd to the rooms where the Indians were staying, the woman and a young Indian who was her confederate took up their station by the door and kept it shut. Whenever anyone knocked, the door was opened cautiously, and the woman put out her hand and said, "Two shinnin."

It was paid without argument every time, and the game went on until she had built up a considerable sum. But that did not satisfy her. The first time a visitor tried to leave the room, the door was held shut just as before, the hand came out, and "Two shinnin" was in his ear again. He tried to explain that having paid to come in, he ought to be free to go out. She shook her head innocently: "Two shinnin" was all the English she could understand.

The Agent, who had come in a little earlier and had overheard the whole exchange, sat laughing behind his newspaper, waiting to see how it would end. Now he came forward and intervened, and the visitors were allowed to leave without paying a second contribution.

The good woman was also given to understand that it is not at all the custom among white people to tax their friends and visitors this way, and that the practice would have to be dropped in future.

Here is another instance of the Indians' readiness to make the most of any good thing fortune puts in their way.

On the same trip, while they were passing through Ohio, one of the party asked the Agent, "Do you pay for all that food they set in front of us at the hotels?"

"Yes. Why do you ask?"

"Nothing. I thought perhaps you only paid for what we ate of it."

At the next stopping-place a fine breakfast was laid on the table, and as usual they ate plentifully of it. Just as they had finished, the horn sounded for everyone to take their places in the stage-coaches. Every man sprang to his feet. One seized the plates of biscuits and poured them into a corner of his blanket; another took what was left of a pair of chickens; a third emptied the sugar-bowls; each laid hold of whatever was nearest him; and in a moment nothing was left on the table but empty plates and dishes. The landlord and the waiters stood there laughing, enjoying the trick as much as anyone watching.

On another occasion their Father had tried to impress on them how unseemly it was to throw their scraps, bones, and leftovers about on the table-cloth. He pointed out how orderly white people are at table, and how proper it is to keep everything neat and clean around you.

At their next meal they were served first with a chicken-pie, of which they ate very heartily, and the bones piled up abundantly on their plates. Then a second and more popular dish appeared — a fine large roast turkey. A gentleman sitting near it was plainly getting ready to carve. There was no time to lose. What was to be done with the bones? They looked around in some perplexity. A large apple-pie stood nearby. The most eager of them pulled it toward him, and quick as thought the bones all went onto it; then, with a triumphant laugh at his own happy idea, he coolly moved the bird over to his own dish and set about sharing it out among his companions. The astonished stranger soon joined in the laughter at the unceremonious way his share of the dinner had vanished.
