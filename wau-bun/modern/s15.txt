It was hard pulling for the men up the rapids. Wish-tay-yun's clear, carrying voice was the bugle of the party; he shouted and whooped, and every man answered with a chorus and a harder effort. Every so often the boat would jam solid between two big stones.

"Whoop la! Whoop! Whoop!"

Another pull, and another, every nerve strained — nothing.

"She won't budge!"

"Men, overboard!" And in an instant every rower is over the side and in the water.

By hauling and shoving and wrestling, the boat is finally freed, and the men walk alongside her, helping and steering her, until they reach a stretch of comparatively smooth water and take their seats and their oars again.

You can imagine there were not many songs that day. There were, however, very frequent pipes, to give the poor fellows some relief after work like that.

The whole thing was new to me. The truth is I had almost never been made to watch hard physical labor before, and my sympathy and my nerves were the more engaged because of it. Having to work that violently seemed hardship enough by itself. But to wade in cold water up to your waist and then sit down again in soaking clothes without going near a fire — poor men, it was too much for anybody to bear. Imagine my horror, then, when my husband — who a little after our noon meal had startled me by appearing in duck trousers and a light jacket — went over the side with the men at the first shout of "Fast again!" and bore a hand for the whole rest of that long stretch.

When he came back aboard, it was to take the oar of a poor, delicate-looking boy in the company of soldiers who had been bleeding at the nose from the beginning at every unusual exertion. I was not surprised to learn, when I asked, that the boy was a recruit who had only just entered the service. He went by the name Gridley, which was certainly not his own. He had the look of someone who had been delicately raised, and he had probably enlisted with no idea at all of the hardship and discomfort of a soldier's life. That much is plain from the dissatisfaction he never stopped feeling, until in the end he deserted his post. That was some months after the time I am writing about. He was caught once and held a while in confinement, and the moment he was released he deserted again. His remains were found the following spring, not many miles from the fort. He had died of cold or of starvation. A sad interruption — let us get back to the boat.
