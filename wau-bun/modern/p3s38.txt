The war was now reckoned to be over. The news of the battle of the Bad Axe, where the regulars, the militia and the steamboat Warrior together finished off the last handful of Sauks, had reached us and brought peace of mind back to the hearts and homes of the frontier settlers.

It may seem astonishing that an enemy so small in numbers and so poor in resources could have caused such a panic, and taken such an enormous force to put down. The trouble was simply that nobody ever knew where to find them, either to attack them or to guard against them. At the start, probably every military man thought and felt exactly as that fine old veteran General Brady did. "Give me two infantry companies mounted," he said, "and I will engage to whip the Sauks out of the country in one week!"

True enough — but before you can whip an enemy you have to meet him, and to pursue Indians effectively and actually catch them takes a particular kind of training, which in those days very few men could claim, even among the frontier militia.

In some parts of this campaign there was another problem: the two branches of the service would not work together. The regular troops looked down on the militia's unprofessional way of doing things, and the militia jeered at the regulars' slow, useless formalities. Each side declared it was convinced the whole business would go much better without the other, and the militia, being quick to act, sometimes took matters into their own hands and produced defeat and disgrace, as in the affair of "Stillman's Run."

The contempt the army officers felt for the militia spread down to their subordinates and their servants. After the Ranger officers had visited Fort Winnebago, before the battle of the Wisconsin, the officer of the mess that had entertained them called in his servant one day to go over the sutler's accounts. He was the same little "Yellow David" who had belonged to Captain Harney before.

"David," the young gentleman said, "I see three bottles of cologne-water charged in the month's account of the mess at the sutler's. What does that mean?"

"If you please, lieutenant," said David, respectfully, "it was to sweeten up the dining-room and quarters after them milish' officers were here visiting."
