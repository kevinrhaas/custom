She came one day with a half-grown boy along, carrying a young fawn she had brought me as a present. I was delighted with the pretty creature — the soft eyes, the dappled coat — but I had heard the phrase "as wild as a fawn" often enough that I didn't expect much success in taming it. To my great surprise, it soon learned to follow me about like a dog. Wherever I went, Fan was sure to be there. At breakfast she would lie down at my feet under the table. One of her first tokens of affection was to gnaw all the trimming off my black silk apron while she lay there pretending to caress me and make much of me. And that was not her only line of mischief.

One day we heard a great rattling among the crockery in the kitchen. We ran to see what was the matter and found that Miss Fan had climbed up onto a shelf of the dresser, about two feet off the ground, and was trying to work out a comfortable place to lie down among the plates and dishes. I soon noticed that what really attracted her was the shelter of the shelf over her head, and that she was in the habit of hunting out a resting place under a chair, or anything else that came close to an "umbrageous bower." So after that, as the hour for her morning nap came round, I took care to open a big green parasol and stand it on the matting in the corner — and then when I called "Fan, Fan," she would come and nestle under it and be fast asleep in no time.

One morning Fan was missing. We called and hunted for her, all for nothing — in the garden, in the enclosure for the cattle, at the Frenchmen's houses, along the hill toward Paquette's — no Fan was to be found anywhere. We supposed she had asserted her own wild nature and gone off to the woods.

It was a hot forenoon, and all the doors stood open. About dinner-time Fan came rushing in, panting violently, and threw herself down on her side, where she lay with her legs stretched out and her mouth foaming, showing every sign of mortal agony. We tried to give her water and to soothe her, in case it was only fright that had done it — but in a few minutes, with a gasp and a spasm, she was dead. Whether the greyhounds had chased her, or whether she had eaten some poisonous weed that had brought on the suffering and sent her running to her best friends for help, we never found out. But we had lost our pretty pet, and a good many tears were shed for her.
