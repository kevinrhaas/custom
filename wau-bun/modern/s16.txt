For all that hauling and toiling, we had made only thirteen miles since leaving the Kakalin, and it was already late when we came in sight of the Grande Chûte, near which we were going to camp.

We had passed the Little Chûte — the spot where the town of Appleton stands now — without anything more to say about it than that it took an enormous amount of extra effort to fight that rushing water and come out on top, which we did.

The brilliant light of the setting sun lay on the high wooded banks the beautiful foaming water of the Chûte came dashing down between. The boat was swung quickly toward a small headland jutting out from the left bank, which had the advantage of a long strip of level ground, roomy enough to make a good campsite. I jumped ashore before the men had properly pulled the boat up and, with the Judge's help, got as fast as I could to a point lower down the river where he said the best view of the Chûte was. I wanted a sketch before the daylight went entirely.

The left bank was to the west, and over a lower section of it the sun's last rays fell on the boat, on the men in their red caps and belts, and on the two tents already pitched. The smoke starting to rise from the evening fires, the high wooded bank beyond with the steep portage path just visible going up it, and, farther off still, the long run of falling water going dark in the shadow of the overhanging forest — together they made a lovely landscape that only an artist's pencil could have done justice to.

This was my first camp, and every new thing about it enchanted me.

The fires had been built out of small saplings and underbrush gathered in a hurry; the weather was mild enough that anything beyond what was needed for cooking and drying the men's clothes would have been wasted. The soldiers' tent was pitched some distance from ours, but not so far that we could not clearly hear them laughing and plainly enjoying themselves after the day's work.

Under Corporal Kilgour's careful management, though, their high spirits never crossed the line of respectful propriety, and by the time we had eaten our suppers — cooked in the open air with nothing but a tea-kettle and a frying pan — every one of us was ready to turn in.

The first sound to reach us at first light the next morning was the bourgeois's long call:

"How! how! how!" — at the absolute top of his voice.

Everyone jumps at that summons. The men come piling out of their tents or wake up beside the fire and start getting ready for the day.

The fire is fed, the kettles go on to boil, the mess-baskets are opened and part of what is in them is brought out for breakfast. One of the Frenchmen spreads our mat inside the tent, from which the bedding has already been carefully cleared and packed for stowing in the boat. Tin cups and plates go around on that newfangled tablecloth. The heavy dew makes it a little too damp to eat outside; otherwise everything would be laid out on the green grass. In an unbelievably short time our steaming coffee and broiled ham are in front of us, with slices of toast added now and then, hot and fresh off the coals.

When all is said and done, there is no breakfast like a breakfast in the woods with a well-trained Frenchman running the ceremony.

What the men turned to now was a hard day's work: dragging that heavy boat up the Chûte. Leaving the piano aboard was judged safest, but everything else had to be carried up the steep bank and along the top of it for several hundred rods to the smooth water beyond, where the difficulties of our navigation finally ended.

The Judge kindly took charge of me while the bourgeois oversaw that important business, and between reading, sketching and walking about, the morning slid by. Noon came, and the preparations for starting still were not finished.

Wandering around looking for the best of the wild flowers to make a bouquet before my husband came back, I came on the soldiers' campfire. A tall, red-faced, light-haired young man in fatigue dress was tending a kettle of soup, and the smell coming off it was very attractive.

Seeing me watching him work, he politely ladled out a tin cup of it and offered it to me.

I declined, saying we would be having our own dinner shortly.

"They left me here to get their dinner," he said, plainly not sorry to have somebody to talk to, "and I thought I might as well make some soup. Down on the German Flats, where I come from, they always like soup."

"Ah, you're from the German Flats — then your name must be Bellinger or Weber."

"No, it ain't — it's Krissman."

"Well, Krissman, how do you like the service?"

"Very well. I was only recruited last summer. I used to ride horse on the Canawl, and since I can blow a horn first-rate, I expect I'll soon be able to play a bugle, and then, when I get to be musician, you know, I'll have extra pay."

I did not know it, but I expressed suitable pleasure at the news, and wishing Krissman every success in his ambitions — or rather in his avarice, since the hope of extra pay clearly outweighed the hope of glory — I went back to my own quarters.

My husband, with his French tastes, was a little disappointed when I told him about the encounter and about refusing Krissman's soup. But we were soon rewarded by the sight of that tall, awkward figure carrying over a kettle of the stuff, which he set down in front of the two gentlemen, who pronounced it excellent, to his enormous satisfaction.

Everything was finally ready, the tents were struck and carried around the Portage, and my husband, the Judge and I followed at our own pace.

The woods were brilliant with wild flowers, even though it was so late in the season that the glory of the summer was nearly over. The lupin, the moss-pink and the yellow wallflower were out, and every variety of the helianthus, the aster and the solidago, spreading their color all around us. The gentlemen picked clusters of bittersweet — celastrus scandens — from the boughs hanging over us to make a wreath for my hat as we walked the tangled path, which, like Christabel's, was

"Now in glimmer and now in gloom,"

as it passed from open glade into shady thicket and out again. Soon, like that same lovely heroine,

"We reached the place — right glad we were,"

and without any further delay we were back aboard our little boat, skimming over water that had gone entirely calm.
