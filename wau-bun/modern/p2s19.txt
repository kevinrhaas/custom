At their own house Mr. Kinzie's family were closely guarded by their Indian friends, who meant to take them to Detroit where they would be safe. The rest of the prisoners stayed in the wigwams of the men who had captured them.

The next morning, once the plundering was finished, the Indians set fire to the fort. The finery seemed to have been shared out very fairly, and shawls and ribbons and feathers were fluttering about in every direction. One young fellow had got himself up in a muslin gown and one of the ladies' bonnets, and in any other circumstances the sight of him would have been something to laugh at.

Black Partridge, Wau-ban-see and Kee-po-tah, with two other Indians, posted themselves in the porch of the house as sentries, to protect the family from any harm the young men might be worked up to do. For a short while after the fire everything stayed quiet.

Very soon, though, a party of Indians from the Wabash turned up. These were far and away the most hostile and unforgiving of all the Pottowattamie bands.

Living further off, they had shared less than some of their fellows in the kindness of Mr. Kinzie and his family, and so their feelings of regard for them were much weaker.

Runners had been sent out to the villages to tell them that the post was going to be evacuated, and about the plan the assembled Indians had made to attack the troops.

Thirsting to have a share in a scene like that, they hurried on, and it was a bitter mortification when they reached the river Aux Plaines and met a party of their friends coming the other way with their chief Nee-scot-nee-meg badly wounded, and learned that the battle was over, the spoils divided, and all the scalps taken.

When they got to Chicago they blackened their faces and went on toward Mr. Kinzie's house.

Black Partridge had been watching them come from his station on the piazza, and he was especially afraid for Mrs. Helm, Mr. Kinzie's step-daughter, who had only recently come to the post and was not known personally to the Indians from further away. On his advice she was dressed in the ordinary clothes of a Frenchwoman of that country — a short gown and petticoat, with a blue cotton handkerchief wrapped around her head. In this disguise Black Partridge himself took her to the house of Ouilmette, a Frenchman with a half-breed wife who belonged to Mr. Kinzie's establishment and whose house was close at hand.

As it happened, this was the first house the Indians came to when they went looking for prisoners. As they came up, the people inside were afraid that Mrs. Helm's fair complexion and general appearance would give her away as an American, so they raised a large feather bed and pushed her under the edge of it on the bedstead, with her face to the wall. Mrs. Bisson, a half-breed and the sister of Ouilmette's wife, then sat down on the front of the bed with her sewing.

It was a hot day in August, and between the feverish agitation of being frightened and the position she was in, which nearly suffocated her, it grew so unbearable that Mrs. Helm finally begged them to let her out and give her up to the Indians.

"I can but die," she said. "Let them put an end to my misery at once."

Mrs. Bisson answered, "Your death would be the destruction of us all, for Black Partridge has resolved that if one drop of your family's blood is spilled, he will take the lives of everyone concerned in it, even his nearest friends; and once the work of murder starts, there will be no end of it as long as there is one white person or half-breed left in the country."

That argument gave Mrs. Helm fresh resolution.

The Indians came in, and now and then she could see them from her hiding-place, gliding about and stealthily looking over every part of the room, though without making any open search, until, apparently satisfied that nobody was concealed there, they left the house.

The whole time, Mrs. Bisson had kept her seat on the side of the bed, calmly sorting and arranging the patch-work of the quilt she was working on, looking perfectly tranquil, though for all she knew the next moment might bring a tomahawk into her brain. Her self-command undoubtedly saved the lives of everyone in the house.
