A few weeks after we got back, my husband took his mother down to Prairie du Chien so that she could get medical advice from Dr. Beaumont of the U.S. Army.

The trip was made in a large open boat down the Wisconsin River, and the idea was to use the chance to bring back a good supply of corn for the winter, for the men and the cattle both.

But the ice formed in the river so early that after he had started out with his load he had to turn back with it to the Prairie and wait until the thick winter ice let him make a second trip and bring it up by sleigh. That is what it cost in time, labor, and exposure to move the plain necessities of life from one point to another in that wild, empty country.

My brother Arthur arrived from Kentucky by way of the Mississippi at the end of April, and brought us the unwelcome news of fresh trouble with the Sauks and Foxes. Black Hawk had crossed back over the Mississippi with the best men of his nation, to take possession of their old homes and cornfields again. It was not long before our own Indians came flocking in to confirm it, and to assure us that they meant to stay faithful friends to the Americans. Soon we heard that the Illinois Rangers had reached the Rock River country, and that the regular force under General Atkinson was making its way after the hostile Indians, who by every report kept slipping out of their reach. It was not the Sauks' way to stop and give battle, so they scattered themselves through the country and trusted to some lucky chance — and such chances came, unfortunately, only too often — to let them fall on their enemies without warning.

For the most part, what the pursuing army did was to make its way by long, exhausting marches to the spot where it supposed the Sauks would be waiting to receive it, and then discover that the rogues had run off to a completely different part of the country.

Wherever the Sauks went, their track was marked by the most atrocious cruelties, though the worst of it hadn't reached us at this point. All we knew for certain was that they were down around the Rock River and the Kishwaukee, and that they let no chance go by of falling on defenceless settlers and murdering them.

As soon as it was clear that the Sauks and Foxes were not going to do what they had done the year before — retreat peaceably across the Mississippi — Mr. Kinzie decided to hold a council with all the principal Winnebago chiefs he could reach at that time. He knew the Sauks would try every argument to get their neighbors to join them, and that too many of the young savages had it in them to want to distinguish themselves by "taking some white scalps." They didn't love the Americans. Why should they? The Americans had gradually dispossessed them of the broad and beautiful country their forefathers held, and hunted them from place to place, and the only thing they had received in exchange was a few thousand dollars a year in silver and presents — that, along with the pernicious example, the debasing influence, and the outright ill treatment of far too many of the new settlers on their lands.
