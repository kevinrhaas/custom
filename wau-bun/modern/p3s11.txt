In time, though, permission came through to build a house for the blacksmith — that is, for the man the Government kept on pay at this station to mend the Indians' guns, traps and so on.

It was very lucky for us that Monsieur Isidore Morrin was a bachelor, and perfectly content to go on boarding with his friend Louis Frum, called Manaigre, so that once the new house was fairly under way we planned it and pushed it along entirely for our own benefit.

It wasn't much of a house, admittedly — just a parlor and two bedrooms on the ground floor, two low chambers under the roof, and a kitchen at the back. But next to the rambling old stable of a building we were living in, it looked like a palace.

Before it was finished, Mr. Kinzie got word that the money for the annual Indian payment was waiting for him at Detroit, for him to take charge of and see safely carried to the Portage, and he had to leave at once to do that part of his duty.

The workmen who had been brought over from the Mississippi to put up the main building were perfectly able to carry on without anyone standing over them, but the kitchen was to be the Frenchmen's job, and the question was how that could be got done with the bourgeois away.

"You'll have to make do in the old quarters until I get back," my husband said, "and then we'll soon have everything in order."

His trip was going to be a long, tedious one, because the Government did not carry on its business by railroad and telegraph in those days.

After he had gone I said to the men, "Come now — your logs are all cut and hauled, the squaws have brought the bark for the roof — what's to stop us finishing the house and getting everything moved and settled, to surprise Monsieur John when he comes back?"

"Ah, to be sure, Madame John," said Plante, who always did the talking, "provided whoever plants a green bough on the chimney-top gets a treat."

"Certainly. Everybody get to work, and let's see who wins the treat."

With that inducement held out to whoever put the last stroke to the building, Plante, Pillon, and Manaigre — whom the waggish Plante insisted on calling "mon nègre" whenever he judged himself out of reach of the other man's arm — all set to work with a will.

Building a log house is a curious sort of process. First, as you would expect, the logs are laid one on top of another and jointed at the corners until the walls have reached the height they need. The chimney is made of four poles of the right length, laced together with a wickerwork of small branches. A hole or pit is dug close by, and clay and water are mixed in it into a kind of mortar. Big wisps of hay are filled with this thick stuff and shaped by hand into what are technically called "clay cats," and these are packed in among the framework of the chimney until there isn't a chink left anywhere. The whole thing is then given a smooth coat of the wet clay, which goes by the name of "plastering."

Between the logs that make up the walls of the building, small bits of wood are driven in close together; that is called "chinking," and once it is done the clay cats go in and are smoothed over with the plaster. When it has all dried, the walls and the chimney are whitewashed, and they look comfortable and tidy.

The roof is made by laying thick sheets of bark across the transverse logs. Around the chimney, for extra safety against the rain, we were careful to have a few layers laid of the palisades left over from when Mr. Peach, an odd little travelling genius, had fenced in our garden — the pride and the wonder of the settlement and the wigwams round about.
