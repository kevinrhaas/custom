Our next day's stage ran from the Kakalin to the Butte des Morts, where a man named Knaggs lived. The country was rough and wild, a good deal like what we had come through the spring before on the way from Hamilton's diggings to Kellogg's Grove — but we had the good luck to have Wish-tay-yun for our guide instead of "Uncle Billy," so we could make our way at a reasonable pace.

We had only covered forty miles when we got to Knaggs's, and yet I was both cold and tired, so the cosy little room where we found Mrs. Knaggs, and the bright fire, were the most cheering sight in the world. Since all we had eaten since morning was a few crackers we carried in our pockets, I must admit we did full justice to her good coffee and cakes — to say nothing of the venison steaks and the bear's meat, which I had never tasted before.

Supper over, we looked about for somewhere to sleep. The room we had eaten in was small, just big enough to hold a bed, a table pushed against the wall, and the few chairs we were sitting on. There was no room for a "shakedown" of any sort.

"Where can you put us for the night?" my husband asked Mr. Knaggs when he turned up.

"Why, there's no place I know of, unless you can camp down in the old building outside."

We went to have a look at it. It was one room, bare and dirty. A huge chimney with a few brands burning in it took up nearly one whole side. Against another side was built a rickety sort of bunk. That was the only trace of furniture in the place. The floor was thick with mud and dirt, and in the middle of it, near the fire, sat an old Indian with a pan of boiled corn in his lap, scooping it up with both hands and devouring it as fast as he could.

We soon realized that he was blind. Hearing footsteps and voices, he instinctively pulled his dish of food in close to him and began some sullen grumbling — but when he was told that it was "Shaw-nee-aw-kee" speaking to him, his face relaxed into a pleasanter expression, and he even held out his dish and invited us to share what was in it.

"But are we to stay here?" I asked. "Can't we sleep outdoors?"

"We have no tent," my husband said, "and the weather is too cold to risk being out without one."

"I could sit in a chair by the fire all night."

"Then you wouldn't be fit to ride to Bellefontaine tomorrow."

There was no alternative. The only thing in the way of bedding that Mr. Knaggs could produce was a small bear-skin. The bunk was a shade less filthy than the floor, so on its boards we spread the skin first, then our saddle-blankets, and with a pair of saddle-bags for a bolster I wrapped myself in my cloak and resigned myself to my distasteful accommodations.

Simply changing position after the day I had spent probably brought me some rest, but sleep was out of the question. Even on a softer and pleasanter bed, the snoring of the old Indian and the two or three companions who had joined him, and his frequent peevish exclamations whenever he felt himself crowded in the dark, would have driven sleep away completely.

It was a relief to get up in the early morning and prepare for the day's travel. Where our fellow-travelers had stowed themselves I do not know, but they clearly had fared no better than we had. They were in fine spirits, though, and we cheerfully ate our breakfast and were ferried across the river to take up the trail from there to Bellefontaine, twelve miles from Fort Winnebago.
