The commandant at Fort Pitt threw himself warmly into Mr. Lytle's business and readily gave him a detachment of soldiers to help him and his friends go after the marauders. Some things had come to light that threw suspicion on the Senecas, so the party soon aimed their search at the villages of that tribe.

They pushed their inquiries in every direction, and always very cautiously, because all the tribes of the Iroquois — or, as they grandly called themselves, the Five Nations — were allies of Great Britain and were relentless in their hostility to the Americans. So a good deal of time went by before the father and his companions reached the village of the Big White Man.

Terms for ransoming the captives were agreed to at once, and in the case of Mrs. Lytle and the younger child it was easily done. But no offers, no pleading, no promises could win the release of the little Eleanor, the tribe's adopted child. "No," the chief said; she was his sister, he had taken her to fill the place of his brother who was killed by the enemy, she was dear to him, and he would not part with her.

Finding that nothing he did would shake this decision, the father had no choice but to make his sorrowful departure with the ones he had been fortunate enough to get back.

We will not try to describe what parents feel when they are forced to give up a beloved child that way and leave her in the hands of savages whom until now they had had far too much reason to think merciless. But there was no alternative. Committing her to the care of their heavenly Father, and taking some comfort from the obvious tenderness with which she had been treated so far, they set off on the miserable journey home, trusting that some later effort would do more toward recovering their little girl.

Once he had his family safe at Pittsburg, Mr. Lytle — still helped by the commandant and the Indian Agent — set out on an expedition to the frontier, to the home of the British Agent, Colonel Johnson. His account of the case stirred that benevolent officer's feelings deeply, and he promised to spare no effort on his behalf. He kept that promise religiously. As soon as the opening of spring allowed it he went in person to the village of the Big White Man and offered him a great many splendid presents of guns and horses. The chief would not budge.
