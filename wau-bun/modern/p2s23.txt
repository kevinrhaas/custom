Mrs. Burns and her baby became the prisoners of a chief, who took her to his village and treated her with great kindness. His wife, jealous of the favor being shown to "the white woman" and her child, treated the two of them with constant hostility. On one occasion she struck the baby with a tomahawk, and only just missed her aim of putting an end to it altogether. They were not left long in the old hag's power after that display; at the first opportunity they were carried off to a place of safety.

Mr. Lee's family had lived in a house on the lake shore, not far from the fort. Mr. Lee owned Lee's Place, which he worked as a farm. It was his son who had run down with the discharged soldier to raise the alarm of "Indians" at the fort on the afternoon of the 7th of April. The father, the son, and every other member of the family had been killed on the 15th of August, except Mrs. Lee and her young infant. Black Partridge claimed those two and carried them to his village on the Au Sable. He had been particularly fond of a little girl of Mrs. Lee's, about twelve years old. The child had been put on horseback for the march, and since she was not used to riding she was tied fast to the saddle so that she could not slip off or be thrown by any accident.

She was within range of the bullets when the fighting began, and was badly wounded. The horse set off at a full gallop, which partly threw her, but the bands holding her kept her on, and she hung dangling while the animal ran wildly about. In that state Black Partridge came upon her. He caught the horse and got her loose from the saddle. Finding her so badly wounded that she could not recover, and in terrible agony, he finished her at once with his tomahawk. He said afterward that it was the hardest thing he ever had to do, but that he did it because he could not bear to watch her suffer.

He took the mother and her baby to his village, where he grew warmly attached to her — so much so that he wanted to marry her. She naturally refused, and he treated her from then on with the greatest respect and consideration. He was in no hurry to let her go, since he still hoped to talk her into becoming his wife. During the winter her child fell ill. When none of the remedies within their reach did any good, Black Partridge proposed to carry the little one to Chicago, where a French trader was now living in Mr. Kinzie's house, and get some medical help from him. He wrapped his charge up with the greatest care and set out on the trip.

When he reached M. Du Pin's place, he walked into the room where the trader was and laid his bundle carefully down on the floor.

"What have you got there?" asked M. Du Pin.

"A young raccoon, which I have brought you as a present," he answered, and he opened the pack to show the sick little baby inside.

When the trader had prescribed for the child's complaint, and Black Partridge was on the point of setting off home again, he told his friend about the offer he had made to Mrs. Lee, and the way she had taken it.

M. Du Pin had his doubts about whether the chief's honorable resolution to leave the decision entirely to the lady herself would hold, so he opened negotiations for her ransom at once. He worked on Black Partridge's good feelings so effectively that the chief agreed to bring his fair prisoner straight to Chicago, so that she could be restored to her friends.

Whether the kind trader had any feeling in the business at the start beyond sympathy and brotherly kindness, we cannot say. All we know is that in due course Mrs. Lee became Madame Du Pin, and that the worthy couple lived together in great happiness for many years afterward.

The fate of Nau-non-gee, one of the chiefs of the Calumet village, who was mentioned in the early part of this narrative, deserves to be put on record.

During the battle of the 15th of August, the man he chiefly went after was a Sergeant Hays, from whom he had received many acts of kindness.

After Hays had taken a ball through the body, this Indian ran up to tomahawk him, and the sergeant gathered what strength he had left and ran him through the body with his bayonet. They fell together. Other Indians came running up and soon dispatched Hays, and it was only then that his bayonet was pulled out of his adversary's body.

The wounded chief was carried after the battle to his village on the Calumet, where he lived on for several days. Feeling his end coming, he called his young men together and charged them, in the most solemn way, to look after the safety of their prisoners once he was dead, and to take none of their lives out of respect to his memory — since he had deserved his own fate at the hands of people whose kindness he had repaid so badly.
