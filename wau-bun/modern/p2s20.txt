From Ouilmette's house the party of Indians went on to Mr. Kinzie's. They walked into the parlor where the family were sitting with their faithful protectors, and sat down on the floor without saying a word. Black Partridge could see from their sullen, vengeful faces what was in their minds, but he did not dare argue with them. All he did was remark quietly to Wau-ban-see, "We have tried to save our friends, but it is no use — nothing will save them now."

Just then a friendly whoop went up from a party of newcomers on the opposite bank of the river. Black Partridge sprang up to meet their leader as the canoes they had hurriedly climbed into touched the bank near the house.

"Who are you?" he demanded.

"A man. Who are you?"

"A man like yourself. But tell me who you are" — meaning, tell me where you stand and which side you are on.

"I am a Sau-ga-nash!"

"Then get to the house as fast as you can — your friend is in danger, and you are the only one who can save him."

It was Billy Caldwell, and he came into the parlor at a calm walk, without a trace of agitation about him. He took his time about removing his accoutrements, put them with his rifle behind the door, and then greeted the hostile savages.

"Well now, my friends! Good day to you. I was told there were enemies here, but I am glad to find only friends. Why have you blackened your faces? Is it that you are mourning for the friends you lost in the battle?" — deliberately choosing to misread this sign of murderous intent.

"Or is it that you are fasting? If so, ask our friend here and he will give you something to eat. He is the Indian's friend, and he has never yet refused them anything they needed."

Caught off guard like that, the savages were ashamed to admit what they had really come for. So they said, modestly, that they had come to ask their friends for some white cotton to wrap their dead in before burying them. They were given it, along with some other presents, and they left the place peaceably.
