The officers at the fort at this time were Captain Heald, who was in command; Lieutenant Helm, Mr. Kinzie's son-in-law; Ensign Ronan — the last two both very young men — and the surgeon, Dr. Van Voorhees.

The command came to about seventy-five men, and very few of those were fit for service.

Relations between these troops and the Indians had been steady and friendly all along. It is true that the leading men of the Pottowattamie nation, like those of most other tribes, went every year to Fort Malden in Canada to collect a great quantity of presents, which the British Government had been using for many years to buy their alliance. And it was well known that a good many Pottowattamies, and Winnebagoes too, had fought alongside the Ottawas and Shawnees at the battle of Tippecanoe the previous autumn. Even so, since the principal chiefs of all the bands in the neighborhood seemed to be on the friendliest possible terms with the Americans, nobody expected any break in the peace.

After the 15th of August, though, people remembered a great many things that might have opened the whites' eyes if they had not been lulled into a fatal sense of safety. One instance in particular is worth mentioning.

In the spring before the fort was destroyed, two Indians of the Calumet band came to the fort to visit the commanding officer. Passing through the quarters, they saw Mrs. Heald and Mrs. Helm playing at battledoor.

One of them, Nau-non-gee, turned to the interpreter and said, "The white chiefs' wives are amusing themselves very much. It won't be long before they are hoeing in our corn-fields!"

At the time this was taken for an idle threat, or at most a flash of jealousy at the difference between the lives of their own women and those of the "white chiefs' wives." A few months later, how bitterly it was remembered.
