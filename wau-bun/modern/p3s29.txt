It was after the news of this last event that the council appointed with the Winnebagoes was to be held at the Four Lakes, thirty-five miles from Fort Winnebago.

We pleaded and argued against his exposing himself like that, and got nowhere. "It's my duty to assemble my people and talk to them," my husband said, "and I must run the risk, if there is any. I have perfect confidence in the Winnebagoes. From everything I can learn, the enemy are a long way from the Four Lakes now — probably at Kosh-ko-nong. I'll set off early in the morning with Paquette, hold my council, and be back with you the same evening."

There is no use trying to describe how we felt through that long, dreary day. When night came, the shout of a drunken Indian, or even a dog barking, was enough to fill our hearts with terror.

We sat up late at the open window, listening to every sound — and what a joy it was when we finally made out the tramp of horses! We knew it was Griffin and Jerry coming up the hill, and a cheerful shout soon told us that all was well. My husband and his interpreter had ridden seventy miles that day, on top of holding a long "talk" with the Indians.

In council, the Winnebagoes had promised to do everything they possibly could to keep peace and good order among their young men. They told their Father that the bands on the Rock River, except Win-no-sheek's, were all determined to stay friendly and keep clear of the Sauks. To that end they were giving up their villages and cornfields and moving north, so that their Great Father the President would have no reason to be displeased with them. About Win-no-sheek and his people, they said they couldn't answer.

Time went on, and brought stories of new outrages with it. Among them were the murders of Auberry, Green, and Force at Blue Mound, and the attack on Apple Fort. The news of that last one was brought in by old Crély, Mrs. Paquette's father, who rode express from Galena. He swore that he had once ridden past a bush with Sauks hiding behind it, but that his horse smelled the sweet-scented grass they always decorate their people with when they are on a war-party, and set off at such a gallop that he never stopped until he reached the Portage.

Another man who brought us news was a young gentleman named Follett, whose eyes had bulged and fixed themselves so far out of his head from keeping an anxious watch for the enemy that it was many days after he reached a place of safety before they went back to their usual limits and expression.
