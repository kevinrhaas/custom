It is well known that before the war of the Revolution the whole western part of Pennsylvania was inhabited by various Indian tribes. Of these, the Delawares were friends of the whites, and once the great struggle began they took the side of the United States. The Iroquois, on the other hand, were friends and allies of the mother country.

Very few white settlers had ventured past the Susquehanna. The many roving bands of Shawanoes, Nanticokes, and the rest professed friendship with the Americans at times and acted in concert with the Delawares, or Lenape, as allies; at other times they let their neighbors the Iroquois talk them into showing the most bloodthirsty hostility.

For that reason, life on the frontier was one of constant peril and alarm. Many a scene of dismal barbarity was acted out, as the history of the times testifies; and even the people who felt themselves somewhat protected by their immediate neighbors, the Delawares, never lost sight of the caution their exposed position demanded of them.

The military garrison at Pittsburg — or Fort Pitt, as it was then called — gave some extra security to those who had pushed farther west, into the fertile valleys of the Alleghany and the Monongahela. Among these was the family of Mr. Lytle, who some years before our story opens had moved from Baltimore to Path Valley, near Carlisle, and afterward settled himself on the banks of Plum River, a tributary of the Alleghany. Here, with his wife and five children, he had lived in comfort and security, never troubled by a hostile visit, and annoyed only by the occasional false alarm from his more timorous neighbors, who had more experience of frontier life and were prone to expect the worst and to magnify every appearance of danger.

On a bright afternoon in the autumn of 1779, two of Mr. Lytle's children — a girl of nine and her brother, two years younger — were playing in a little dingle or hollow behind their father's house. Several large trees had been felled there not long before and were lying about, still untrimmed of their branches, and there were many logs cut for fuel scattered around. The children got tired of their games and sat down on one of these, and to pass the time they fell to talking about something that puzzled them a great deal.

A few hours earlier, playing in the same place, they had thought they saw an Indian lurking behind one of the fallen trees. The Indians of the neighborhood were in the habit of visiting the family now and then, and the children had grown familiar and even affectionate with a good many of them; but this one seemed to be a stranger, and after one hurried look they fled to the house in alarm.

Their mother scolded them for the report they brought and tried to convince them there was nothing in it. "You know," she said, "you are always alarming us for no reason. The neighbors' children have frightened you to death. Go back to your play and learn to be more courageous."

So the children went back to their games, hardly persuaded by their mother's arguments. While they were sitting there on the trunk of the tree, their talk was broken into by what sounded like the note of a quail somewhere not far off.

"Listen," said the boy, as a second note answered the first. "Do you hear that?"

"Yes," she said; and then, after a few moments' silence, "Don't you hear a rustling among the branches of that tree over there?"

"Maybe it's a squirrel — but look, what is that? I'm sure I saw something red among the branches. It looked like a fawn popping its head up."

At that moment the children, who had been staring so hard in the direction of the fallen tree that they had forgotten everything else, felt themselves seized from behind and pinned in an iron grip. Imagine their horror and dismay at finding themselves in the arms of savages whose terrible faces and gestures showed plainly that they were enemies.

The men signed to the children to keep quiet on pain of death, and hurried them off, half dead with terror, in a direction leading away from their father's home. After they had gone some distance in complete silence, their captors relaxed a little, and as night came on the party halted, having first taken the usual precautions against a surprise.

In an agony of uncertainty and terror, torn away from the home and parents they loved, and expecting every one of the horrors the rumors of the time attached to a captivity among the Indians — perhaps even a death by torture — the poor children could hold their grief in no longer, and gave way to sobs and lamentations.

Their distress seemed to touch the compassion of one of the party, a mild-looking man, who came over and tried to soothe them. He spread them a bed of the long grass that grew near the camping-place, offered them a share of his own stock of dried meat and parched corn, and made them understand by signs that no further harm was meant them.
