In the meantime we had room for one more party, and this one was to be a real Western hop. Nobody who was there will forget that dance at Mrs. Baird's. Every soul, young and old, who could be gathered from anywhere on Green Bay — that was the phrase, on Green Bay — came to it. The young officers were up from Fort Howard, looking very smart in their uniforms. Treasures of finery that had not been called for in years were brought back into the light. Everybody was determined to honor the strangers by turning out in the absolute best they had. It was going to be an entertainment beyond anything given there before. The whole house was pressed into service. Desks and chairs were thrown unceremoniously out of Mr. B.'s office, which made up one wing, to give the dancers more room. Not just the front of the house but even the kitchen was made fit to receive company, in case some primitive visitor — as happened now and then — preferred to sit quietly in there and smoke his cigar. In those days that was a contingency you always had to plan for.

Nothing could have been merrier. No restraint except good manners, no excess of formality — just genuine, hearty good humor and enjoyment, the kind that pleasant, hospitable people with exactly enough French in them to give any amusement its zest can produce. In a country that new, where the size of the company at a gathering mattered more than its quality, the circle was not always what you would strictly call select.

I knew that, so I was more amused than surprised when a clumsy little man with a broad, red, laughing face waddled across the room to the chair I had taken after a dance and addressed me like this:

"Miss K——, nobody ain't never introduced you to me, but I've seen you a good many times, and I know your husband very well, so I thought I might as well come and speak to you. My name is A——."

"Ah, Mr. A——, good evening. I hope you're enjoying yourself. How is your sister?"

"Oh, she's a good deal worse — her cold's got into her eye and it's all shut up."

Then he turned squarely on a lady sitting nearby, who was radiant with youth and beauty and sparkling with wit and real humor.

"Oh, Mrs. Beall," he began, "what a beautiful gown you've got on, and how handsome you do look! I declare you're the prettiest woman in the room, and you dance the handsomest."

"Indeed, Mr. A——," she said, holding down her love of fun and putting on a demure face, "I'm afraid you flatter me."

"No, I don't — I'm in earnest. I've just come to ask you to dance."

Such is the penalty of being too charming.
