The quick, chattering flourish of the bob-o'-link, the soft whistle of the thrush, the tender coo of the wood-dove, the deep warbling bass of the grouse, the drumming of the partridge, the melodious trill of the lark, the gay carol of the robin, the friendly, familiar call of the duck and the teal — all of it sounding out from tree and knoll and lowland, and drawing from the simple half-breed that expressive exclamation, "Voilà la forêt qui parle!" It feels as though a man must raise his own voice without meaning to, and take his part in the general chorus — the morning song of praise.

Birds and flowers, and the soft, mild air of morning! Surely it must have been in a scene like this that Milton's Adam poured out his beautiful hymn of adoration, "These are thy glorious works, Parent of Good"?

That day we were travelling in the hope of reaching, early, the broad stretch of the Rock River that widens here into the Kosh-ko-nong. The name of that water, made doubly moving by what afterward happened to its people, means "the lake we live on."

For the first part of the day our road went through forests so thick and tangled that Grignon and Lecuyer often had to go ahead as pioneers with their axes, to cut away the shrubs and branches in the way. It was slow work, and at times quite discouraging, but we got through it at last, and then we came into country of an entirely different kind: low prairies cut across by deep, narrow streams like canals, and getting over those, whether on horseback or with the carriage, was often a matter of delay and sometimes of real difficulty.

Several times that morning the horses had to be taken out of the carriage and the carriage itself pulled and pushed across the deep, narrow channels as best it could be.

Nobody ever welcomed the wooded banks of the Kosh-ko-nong more gladly than we did when they finally came into view. A ride of five or six miles through the beautiful oak openings brought us to Man-Eater's village, a cluster of neat bark wigwams, with large fields on both sides of corn, beans and squashes, only recently planted but already promising a fine crop. In front of it lay the broad blue lake, its shores to the south open and marshy, but near the village, and stretching far away to the north, bordered with fine, tall trees. The village stood only a little below the place where the Rock River opens into the lake, and during a talk between our party and the Indians there, an arrangement was made for them to take us across at a spot about half a mile above.

After a short halt, we took up our line of march again through the woods, along the bank of the river.

A number of the Winnebagoes — we had been among our own people ever since leaving Gros-pied Lake — set off for the appointed place by water, paddling the largest and strongest canoes they had.

When we reached the spot they had named, we dismounted and the men set to work unsaddling and unloading. We were soon put into the canoes and paddled across to the opposite bank. Next the horses were swum over. After them was to come the carriage. Two long wooden canoes were lashed firmly together side by side, and since they were wide enough for the carriage to stand inside them, the crossing began. Again and again the tottering craft would sway from one side to the other, and a cry or a shout would go up from our party on shore as the whole mass looked about to plunge sideways into the water — but it would right itself, and eventually, after a good many departures from the perpendicular, it reached the shore safely.

We now hoped our troubles were over, and that all we had to do was mount up and trot on as fast as we could to Fort Winnebago. But no. Half a mile farther on there was a formidable swamp — not very wide, it is true, but with two to three feet of mud and water in it. Whether we could get through it at all with the carriage was an open question. Several of the Indians came along with us to this place, partly to give us their help and advice, and partly to enjoy the fun of the spectacle.

When we got to the swamp we were inclined to laugh at the formidable descriptions we had been given. All we could see was a strip of what looked like rather low ground covered with tall, dry rushes.

The ground did look a little wet, that is true, but there seemed to be nothing there to justify all the alarm that had been raised. So I was very surprised to see my husband, who had been away for a few minutes, come back to our group in his duck trousers, without shoes or stockings.

"What are you going to do?" I asked.

"Carry you through the swamp on my shoulders. Come on, Petaille, you're the strongest — you're to carry Madame Kinzie, and To-shun-nuck there" (pointing to a tall, stout Winnebago) "will take Madame Helm."

"Wait a minute," I said, and sitting down on the grass I deliberately took off my own boots and stockings.

"What is that for?" they all asked.

"Because I don't want to ride with wet feet for the rest of the day."

"No danger of that," they said, and nobody followed my example.

By the time they were out in the middle of the swamp, though, they found my precaution had not been useless at all. The water the men carrying them had to walk through was deep enough that nothing the ladies could do kept their feet above the surface, and I had the satisfaction of knowing that my weight on my husband's shoulders was a good deal lighter, since I could stay in my first position instead of shifting about constantly to keep out of the water.

The laugh was entirely on my side when I put my things back on and climbed dry-shod into my saddle.

It will be noticed that travelling in the woods does upset ceremony and formality to a degree, and that it necessarily cuts down our conventionalities somewhat. The only remedy is to make it up to ourselves with a double share when we get back to the civilized walks of life.

By dint of a great deal of pulling, shouting, encouraging and threatening, the horses did finally drag the carriage through the difficult place, and our red friends were left to go back to their village with what was no doubt a very exaggerated and very funny account of everything they had seen and helped with.

We had not forgotten our promise to Lieutenant Foster to put up a "guide-board" of some kind for his convenience in following us. So on several occasions we carried pieces of wood three or four feet long out of the woods with us and planted them at certain points, with a crosspiece stuck through a cleft in the top, to show the direction he and his party should take.

That way we felt sure that, although he was a few days behind, he would find our trail and get the benefit of the same help we had had in getting past the difficulties of the way.
