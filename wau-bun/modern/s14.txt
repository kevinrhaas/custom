By the evening of the first day we reached the Kakalin, where another branch of the Grignon family lived. They entertained us very pleasantly, though I was so eager to begin my life in the forest that I would gladly have had the tent pitched on the riverbank and dropped the comforts of civilization on the spot. That would have been a slight, though — possibly an insult — so we did far better and enjoyed what was offered us: hot venison steaks, crêpes, and that excellent cup of coffee nobody makes the way a Frenchwoman does, which is so reviving after a day in the open air.

The Kakalin is a rapid on the Fox River, and enough of one that a boat's heavy freight has to be portaged around it while the boat itself is poled or hauled up against the current on ropes. It is one of a whole series of rapids and chûtes, or falls, between that point and Lake Winnebago twenty miles above.

The next morning after breakfast we said goodbye to our hosts and got ready to go on. The bourgeois had been up since early, overseeing his men getting the boat and its cargo past the Kakalin. The recent rain had left the paths through the woods and along the bank too muddy for comfortable walking, so I was put in an ox-cart and jolted over the uneven road, saluting every stump and stone impartially as we came to it. The only way to avoid one, apparently, was for our driver — a thick-headed little Frenchman — to suddenly steer his cattle into an overhanging tree or a thorn bush, at serious cost to my straw bonnet and to my eyes.

We got through in the end, and at the head of the rapids I found the boat lying ready for us to board again.

Our Menomonee guide, Wish-tay-yun, came forward to be introduced: a fine, powerfully built man with an open, good-humored, you might almost say roguish face.

"Bon-jour, bon-jour, maman," was his laughing greeting. Once again I was surprised — not by the French this time, since I had gotten used to that, but by the respectable title he had decided to give me.

"Yes," said my husband. "You had better make up your mind to a very large and fully grown family: all the Winnebagoes, Pottowattamies, Chippewas and Ottawas, plus any Sioux, Sacs and Foxes and Iowas who have some point to gain by coming to me. The first of those call me father because of my office; the others do it as a courtesy. You, naturally, will be their mother."

Wish-tay-yun and I were friends in no time, my husband translating the Chippewa he spoke. We were impatient to get moving, the morning being well along, and when everything was ready the word came:

"Pousse au large, mes gens!" (Push out, my men.)

At that moment a boat put out from the far bank and came toward us. There were white men in it, and they signaled that they wanted us to wait until they caught up. When they came alongside we found they were Mr. Marsh, a missionary among the Waubanakees — the New York Indians, recently brought into this country — and the Rev. Eleazar Williams, who was then living among his red brethren on the right bank of the Fox.

For people placed like that, even more than for people in the settlements, visitors out of the "east countrie" are an outright godsend. We had to hand over every kind of news we had brought — political, ecclesiastical and social — along with a fairly detailed account of what we intended to do, or rather of what we hoped we might be able to do, among our native children at the Portage.

For my part I had to admit that since I was almost a total stranger to Indian character and Indian habits, I was going among them with no settled plan of any kind. General good will, and a hope of making them my friends, were the only principles I could claim so far. Time and better acquaintance would have to show me how that principle could actually be put to work for their greatest good.

Mr. Williams was a dark-complexioned, good-looking man. Since his relations in Connecticut had always spoken of him as "our Indian cousin," it never crossed my mind to doubt that he belonged to that race — though I think now that if I had met him anywhere else I would have taken him for a Spaniard or a Mexican. His color leaned much more toward olive than copper, and his face was grave, nearly melancholy. He said very little the whole time, asked few questions, and offered no remarks except in answer to a question put to him.
