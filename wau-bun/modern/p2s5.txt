The people living outside the fort — a few discharged soldiers and some half-breed families — now dug themselves in at the Agency House. It stood west of the fort, between the pickets and the river, about twenty rods from the fort itself.

It was an old-fashioned log building with a hall running down the middle and one large room on either side. Piazzas ran the whole length of the building, front and back. These were planked up for extra security, port-holes were cut in them, and sentries were posted at night.

The enemy were thought to be still lurking nearby, or else, emboldened by their earlier success, likely to come back at any moment. So an order went out that no soldier or citizen was to leave the area around the garrison without a guard.

One night a sergeant and a private out on patrol came suddenly on a party of Indians in the pasture next to the esplanade. The sergeant fired his piece and the two of them fell back toward the fort. Before they could reach it an Indian threw his tomahawk; it missed the sergeant and struck a wagon standing nearby. The sentry in the block-house fired at once, and to some effect, while the men got safely inside. The next morning they found blood trailing far out into the prairie, and a place in the long grass where a body had been laid, and knew some damage had been done.

Another time the enemy came into the esplanade to steal horses. The horses were not in the stable where they had expected them, so they made up for the disappointment by stabbing every sheep in the stable and then turning them loose. The poor animals flocked toward the fort. That raised the alarm — the garrison turned out, parties were sent after them — but the raiders got clean away.

The people in the fort had no further alarms for many weeks.
