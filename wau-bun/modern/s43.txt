Our Indian guide had come to us early. He led us carefully out of the wood, pointing out on the way the many bee-trees the grove was famous for, and then set off at a long trot. About nine o'clock he brought us to Piché's — a log cabin on rising ground that looked out east over the broad prairie. We had hoped to get something to eat and drink there, since Piché was an old acquaintance of some of the party, but the master of the house was away. We found the cabin full of Indians and travelers: a few of the travelers, a great many of the Indians.

There was nothing to keep us there except a bright fire burning in the clay chimney, which we were glad to warm ourselves at. A man in Quaker dress came forward to answer our questions, and offered to be our escort to Chicago, where he was headed himself. So we said goodbye to our Indian friend and paid him well for all the trouble he had so kindly taken on our account.

From Piché's a long reach of prairie stretched away to the Du Page. Our new acquaintance, Mr. Dogherty, told us that between the two forks of that river we would find the house of a Mr. Hawley, who would give us a good dinner.

The cold was brutal. The wind came across that wide prairie with nothing at all to break it, and it chilled us to the heart. Whenever my feet went numb I beat them against the saddle to bring the blood back, and I kept it up until they were so bruised I couldn't beat them any longer. For mile after weary mile there was not a house, not a wigwam, not so much as a clump of trees to shelter behind. Eventually we came to the west fork of the Du Page. It was frozen, but not hard enough to carry the horses. The only thing to do was cut a way through the ice for them. It took a long time, because the ice had frozen several inches thick over that last bitter night. Plante went first with an axe and cut as far out as he could reach, then got up on one of the tough little ponies and, with some difficulty, broke the ice ahead of him until he had opened a passage to the far shore.

How the poor animals shivered as they were reined in among the floating ice! And we, sitting waiting in that piercing wind, were not much better off. Brunet probably thought so too, because in his usual contrary way he plunged in right behind Plante and stood there shaking and quaking at his back, glancing around every so often as if to say, "I got ahead of you this time!" We were all across at last, and we spurred the horses on until we reached Hawley's, a large, roomy house near the east fork of the river.

The good woman of the house made us welcome, and soon had us warm and comfortable. It felt as though we were back in a civilized country. She set about making dinner for us right away, and we watched her with hungry eyes: she took a huge ham down from the rafters and cut slice after slice off it, broke a dozen or more fine fresh eggs into a pan ready for frying, then mixed a johnny-cake and stood it against a board in front of the fire to bake. Even with that fine bright fire, the dinner seemed to take an unreasonable amount of time to cook — but it did cook, at last, and the good woman had every right to stare at the appetites we had brought with us. She had no idea how short our rations had been for the last two days.

We asked, and found that if we pushed on we could make Lawton's, on the Aux Plaines, that night, which would put us within twelve miles of Chicago. Naturally we wasted no time, and set out as soon after dinner as we could.

Crossing the east fork of the Du Page was worse than the first crossing had been. The ice had already been broken up, either by the force of the current or by riders who had gone through ahead of us, so that by the time we reached the bank large cakes of it were floating downstream. The horses had to make a quick dash through the water, and the water was so high and running in such a torrent that if I had not been riding Jerry, the tallest horse in the party, I would have been soaked through.

As it was, I was thoroughly frightened, and I held on to bridle and mane for dear life. After that we traveled as fast as we could, to reach the place we were headed for before dark.

Mr. Dogherty — a tall, bolt-upright man, half Quaker and half Methodist — did his best to keep me entertained by laying out for me a complete schedule of his religious opinions, together with the Scripture they rested on. He was something of a perfectionist, and he clearly regarded himself, with no small satisfaction, as a living demonstration of his favorite doctrine.

"St. John says" — this was the style of the thing — "St. John says, 'He that is born of God doth not commit sin.' Now, if I am born of God, I do not commit sin."

I was too cold and too tired to argue the point, so I let him have it entirely his own way. I imagine he found me rather dull company, but at least he gave me credit for being a good listener.
