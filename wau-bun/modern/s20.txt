About six miles above the Butte a fairly wide stream called Wolf River joins the Fox, and since it is much the straighter and more promising-looking of the two, strangers have sometimes taken it for the main channel and gone a considerable distance up it before discovering, to their great disgust, that they have to turn around and come back.

Past that point the river starts playing tricks on the compass. Since I was always on the lookout for pretty scenery to sketch, I was very taken with a picturesque group on a bank right down close to the water: broad overhanging trees, and two or three wigwams tucked into their shade. Bright-looking little children with no clothes on at all were playing around, and their two mothers sat on the ground weaving a mat for the lodge. It was a lovely scene, and I started a sketch. As usual, everyone on the bank set up a shout when they recognized Shaw-nee-aw-kee.

"Ee-awn-chee-wee-rah, Hee-nee-kar-ray-kay-noo."

It was one of those occasions when they became demonstrative. After a little while we went on and I kept working on my drawing. The sun, meanwhile, kept getting into the wrong place. It had been directly behind me; then it was on my left; then it was straight ahead of me. I shifted position a few times. Eventually the sun was decidedly on my right. What on earth was the matter? I looked up. "Oh, here's a pretty scene — I must have this one too. But how surprisingly like the one I've just finished, only facing a different way." Once again we were met with shouting and laughter. It was the same spot we had passed less than an hour before. Having gone around a circuit of nearly four miles, we had come back to find our actual progress was the width of the bank the trees and wigwams stood on. Not very encouraging for an impatient traveler.

We reached Lake Puckaway late in the evening of our second day out from Butte des Morts. A white man named Gleason lived there — the one whose enormous powers of exaggeration led poor Hooe to make his little pun, "All is not gold that Gleasons." We did not look for shelter at his house, because late in the season as it was, we found the shore so infested with mosquitoes that we were glad to pick a spot as far from the bank as we could get and make ourselves comfortable in the boat.

The lake takes its name from the long flags or rushes that grow in its water in great quantity, which the women use to make the coarse matting that covers their wigwams. Their way of making it is very simple and primitive. They sit on the ground with the rushes laid side by side and fastened at both ends, and pass a shuttle — a long flat needle of bone with a piece of cord made from tree bark attached to it — through each rush, binding it tight against the next one and producing a fine, substantial mat. The mats are seldom more than five or six feet long, since anything bigger would be awkward to handle when setting up a lodge.

This is work usually given to the older women of a family. When they are worn down and broken by exposure and hard use, so that they can no longer cut trees, hoe corn or carry heavy loads, they are put to weaving mats, minding the children, and disciplining the dogs, which every Indian lodge has in abundance.

Lac de Boeuf, or Buffalo Lake, which our course brought us into next, is a lovely body of water. In places its banks are extremely picturesque, with beautiful headlands running out into the clear depths where they and the magnificent stands of trees on top of them lie reflected as in a mirror. Now and then we caught sight of deer darting across the glades that opened here and there through the woodland, or a pair of sandhill cranes would get up, flapping slowly, and go looking for a more undisturbed place to rest. The flocks of teal — skimming the water one minute, rising toward the shelter of the woods the next — were a sore temptation to our sportsman, but since there was little chance of recovering the game once it was down, he did not indulge himself in the wanton pleasure of shooting simply to destroy life.

Leaving that charming lake and going back into the narrow, twisting course of the river, we said goodbye to anything worth calling scenery until the end of the journey.
