It was the evening of the 7th of April, 1812. Mr. Kinzie's children were dancing in front of the fire while their father played the violin. The tea-table was laid, and they were all waiting for their mother to come home; she had gone about a quarter of a mile up the river to sit with a sick neighbor.

Their play broke off all at once. The door flew open and Mrs. Kinzie burst in, white with fear and barely able to get the words out: "The Indians! The Indians!"

"The Indians? What about them? Where?" they all demanded at once.

"Up at Lee's Place, killing and scalping!"

It took Mrs. Kinzie some effort to steady herself enough to tell them what she knew: that while she was up at Burns's, a man and a boy had been seen running downstream as hard as they could go on the far bank of the river; that they had shouted across to warn the Burns family to save themselves, because the Indians were at Lee's Place, where the two of them had just escaped from. Having delivered that terrifying news, they had run on as fast as they could for the fort, which was on the same side of the river as they were.

Everything now was panic and dread. The family were bundled into two old pirogues moored near the house and paddled across the river as fast as they could go, to take shelter in the fort.

Everything the man and the boy had managed to escape with was soon common knowledge — but to make their story clear, the scene of the action has to be described.

Lee's Place, later known as Hardscrabble, was a farm with the Chicago River running through it, about four miles up from the river's mouth. The farm-house stood on the west bank of the south branch. On the north side of the main stream, quite close to where it joins Lake Michigan, stood Mr. Kinzie's own house and trading-establishment, which has already been described.

The fort was on the southern bank, directly opposite that house. Nothing lay between the two but the river and a few rods of sloping green turf on either side of it.

The fort was built differently from the one put up on the same site in 1816. It had two block-houses on its southern side, and on the northern side a sally-port — an underground passage running from the parade-ground down to the river. That was meant either to make escape possible in an emergency, or to keep the garrison supplied with water during a siege.
