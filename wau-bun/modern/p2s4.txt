They did not stop or slow down until they came opposite Burns's, where, as already told, they called across the water to warn the family they were in danger, and then hurried on to the fort.

Now that they were safe themselves, it struck them that the Burns family was at that very moment in immediate danger. The question was who would risk his own life to bring them somewhere safe. A gallant young officer, Ensign Ronan, volunteered to go after them with a party of five or six soldiers.

They went up the river in a scow and carried the mother, with her baby barely a day old, on her bed down to the boat, and then took her and the rest of the family carefully back to the fort.

That afternoon a party of soldiers — a corporal and six men — had been given leave to go up the river and fish.

They were still out when the people fleeing Lee's Place reached the fort, and, afraid they might run into the Indians, the commanding officer ordered a cannon fired to warn them there was danger.

They were about two miles above Lee's Place at the time. They heard the signal, took the hint, put out their torches — it was night by then — and dropped back down the river toward the garrison as quietly as they could. The country had been unsettled since the battle of Tippecanoe the previous November, and that had made every man watchful; the smallest alarm was a reminder to beware of the Indians.

When the fishing party reached Lee's Place, somebody proposed stopping to warn the people there to be on their guard, since the signal from the fort meant danger of some kind. Everything around the house was as still as death. They felt their way along, and as the corporal climbed over the small enclosure he put his hand down on a man's dead body. By touch alone he soon worked out that the head had been scalped and otherwise mutilated. The murdered man's faithful dog stood guarding what was left of his master.

That told the whole story. The men fell back to their canoes and reached the fort about eleven o'clock that night without being attacked. The next morning a party of citizens and soldiers volunteered to go to Lee's Place and find out more about what had become of the people there. Mr. White's body was found with two bullets in it and eleven stab wounds in the chest. The Frenchman lay dead as already described, his dog still beside him. Both bodies were brought to the fort and buried close by.

Traders out in the Indian country reported later that the men who did this were a party of Winnebagoes who had come into the neighborhood to "take some white scalps." Their plan had been to work their way down the river from Lee's Place and kill every white man outside the walls of the fort. But they heard the cannon go off, and not knowing what it meant, they decided to be satisfied with this one exploit and went straight back to their homes on Rock River.
