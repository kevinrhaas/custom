It was late in the day (March 13th) when we said goodbye to our kind hostess. She loaded us up with cakes, good wishes, and messages for her sister Dixon and the children. We rode along pleasantly through a country that was beautiful in spite of how wintry it looked.

There was a house at Buffalo Grove where we stopped for half an hour, and a nice-looking young girl there gave us some maple sugar she had made herself. She entertained us with the story of a contest between two rivals for the business of the stage-wagon: its owners had not yet decided whether to send it by way of Buffalo Grove or by another road, which she pointed out to us not far off. The driver, she was careful to let us know, was in favor of the Buffalo Grove route — and the blush with which she answered yes to our question, "Is he a young man?" explained the whole matter to everyone's satisfaction.

Finally, right at sunset, we came to the dark, fast-running water of the Rock River. The ferry we had gone so far out of our way to take advantage of turned out to be nothing but a small boat, a skiff. The bigger one had been swept off into the current and carried away the week before, when the ice broke up.

My husband's first concern was to get me across. He put me into the boat along with the saddles, the packs, and the rest of it, and since it was late and there was no time to lose, he undertook at the same time to hold the bridles of the two most docile horses and guide them as they swam the river.

When we had gotten a few rods out from shore, a loud puffing and blowing right beside us made us jump, and we looked around to find, to our great surprise, little Brunet just off our weather-bow. Determined not to be outdone by the horse he modeled himself on, Jerry, he had taken to the water entirely on his own authority, and he reached the far bank as soon as anybody in the party.

Once everyone was safely landed, a short walk brought us to Mr. Dixon's house. New as he was to the country, he had managed to get everything around him comfortable, and when he showed us into Mrs. Dixon's sitting-room and sat us down by a glowing wood fire, while Mrs. Dixon busied herself getting us a good supper, I felt that the comfort more than made up for the inconvenience of a trip like this.

Mrs. Dixon had several children around her. One of them, leaning against the chimney-piece, was dressed in full Indian costume — calico shirt, blanket, and leggings. His dark complexion and his large, melancholy eyes, which he kept fixed on the ashes where he was making marks with a stick, hardly ever raising them to look at us the way children usually do, interested me enormously, and I asked an intelligent little girl who was plainly a daughter of our host, "Who is that boy?"

"Oh, that is John Ogie," she said.

"What is the matter with him? He looks very sad."

"Oh, he is fretting after his mother."

"Is she dead, then?"

"Some say she is dead, and some say she is gone away. I guess she is dead, and buried up in one of those graves yonder" — and she pointed to two or three little picketed enclosures on a rise of ground across from the window.

I felt a strong sympathy for the child, and it grew stronger when the little spokeswoman answered my next question — "Has he no father?" — with, "Oh, yes, but he goes away, and drinks, and don't care for his children."

"And what becomes of John then?"

"He stays here with us, and we teach him to read, and he learns dreadful fast."

When the boy finally turned those big dark eyes on me, it went straight to my heart. It was such a motherless look. And it was explained long afterward, when I learned the rest of his history. His mother was still alive, and he knew it, although — with the reserve that is peculiar to his people — he never spoke of her to his young companions. She had not been able to bear any more of the ill treatment of her husband, a surly, intemperate Canadian, so she had left him and gone back to her own family among the Pottowattamies. Years later this boy and a brother, who had also been left behind with their father, made their way up to the Upper Missouri to join their mother, after she and the rest of her tribe had been taken by the Government from the shores of Lake Michigan.

A thoroughly savory supper of ducks and venison, with everything that goes with them, was soon steaming on the table, and we did it full justice. Traveling is a great sharpener of the appetite, and so is cheerfulness — and ours was raised by the encouraging account Mr. Dixon gave us of the rest of the route still ahead of us.

"There is no difficulty," he said, "if you keep a little to the north, and strike the great Sauk trail. If you get too far to the south, you will come upon the Winnebago Swamp, and, once in that, there is no telling when you will ever get out again. As for the distance, it is nothing at all to speak of. Two young men came out here from Chicago, on foot, last fall. They got here the evening of the second day, and, even with a lady in your party, you could go on horseback in less time than that. The only thing is to be sure and get on the great track that the Sauks have made, in going every year from the Mississippi to Canada, to receive their presents from the British Indian Agent."
