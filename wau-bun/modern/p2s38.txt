At fourteen the heroine of the story just told married Colonel McKillip, a British officer. He was killed near what was afterward called Fort Defiance, at the Miami Rapids, in 1794. A detachment of British troops had been sent down from Detroit to take possession of that post. General Wayne was then on a campaign against the Indians, and the British Government thought it proper to make a few demonstrations on behalf of their allies. Colonel McKillip had gone out with a party to reconnoiter and was coming back to his post after dark when one of his own sentinels fired on him and killed him. Mrs. Helm was the daughter of that marriage.

While she was a widow, Mrs. McKillip lived with her parents at Grosse Pointe, eight miles above Detroit, and it was during those years that something happened which — because of the sad and mysterious circumstances around it — she always came back to with a particular interest.

Her second brother, Thomas Lytle, was, because of his amiable and affectionate character, the one she loved best of that whole large family circle. He was paying his addresses to a young lady who lived on the river Trench, as it was called then — now the river Thames, a stream that empties into Lake St. Clair about twenty miles above Detroit. When he went to see her, his habit was to take his horse across the Detroit River by the ferry and then go on overland to the river Trench, which at some seasons of the year could be forded.

On a fine forenoon late in the spring, he said goodbye to his mother and sister and set off on one of these regular visits, which usually lasted two or three days.

After dinner his sister was sitting at her work by an open window that looked out on a little side enclosure planted with fruit trees, when she was startled to see something outside the window, between her and the light. She raised her eyes and saw her brother Thomas. He had no horse with him, and he was carrying his saddle on his shoulders.

Surprised that she had not heard the gate open to let him in, and surprised too by how strange he looked, loaded down that way, she spoke to him and asked what had happened and why he was back so soon. He gave her no answer. He only looked hard into her face as he moved slowly along the paved walk that led to the stables.

She waited a few moments, expecting him to reappear and give some account of himself and his adventures. Finally she lost patience with the delay, put down her work, and went toward the back of the house to find him.

The first person she met was her mother. "Have you seen Thomas?" she asked.

"Thomas! He's gone to the river Trench."

"No, he's come back — I saw him pass the window not fifteen minutes ago."

"Then he'll be in soon."

His sister, though, could not wait. She went to the stables; she searched in every direction. No Thomas, no horse, no saddle. She asked the servants. Nobody had seen him. Then she went back and told her mother what had happened.

"You must have fallen asleep and dreamed it," her mother said.

"No, indeed! I was wide awake — I spoke to him, and he gave me no answer, but such a look!"

All that afternoon she felt an uneasiness she could not reason herself out of.

The next morning a messenger came from the river Trench with dismal news.

The bodies of the young man and his horse had been found drowned a little way below the ford of the river.

It seemed that when he reached the riverbank he found the water swollen well past its usual depth by the recent rains. Since he would have to swim the stream with his horse, he took off his clothes and made them into a packet that he fastened on his shoulders. It was thought that the force of the fast current shifted the bundle, and that this dragged his head under the water and held it there, with no way for him to raise it. All of this was worked out from the position and appearance of the bodies when they were found.

Going by the time he had been seen passing a house that stood near the stream, on his way to the ford, he must clearly have met his fate at the very moment his sister saw, or thought she saw, him passing in front of her.

When these sad details were told to me, I could not help asking, "Mother, isn't it possible this was a dream?"

"A dream? No, indeed, my child. I was perfectly wide awake — as wide awake as I am at this moment. I am not superstitious. I have never believed in ghosts or witches. But nothing will ever persuade me that this was not a warning sent from God, to prepare me for my brother's death."

And anyone who knew her rational good sense — how free she was of fancies and fears, and the calm self-possession that never deserted her under the most trying circumstances — would come close to being won over to seeing the thing as she saw it.

The order to evacuate Fort Dearborn and move the troops to Fort Howard, at Green Bay, had now come. The family circle was going to be broken up. Our mother, our sister Mrs. Helm, and her little boy were to go back with us to Fort Winnebago; the rest of the family, except Robert, were to move with the command to Green Bay.

The schooner Napoleon was to be sent from Detroit to carry the troops and all their goods and chattels to their new post. Our own party was to make the trip by land — though we were going to pick a shorter and pleasanter route than the one we had come by. My husband had arrived with his Frenchmen, Petaille Grignon and Simon Lecuyer, and every hand was now busy with the preparations for breaking up and moving out.
