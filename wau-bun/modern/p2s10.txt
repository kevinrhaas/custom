The general despondency was relieved a little on the 14th of August, when Captain Wells arrived with fifteen friendly Miamis.

About this brave man, who cuts such a conspicuous figure in our frontier annals, it is enough to say here that he had lived among the Indians since he was a boy and so knew their character and their habits perfectly.

He had heard at Fort Wayne about the order to evacuate the fort at Chicago, and knowing how determined the Pottowattamies were to be hostile, he had made a fast march across country to keep his relative Captain Heald and his troops from being exposed to certain destruction.

But he came, in the phrase, all too late. When he reached the post he found the ammunition already destroyed and the provisions already given away to the Indians. There was nothing else for it now, and every preparation was made for the troops to march the following morning.

That same afternoon a second council was held with the Indians. They were furious about the destruction of the ammunition and the liquor.

For all the care taken to keep it quiet, the sound of the barrel-heads being knocked in had given away what was being done the night before. In fact so much liquor had been poured into the river that the next morning the water tasted, as one man put it, like "strong grog."

Grumbling and threats were heard everywhere among the savages. It was plain that the first moment the troops were out in the open, they would meet some expression of that disappointment and resentment.
