We soon learned that Green Bay was in a great panic over the Sauks. The people seemed to have got it into their heads that the enemy would call in here on their way to Canada, to put themselves under the protection of the British Government. Exactly how they were to get to Canada from this point — whether they would stop and build themselves bark canoes for the trip, or whether they would charter one of Mr. Newbery's schooners — the good people did not seem to have entirely settled in their minds. One thing is certain: a good part of the citizens were frightened nearly to death, and were completely convinced that there was no safety for them anywhere but inside the walls of the old broken-down fort, from which nearly all the troops had been pulled out and sent to Fort Winnebago some time before.

Their fears were made a great deal worse by a story some traveler brought in, that he had slept the night on the very spot where the Sauks ate their breakfast the next morning. Now, since the Sauks were known to be on very short rations, there was every reason to think that if the man had waited half an hour longer they would have eaten him — so he was reckoned to have had a miraculous escape.

Our own friends and acquaintances were nowhere near joining in these fears. Anybody who thought about the kind of country the Sauks would have to cross, and the large and capable body of whites who would meet them the moment they came anywhere near it, could see at once how completely improbable such a move was. There were some, though, who could not be talked out of the belief that there was no safety except in running, and the arrival of the Mariner was watched for more and more eagerly as the anxiety kept building.
