Very soon after we were settled into our new home at the Agency, we tried starting a little Sunday school. Edwin, Harry and Josette were our most reliable scholars, but besides them there were the two little Manaigres, Thérèse Paquette, and her mother's half-sister Florence Courville, a pretty young girl of fifteen. Not one of the girls had so much as learned her letters. They spoke only French — or rather the Canadian patois — and it was extremely hard to give them the sound of the words and their meaning at the same time, which they were careful to ask about. On top of that there was the work of correcting false ideas and undoing the ignorance and superstition that stood as such a formidable obstacle in the way of any rational improvement. We did our best, though, and had the satisfaction of seeing them, after a while, making really respectable progress with their spelling-book — and, what was more encouraging still, picking up a degree of light and knowledge about better things.

As time went on, though, Florence was often missing from her class. "Her sister," she said, "couldn't always spare her. She wanted her to keep house while she herself went over on Sunday to visit her friends the Roys, who lived on the Wisconsin."

We reasoned with Madame Paquette about it. Couldn't she spare Florence at some hour of the day? We would gladly teach her on a weekday, since she seemed anxious to learn — but we had always been told that there was no time for that.

"Well — she would see. Madame Alum (Helm) and Madame John were so kind!"

There was no improvement in her regularity, though. After a while Manaigre was persuaded to send his children to Mr. Cadle's mission school at Green Bay. Thérèse went with them, and very soon Florence stopped coming altogether.

From that time on we were obliged to keep our teaching within our own family circle.
