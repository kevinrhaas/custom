The payment was over now, and the Indians had scattered and gone off to their wintering grounds. The traders had gone too, carrying a good share of the silver with them, in exchange for which every family had supplied itself as far as it could with clothing, guns, traps, ammunition, and the rest of the necessities for the winter. The Indians are good at a bargain. They are not easily taken in. On the contrary, they know at once when a charge is exorbitant, and a trader who tries his shrewdness on them is certain to be given a telling nickname that sticks to him ever after.

M. Rolette, for instance, they called "Ah-kay-zaup-ee-tah," five more — because, as they said, no matter how many skins they offered him for an article, his terms were always "five more."

A lady once said to him, "Oh, M. Rolette, I would not be in the Indian trade for anything. It seems to me a system of cheating the poor Indians."

"Let me tell you, madame," he replied, with great naïveté, "cheating the Indians is not so easy a thing as you imagine. I have been trying it these twenty years, and I have never succeeded yet!"

We had settled down by now to a quiet domestic life. Everything ran on the military system, and it broke the day up pleasantly for us: the bugle-call at reveille, followed by the music of a very good band; the light, lively strains for "sick-call," and soon after that for breakfast; the longer ceremony of guard-mounting; "The Old English Roast-Beef" to announce the dinner hour; the sweet, plaintive "Lochaber No More" at retreat, followed most incongruously by "The Little Cock-Sparrow"; and finally the long, rolling tattoo late in the evening. With books, music, and drawing on top of the household work, the time seemed to fly faster than it ever had before. Sunday was when I missed my Eastern home most. I had planned in advance what we should do the first time that sacred day came round under our own roof. "At least," I said to myself, "we shall have the Sabbath's quiet and rest, and among other things I can do poor Louisa some good by giving her a few extra lessons of a serious kind."

So while she was clearing away the breakfast things, I said to her, "Now, Louisa, get all your work finished and everything put away neatly, and then come back up here to me."

"Yes, ma'am."

We sat down with our books and read, and waited; and waited and read another hour — no Louisa.

There was music and the sound of voices on the parade ground in front of our windows, but that did not distract us; we heard it every day.

At last I had to go and see what could be keeping my damsel so long. I went down to the kitchen. The breakfast things stood on the table, the kettles and the spider sat on the hearth, the fire was out, and the kitchen was empty.

Coming back into the hall, which ran the whole length of the house and opened at the front onto the parade, I saw a group gathered out in the open, of every shade and color, and in the middle of it one round, woolly head I could not possibly mistake, bobbing up and down, now on this side, now on that, while the whole group shouted with laughter.

"Louisa," I called, "come here. What are you doing out there?"

"Looking at inspection."

"But why aren't your breakfast things washed and your kitchen swept?

Did I not tell you I wanted you to come up and learn your lessons?"

"Yes, ma'am, but I had to see inspection first. Everybody looks at inspection on Sunday."

I found it was useless to hope for anything more from Louisa than an afternoon lesson, and I had to be satisfied with that.

It seemed to me that it would be very pleasant, and perhaps do some good, if everyone in the garrison came together on that day — surely one of our number could be found who would read a portion of the church service, with a sermon out of one of our various collections.

I approached the subject carefully, with a question along these lines:

"Is there nobody among the officers who is religiously inclined?"

"Oh, yes," said the man I asked. "There is S——. When he is half tipsy he takes his Bible and his Newton's Works to bed and cries over them. He thinks that makes him excessively pious."

S—— was one of the officers who had never called on us. It was fair to conclude that if his religious principles did not correct his own bad habits, they were not likely to do much toward improving anyone else's, so there seemed no point in asking his help with any plan for keeping the Lord's day better.

We had to content ourselves with writing to our friends in the East, asking them to take an interest in getting a missionary sent out to us who could act as chaplain to the garrison — a plan the officers seemed to like. Any hope of holding united religious services was laid aside for the present.

The post-surgeon got a furlough, and his place was taken by Dr. Newhall of Galena; so, with the addition of his gentle, quiet wife, our circle of ladies now came to three. Here we were in a wilderness, and yet how contented and happy we were!

That enviable calm in our home was soon to be replaced by gloom. A Frenchman named Letendre appeared one day without warning. He had come from Chicago with the distressing news that our dear relative Dr. Wolcott was gravely — in fact hopelessly — ill. My husband began at once to prepare to leave immediately. I begged to be allowed to go with him, but he had to refuse me, because of the speed at which he meant to travel. Within a few hours his provisions, his horses, and everything else needed for the journey were ready, and he set off with Petaille Grignon, who always went with him on such expeditions, leaving Letendre to follow once he had rested.

The hours of his absence were sad and dreary ones, for all the kind efforts of our friends to cheer me. In a few days another messenger brought me word that Dr. W.'s illness had ended fatally. That noble heart, so full of warm and kindly affection, had stopped beating, and those who had loved and honored him were left sad and desolate indeed.

My husband came back as soon as he could possibly leave the family, and it was lucky he had put it off no longer, because the winter now set in, and set in hard.

Our quarters were roomy, but they had been built out of green trees from the forest, cut down and sawn into boards by the soldiers themselves, so the wood shrank and warped a good deal and left many a gaping crack. Stuffing the cracks with cotton batting and pasting strips of paper over them took up a great many of our spare hours.

Then the chimneys, in spite of all those currents of air, which you would think might have created a draught, had a sad habit of smoking. To cure this, the commanding officer ordered a couple of gun-barrels sawn off and set into the hearth, one on either side of the fireplace, in the hope that air coming up from the room below would help carry the smoke where it belonged, up the chimney.

The morning after this had been done, Louisa was washing the hearth.

"Please, ma'am," she said, "what are these things put in here for?"

I explained what they were for.

"Oh, I am so glad that is all it is! Uncle Ephraim" — Major Twiggs's servant — "said they were to be filled with powder and fired off on Christmas Day, and he was terribly afraid they would blow the house up with us in it."

Ephraim, who was a most faithful and valuable servant, often amused himself by playing on the credulity of the younger members of the colored fraternity.

"Is it true," Louisa asked one day, "that Pillon and Plante were once prairie-wolves?"

"Prairie-wolves! What an idea! Why do you ask such a foolish question?"

"Because Uncle Ephraim says they, and all the Frenchmen around here, were prairie-wolves once, and that living so near white people they grow after a while to be like them and learn to talk and dress like them. And then, when they get old, they turn back into prairie-wolves again, and all the wolves the officers bait with their dogs used to be Frenchmen."

After a while, though, I stopped correcting Uncle Ephraim's stories, because I was gradually coming round to the conviction that my little colored damsel was nowhere near as simple and unsophisticated as she wanted me to believe, and that I was the one being imposed upon after all.

The snow that winter was prodigious and the cold was intense. Water would freeze in our parlors a very short distance from the fire, because although the fatigue-parties kept the halls piled with wood almost to the ceiling, that did not make up for having the wide doors standing open to the outside air for a great part of the day while they carried it in. We Northerners would have had wood-houses built specially for the purpose, and would not only have kept our great hall doors shut but would have protected them with a "hurricane-house" besides. But the Florida frontier was not a climate in which our Southern bachelors could have picked up the knowledge that comes in useful when the thermometer stands at twenty-five degrees below zero — the point at which brandy froze in the sideboard.
