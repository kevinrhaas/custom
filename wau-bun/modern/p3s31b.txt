It was an exciting and somewhat risky passage down the rapids and over the Grand Chûte, a fall of several feet, but we got through it safely, and toward evening the boat reached the settlement of the Waubanakees at the head of the Little Chûte. These are the Stockbridge or Brothertown Indians, what is left of the old Mohicans, who a few years before had emigrated from Oneida County, in the State of New York, to a tract the United States had granted them on the fertile banks of the Fox River. They had already cleared extensive openings in the forest and built some substantial, comfortable houses near the river banks, which are quite high there and mostly covered with enormous trees.

We decided to ask these people for hospitality, to the extent of borrowing a corner of their fire to boil our tea-kettle and bake the short-cake that had been standing in for bread for nearly two days now. Making it had been a subject of a great deal of merriment. The ingredients — Powell's black flour, some salt, and a little butter — were mixed in the tin box that had held our meat. The box was then turned upside down and, once it had been properly cleaned, served in place of a dough-board. The vinegar bottle did duty as a rolling-pin, and a shallow tin dish was the apparatus for baking. The Waubanakees were good enough to lend us an iron bake-kettle and to superintend the cooking of our cake, after Harry had carried it up to their house.

They showed themselves so kind and hospitable that the boat's crew took the resolution of asking for a lodging on shore, by way of relief after three nights in their crowded quarters in the boat. Arthur and Mâtâ soon adopted the same idea, and we were invited to follow their example, with an assurance that the houses were extremely neat and orderly.

We preferred to stay on board, though, since it was a fine night and everything was so comfortably arranged in the boat, and we kept Edwin and Josette with us.

The boat was moored tight, because the Little Chûte was just below us, and if our craft broke loose in that rapid current and drifted down over the falls, it would be a very serious matter. As an extra precaution, one man was left on board to keep everything safe and in order. With these arrangements made, the others climbed the bank and took up their night's lodging in the Waubanakee cabins.

It was a beautiful, calm, moonlit night, the air just warm enough to be agreeable, and the gentle murmur of the rapids and of the fall, not far off, soon lulled our party to sleep. How long we had slept we had no idea, when we were woken by a rushing wind. It bent the poles supporting the awning, snapped them, and then, as another gust came after it, tent and blanket went off on the blast down the stream. The moonlight was gone, but a flash of lightning showed them sailing away like a spectre into the distance.
