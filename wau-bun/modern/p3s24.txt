There was a young man named Shee-shee-banze (the Little Duck) paddling his canoe along the shore of the lake.

Two girls came down to the water's edge, and, seeing him, the elder said to the younger, "Let's call to him to take us for a sail."

It should be said that in every Indian story where two or more sisters are the characters, the elder is invariably made out to be silly, ridiculous, and repellent, and the younger wise and beautiful.

In this case the younger objected. "Oh, no," she said, "let's not do anything like that. What will he think of us?"

But the other kept at it, and called to him, "Ho! Come and take us into your canoe." The young man did as he was asked, and, bringing the canoe to shore, took them in with him.

"Who are you?" asked the elder sister.

"I am Way-gee-mar-kin," he answered, "the great chief."

This Way-gee-mar-kin was something of a fairy, because when he was surrounded by his followers and wanted to do them a kindness, he had a way of coughing lightly, and out of his mouth would fly quantities of silver brooches, ear-bobs, and other ornaments, which his people made a habit of scrambling for, each one trying, just as in more civilized life, to get more than his share.

So the elder sister said, "If you are Way-gee-mar-kin, let's see you cough."

Shee-shee-banze had a few of these silver ornaments that he had picked up in a scramble, and he kept them stowed in the sides of his mouth in case of emergency. So he coughed a few times, in a spasmodic way, and brought out several, which the girl eagerly grabbed.

After a while, as they paddled along, a fine noble elk came out of the forest and walked down to the water to drink.

"What is that?" asked the spokeswoman, since the younger sister sat silent and modest the whole time.

"It is my dog that I hunt with."

"Call him over to us, so I can see him."

Shee-shee-banze called, but the elk turned and fled into the woods.

"He doesn't seem to obey you, though."

"No — that is because you fill him with disgust, so he runs away from you."

Before long a bear appeared at the water's edge.

"What is that?"

"One of my servants."

Again he was asked to call him, and, when the call was ignored, he gave the same reason as before.

Their outing came to an end at last. There had been a little magic in it, because although the young girls had believed themselves to be in a canoe, in fact there was no canoe at all. They only imagined there was.

Now, Shee-shee-banze lived with his grandmother, and it was to her lodge that he brought his young friends.

They waited outside while he went in.

"Grandmother," he said, "I have brought you two young girls who are to be your daughters-in-law. Invite them into your lodge."

At that the old woman called out, "Ho! Come in," and in they came. They were made welcome and given the best of everything.

Meanwhile the real Way-gee-mar-kin, the great chief, was making preparations for a grand feast. As he was sending his messenger out with the invitations, he said to him, "Be very careful to invite Shee-shee-banze to the feast. He is the smallest and meanest person in the tribe, so you must use double the ceremony with him, or he will be apt to think he has been slighted."

Shee-shee-banze was sitting in his lodge with his new friends when the messenger arrived.

"Ho! Shee-shee-banze," he called, "you are invited to a great feast that Way-gee-mar-kin is giving tonight for all his subjects."

But Shee-shee-banze took no notice of the invitation at all. He only whistled and pretended not to hear. The messenger said his piece over again, and then, finding it got no attention, went on his way.

The young girls looked at each other through this scene, thoroughly astonished. Finally the elder spoke up.

"What does this mean?" she said. "Why does he call you Shee-shee-banze, and invite you to go and visit Way-gee-mar-kin?"

"Oh," said Shee-shee-banze, "that is one of my followers who always likes to be a little impudent. I have to put up with it sometimes — but you noticed that I treated him with silent contempt."

The messenger went back to the chief and reported the way the invitation had been received.

"Oh," said the good-natured chief, "that is because he feels poor and insignificant. Go back again — call him by my name, and make him a flourishing speech."

The messenger carried out his errand exactly as he had been told.

"Way-gee-mar-kin," he said pompously, "a great feast is to be given tonight, and I am sent to request most respectfully the honor of your company!"

"Didn't I tell you?" said Shee-shee-banze to the girls. Then, nodding with careless condescension, he added, "Tell them I'll come."

That night Shee-shee-banze dressed himself in his very best paint, feathers, and ornaments — but before he left he took his grandmother aside.

"Be sure," he said, "that you watch these young people closely until I get back. Shut your lodge up tight, tight. Let nobody come in or go out, and above all, don't fall asleep."

Having given his orders, he went on his way.

The grandmother did her best to stay awake, but as the night wore on she found herself getting sleepier and sleepier, so she took a strong cord and laced it across the mat that hung over the entrance to the lodge, the way the Indians lace up the mouths of their bags. Then, having seen everything secure and the girls quiet in bed, she lay down and was soon comfortably asleep.

The young girls, meanwhile, were dying of curiosity to know what had become of Shee-shee-banze, and as soon as they were sure the old lady was asleep they got ready to follow him and see what was going on. Afraid the grandmother might wake and find them gone, they took two logs of wood and arranged them under the blanket so as to look like people sleeping quietly. Then they cut the cords that fastened the door, and, guided by the sounds of the music and the dancing and the merrymaking, they soon found their way to Way-gee-mar-kin's house.

When they came in, they saw the chief seated on a throne, surrounded by light and splendor. Everything was joy and amusement. The room was crowded with courtiers, all dressed in the most brilliant array. The strangers looked around for their friend Shee-shee-banze, but he was nowhere to be seen.

Every so often the chief would cough, and a shower of silver ornaments and precious things would fly in every direction, and instantly the company would start scrambling to gather them up and keep them.

As they all rushed forward like that, the brides-to-be caught sight of their poor little friend crowded into a corner, where nobody took any notice of him except to shove him aside or step on him whenever he was in the way. He let out pitiful little squeaks as one person and another handled him that way, but he was too busy looking after himself to notice that the two he had left snug at home in the lodge were watching the whole thing.

At last the signal was given for the company to leave — all but the two young women, whom Way-gee-mar-kin had had his eye on, and to whom he had sent, by one of his assistants, great offers to persuade them to stay with him and become his wives.

Poor Shee-shee-banze went back to his lodge — and imagine his consternation at finding the door open!

"Ho! Grandmother," he cried, "is this how you keep watch?"

The old woman started up. "There are my daughters-in-law," she said, pointing at the two logs of wood. Shee-shee-banze threw himself down on the ground between them. His back was broken by hitting them so hard, but he did not care about that. He thought of nothing but revenge, and getting his sweethearts back.

He waited only long enough to get some powerful poison and prepare it, and then he stole softly back to Way-gee-mar-kin's wigwam. Everything was quiet, and he crept in without making the slightest sound. There lay the chief, with a young girl on either side of him.

They were all sound asleep, the chief lying on his back with his mouth wide open. Before he knew anything about it the poison was down his throat, and Shee-shee-banze had slipped quietly back to his own lodge.

Next morning the cry went through the village that Way-gee-mar-kin had been found dead in his bed. Naturally it was put down to over-indulgence at the feast. Everything was grief and lamentation. "Let's go and tell poor Shee-shee-banze," said one of them. "He was so fond of Way-gee-mar-kin."

They found him sitting on a bank, fishing. He had been up since first light getting ready to receive the news.

He had caught two or three fish, and, taking out their bladders, had filled them with blood and tied them under his arm. When Way-gee-mar-kin's friends saw him, they called out, "Oh! Shee-shee-banze — your friend Way-gee-mar-kin is dead!"

With a gesture of despair, Shee-shee-banze drew his knife and plunged it in — not into his heart, but into the blood-filled bladders he had got ready. As he dropped to the ground, apparently lifeless, the messengers began to reproach themselves: "Oh, why did we tell him so suddenly? We might have known he wouldn't survive it. Poor Shee-shee-banze! He loved Way-gee-mar-kin so."

To their great surprise, the day after the funeral, Shee-shee-banze came walking toward the dead chief's wigwam. As he walked he sang, or rather chanted, to a monotonous tune, the following: "Way-gee-mar-kin is dead, is dead, I know who killed him.

I guess it was I — I guess it was I."

The whole village was up in arms. Everybody flew after the murderer, but he dodged them and got away to a safe place.

Soon afterward he showed himself again, mincing along as he walked and singing to the same tune as before, "If you wish to take and punish me, Let the widows come and catch me."

It seemed a good idea, and the young women were urged to go and lure the culprit into the village, so that the dead man's friends could lay hold of him.

Out they went on their errand. Shee-shee-banze would let them get close, and then dance off a little way. Now he would allow them almost to reach him, now he would back off a little in front of them, singing the whole time, "Come, pretty widows, come and catch me."

So he decoyed them along, using honeyed words and flattering speeches now and then, until he had talked them into going back with him to his lodge and living there with him.

The murdered chief's friends were scandalized at such fickleness, and resolved to punish all three of them as soon as they could be caught.

They surrounded his lodge with shouts and threats, but Shee-shee-banze and his two brides had managed to slip past them and reach his canoe, which lay in the river close by.

They were barely on board when the escape was discovered. The whole crowd flew after them. Some plunged into the stream and got hold of the canoe. In the struggle it was upset — but the moment they touched the water, whether from the magical properties of the canoe or the grandmother's skill in necromancy, they were turned into ducks and flew off quacking.

Ever since then, the waterfowl of this species are always found in parties of three — two females and a male.

The Canard de France, or Mallard, and the Brancheuse, or Wood Duck, have different habits from these, and fly in pairs. In fact the wood duck's constancy is said to be so great that if he loses his mate he never takes another, but goes mourning to the end of his days.
