With all of that in front of him, then, their Father felt that the closest watchfulness was needed, and that the strongest arguments would have to be brought out to keep the young men of the Winnebagoes loyal to the Americans. About the older men he felt quite sure. Around fifty lodges had come at the start of the troubles and camped around our house, saying that if the Sauks attacked us it would have to be after killing them first. We knew them well, and we had complete confidence in what they told us.

Having them so close gave us a feeling of protection, but it also gave us a channel for the most exciting and unsettling news, delivered daily. As the scene of operations came nearer and nearer, their runners kept bringing word in — now that "Captain Barney's head had been recognized in the Sauk camp, where it had been brought the day before," next that "the Sauks were carrying Lieutenant Beall's head on a pole in front of them as they marched out to meet the whites." Sometimes it was a story we found out afterward was unhappily true, like the murder of their own Agent, M. St. Vrain, at Kellogg's Grove, by the very Sauks who ought to have protected him.
