Finally, about the beginning of November, two months after the appointed time, Governor Porter arrived with the annuity money, along with Major Forsyth and Mr. Kercheval. The Indians were gathered again, the payment was made, and having laid in a larger stock of ammunition than usual — they could see they needed a good hunt to make up for what they had gone without, then and now — they set off for their wintering grounds.

We were about to move ourselves, to our considerable satisfaction. In spite of the Indian troubles, work on the new Agency House — permission to build it had come from the Government at last, after a great deal of delay — had gone steadily ahead, and soon after the Governor and his party left, we moved in.

We had only been settled a few weeks when Lieutenant Davies appeared one morning, just as we were sitting down to breakfast, with a face full of alarm. The Indian prisoners had escaped from the black-hole! The commanding officer, Colonel Cutler, had sent for Mr. Kinzie to come over to the Fort and talk over what was to be done.

The prisoners had probably started work very soon after being put into the black-hole, a dungeon in the basement of the guard-house. They had noticed that their meals came regularly, three times a day, and that in between they were left entirely to themselves. With their knives they began digging an opening, spreading the earth they took out over the floor of their prison as they went. A blanket was laid over the hole, and one of them was always sitting on it well before the hour when the soldier in charge of them was due to appear. Whenever that regular visit came, the Indians were always found sitting and smoking in the most orderly, quiet way. There was never anything about them to raise a suspicion.

The prisoners had never read the memoirs of Baron Trenck, but they had watched what badgers do, and, profiting by that example, they worked away, curving the opening in a spiral, until in about six weeks they came out into the open air beyond the walls of the Fort.

To be as little encumbered as possible in their flight, they left their blankets behind, and though it was bitter December weather they took to the woods and prairies with nothing on but their calico shirts and leggings. We can readily believe that hope and triumph kept them comfortably warm until they reached shelter among their friends.

It would compromise our own standing as loyal, patriotic citizens to say how quietly delighted this news made us.

The question now was how to get the fugitives back again. The Agent could promise nothing more than to send word to the chiefs and put before them the officers' wish that the prisoners should give themselves up once more, and so clear the men who had had charge of them of the carelessness the Government would be very likely to lay at their door.

When a good many of the chiefs came to the Agency on New Year's Day, as they always did, their Father laid the matter before them.

The Indians answered that if they saw the young men, they would tell them what the officers would like them to do. They themselves could do nothing about it. They had kept their part of the bargain by bringing the men in once and putting them into the officers' hands. The Government had had them in its power and could not keep them — now it could go and catch them itself.

The Government, having had some experience the previous summer in "catching Indians," wisely decided to let the matter drop.
