About this time another thing happened that caused a good deal of excitement in our little community. Robineau, the striker from the blacksmith shop at Sugar Creek, near the Four Lakes, arrived at the Agency on a very cold day. He had come to get medical help for Mâtâ's eldest daughter, Sophy, who had fallen on the ice while sliding on the lake and been badly hurt. Her father was away — he had gone to Prairie du Chien to put his youngest daughter in school. Two or three days had gone by since the accident, a high fever had set in, and the poor girl was suffering terribly, so it had seemed best to send Robineau to us for advice and help, leaving Turcotte and a friendly Indian woman from a lodge nearby to take charge of her.

When the case was put to the commanding officer, he did not think it prudent to let the surgeon leave the post, but he very cheerfully gave leave of absence to Currie, the hospital steward, a young man who knew something of medicine and surgery.

Since it mattered that Sophy should have an experienced nurse, we engaged Madame Bellaire, the wife of the Frenchman who was usually employed to carry express to Chicago; and as a help and a companion for her, Agathe — a daughter of Day-kau-ray who lived with Paquette's family — was added to the party.

I shall have more to say about Agathe further on.

The weather was bitterly cold when Robineau, Currie, and the two women set out for Sugar Creek, about forty miles away. We had given them a good supply of rice, crackers, tea, and sugar for the invalid, and all of it, along with their own provisions for the road, was packed on the horse Robineau had ridden to the Portage. They were expected to reach the place on the second day.

Imagine our surprise, then, when Turcotte appeared on the fourth day after they left, to ask why Robineau had not come back with help for poor Sophy. There was only one explanation. Robineau had guided them as badly as he had guided the boat at the Grande Chûte the summer before, and though he could not wreck them this time, he had certainly lost them somewhere in the woods or on the prairies. One comfort was that they could hardly starve, since the rice and crackers would feed them for several days; and with Agathe, who must be used to that kind of life, they were bound to come across Indians eventually and be brought back to the Portage.

Still, day after day passed and we got no news of them. Turcotte went back to Sugar Creek with comforts and prescriptions for Sophy, and Colonel Cutler sent out a party to hunt for the missing ones. Of them all, poor Currie, with his delicate constitution, was the one we pitied most.

With the snow coming down and the wind howling, we could do nothing but walk from window to window, watching, hoping to see somebody appear in the distance. There were no Indians at hand we could send out to search, and by the tenth day we had nearly given up in despair.

Then all at once the joyful news was brought to us: "They are found! They are at the Fort!" A party of soldiers who had been out searching had come upon them at Hastings's Woods, twelve miles off, making their way slowly and feebly back toward the Portage. They knew they were on the right road, but they barely had the strength to follow it.

They were worn out with cold and hunger — their provisions had run out two days earlier — and they had thought seriously of killing the horse and eating it. The only thing that stopped them was that Currie could not go on foot, and they dreaded having to leave him in the woods to die.

Agathe had been certain from the start that they were on the wrong track, but Robineau, with his usual stubbornness, kept to it until it brought them out at the Rock River. Then he had to admit his mistake, and they began retracing their steps.

Agathe, as her people always do, had carried her hatchet with her, so they always had a fire at night and branches to shelter them from the storms. Without that they must certainly have died.

Two things in the story stirred something stronger in us even than sympathy. The first was that the miserable Robineau had demanded of Currie, first all his money and then his watch, as the price of bringing the party back to the right path — which he swore he knew perfectly well.

The second was that Bellaire gave his kind, excellent wife a hearty flogging "for going off," as he put it, "on such a fool's errand."

The second offender was beyond our jurisdiction, but Monsieur Robineau was discharged on the spot and warned that he might think himself lucky to escape being prosecuted for swindling.

I am glad to say that Sophy Mâtâ, for whose sake all this suffering had been gone through, was quite recovered by the time her father got back from the Prairie.
