"This one carried me toward the lake while I struggled and fought him. Fast as I was hurried along, I recognized, as I passed them, the lifeless remains of the poor surgeon. Some murderous tomahawk had stretched him out on the very spot where I had last seen him.

"I was plunged straight into the water and held there by a strong hand, however hard I resisted. But I soon realized that my captor did not mean to drown me, for he was holding me firmly in a position that kept my head above the surface. That reassured me, and looking at him closely I recognized him, in spite of the paint he had disguised himself with, as The Black Partridge.

"When the firing had nearly stopped, my rescuer carried me out of the water and took me up the sand-banks. It was a burning August morning, and walking through the sand soaked through as I was hurt and exhausted me beyond describing. I stooped and took off my shoes to shake out the sand that nearly filled them, and a squaw seized them and carried them off, so I had to go on without them.

"When we reached the prairie my father met me and told me my husband was safe and only slightly wounded. They led me gently back toward the Chicago River; the Pottowattamie camp was along its south bank. At one point they put me on a horse without a saddle, but the motion was unbearable and I sprang off. Held up partly by my kind guide Black Partridge and partly by another Indian, Pee-so-tum, who had a scalp dangling from his hand that I knew by the black ribbon around the queue was Captain Wells's, I dragged my fainting steps to one of the wigwams.

"The wife of Wau-bee-nee-mah, a chief from the Illinois River, was standing nearby, and seeing the state I was in she took a kettle, dipped up some water from a stream that ran close by, threw in some maple-sugar, stirred it with her hand and gave it to me to drink. That one act of kindness in the middle of so many horrors touched me more than I can say; but my attention was soon pulled away to other things.

"The fort had been given over to plunder by everyone who stayed behind when the troops marched out. The cattle had been shot down as they ran loose, and lay dead and dying all around. That butchery had begun just as we were leaving the fort. I remember distinctly a remark of Ensign Ronan's while the firing was going on. 'That,' he said, turning to me, 'is to be our fate — to be shot down like brutes!'

"'Well, sir,' said the commanding officer, who overheard him, 'are you afraid?'

"'No,' replied the high-spirited young man, 'I can march up to the enemy where you dare not show your face.' And the way he fought afterward showed this was no idle boast.

"As the sound of the firing gradually died away and stragglers from the victorious party came dropping in, I had confirmation of what my father had told me hurriedly when we met on the lake shore: that the whites had surrendered, after losing about two-thirds of their number. Through the interpreter, Peresh Leclerc, they had made terms — that their lives would be spared, along with the lives of the women and children who were left, and that they would be delivered up at one of the British posts unless traders in the Indian country ransomed them first. It seems the wounded prisoners were not counted as covered by that agreement, and a horrid scene followed when they were brought into camp.

"One old squaw, maddened by the loss of friends, or worked up by all the bloodshed around her, seemed possessed by a demoniac ferocity. She seized a stable-fork and set on one wretched man who was lying there groaning and writhing in the agony of his wounds, made worse by the scorching sun. With a delicacy of feeling you would hardly have expected in such circumstances, Wau-bee-nee-mah stretched a mat across two poles between me and this dreadful scene. That spared me some part of the sight of its horrors, though I could not entirely shut my ears to the cries of the man suffering. The following night five more of the wounded prisoners were tomahawked."
