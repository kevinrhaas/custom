This is probably the right place to say something about the Canadian voyageurs, or engagés — a class of men who have now very nearly disappeared.

They were like no other men anywhere. Like the poet, they seemed to have been born to the work. Tough, hard-wearing, resourceful, and cheerful, they had a temperament that could bend itself to any situation. No difficulty stumped them and no hardship discouraged them, and their affectionate natures led them to form the warmest attachments — to their bourgeois, or master, and just as much to the native people among whom their engagements took them.

Montreal — or Marrialle, as they pronounced it themselves — was their depot. That was where the agents hired to fill the quotas for the various companies and traders found the men to pick from.

The usual terms of engagement were four to six hundred livres a year in old Quebec currency for wages, with rations of a quart of lyed corn and two ounces of tallow a day, or "its equivalent in whatever sort of food is to be found in the Indian country." There are cases on record of voyageurs living cheerfully on fresh fish and maple sugar for an entire winter, when they were cut off from anything else.

There was a saying: "Keep an engagé on his corn and tallow and he'll serve you well — give him pork and bread and he's soon past managing." They treat the terms of an engagement as binding to the letter. An old trader, M. Berthelet, once hired a crew at Montreal. The agreement was that they should eat when their bourgeois ate, and eat what he ate. The old gentleman meant it as a joke, but the simple Canadians took it for a stroke of remarkable luck that had handed them such luxurious prospects. The bourgeois stuffed his pockets with crackers and, whenever he was sure nobody was watching, would sneak one out and eat it. Pipe followed pipe; the men got hungry; but seeing that no meal was being got ready for the bourgeois either, they went without complaining.

Finally it got too serious to bear — they could not stand it any longer. In their misery they begged to be let out of the bargain, and were delighted to settle for the ordinary rations instead of the fine fare they had been promising themselves at their master's table.

At Mackinac, which was the clearing-house of the fur trade, the voyageur was advanced a small part of his wages to buy his winter outfit: his pipes and tobacco, his needles and thread, some lengths of bright-colored ribbon, and red and yellow gartering — quality binding — to trade to the Indians for the little things he needed. If he was bound for Lake Superior or some post far to the north, where such things could not easily be got, one or two smoked deer-skins for moccasins were added.

Kitted out like that, he went into his three years' service: to work hard all day, and to laugh, joke, sing, and tell stories when evening brought rest and freedom.

Now and then there was a man who insisted on the exact letter of his agreement about what work he had been hired for — though as a rule the engagé held himself ready to carry out his bourgeois's orders as faithfully as any vassal ever obeyed his chief.

There is a story about M. St. Jean, a trader on the Upper Mississippi, who one day ordered one of his Frenchmen to go out with a party into the forest to chop wood. The man refused. He had not been hired, he said, to chop wood.

"Ah — what were you hired for, then?"

"To steer a boat."

"Very well. Steer a boat, then, since you'd rather."

It was the middle of winter. The objector was marched down to the river, put in the stern of the boat, and left there — the boat frozen fast in the ice.

A couple of hours at his rightful occupation, with the thermometer below zero, made him perfectly willing to join the wood-chopping party, and he never again thought it wise to pick his own work.

There is a kind of aristocracy in the voyageur service that is quite funny. The engagement is usually for three years. A first-year man, called a mangeur-de-lard, or pork-eater, is looked down on with utter contempt by a hivernant, a man who has already wintered in the country. The hivernant will not only refuse to keep company with him — if the pork-eater invites him to a friendly drink he will invent some excuse to say no. The most hopeless drunk among them, tortured as he is by wanting his favorite indulgence, will never let himself be tempted into breaking this custom.

After his first winter, the mangeur-de-lard graduates out of the freshman class, and gets to lord it over all the new arrivals in his turn.

Another thing about the voyageurs is their fondness for twisting their bourgeois's name into something funny that sounds like it. Kinzie, for instance, would be Quinze nez to one man (fifteen noses) and Singé to another (monkeyfied). Mr. Kercheval was called Mons. Court-cheval (short horse), the Judge of Probate le Juge Trop-bête (too foolish), and so on. Here is a case in point.

Mr. Shaw, one of the agents of the Northwest Fur Company, had spent many years on the frontier, and the voyageurs called him Monsieur Le Chat. When he left the Indian country he married a Canadian lady and became the father of several children. Some years after his return to Canada, his old foreman, a man named Louis la Liberté, went to Montreal for the winter. He had heard about his old bourgeois's marriage and was eager to see him.

Mr. Shaw was walking in the Champ de Mars with a couple of officers when La Liberté spotted him. He ran straight up, grabbed him by both hands, and greeted him: "Ah! mon cher Monsieur le Chat, comment vous portez-vous?" (My dear Mr. Cat, how do you do?)

"Très-bien, Louizon."

"Et comment se porte Madame la Chatte?" (How is the mother cat?)

"Bien, bien, Louizon, elle est très-bien." (She is very well.)

"Et tous les petits Chatons?" (And all the kittens?)

That was more than Mr. Shaw could take. He said shortly that the kittens were all well and turned away with his military friends, leaving poor Louizon quite amazed at how abruptly he had gone.

Cut off in the way I have described from the world at large, with no society but the military, Mr. Kinzie's family lived there very contentedly, with every comfort and most of the luxuries of life.

The Indians returned the friendship shown them, and grew attached to the family with a strength well out of the ordinary — as they proved during the events of 1812, eight years after Mr. Kinzie first came to live among them.
