Before we had any right to expect my husband back, I got a message one day inviting me to come up to the new house. We all went in a body, because we had purposely stayed away for a few days, expecting this summons and guessing what it meant.

Plante, in high glee, was sitting astride a small keg on the roof, right beside the kitchen chimney, on the very top of which he had planted a green bough. He held on to it with one hand while he waved the other in triumph and called out, "Eh ban, Madame John! à cette heure, pour le régal!"

"Yes, Plante, you've earned your treat, and I hope you won't enjoy it any less for having Pillon and Manaigre share it with you."

A suitable gratification made all three of them perfectly content with their "bourgeoise" — against whom Plante had sometimes been inclined to grumble, "because," as he put it, "she had him called up too early in the morning." He might have added: and because she couldn't follow the philosophy of his coming in to work in his own garden on the plea that it was too rainy to work in Monsieur John's.

It was with no ordinary satisfaction that we left the old log tenement and took possession of our new home, small and insignificant as it was.

I was only too happy to have the luxury of a real bedchamber, instead of the parlor floor, which had been serving me as one for more than two months. It's true that our cooking arrangements were still on no very improved plan. The clay chimney wasn't strong enough to carry the trammel and pot-hooks, which in those days hadn't yet been replaced by the cooking-stove and the kitchen range. We made our fire the old-fashioned way, with huge logs at the back and smaller sticks in front, laid across the andirons or dogs. Whichever cooking pots wouldn't fit on the hearth were set up on those sticks — but woe betide the dinner or the supper if a little carelessness let one treacherous stick burn through. Down the whole arrangement would come: kettles, saucepans, burning brands and cinders, all in one nearly inextricable mass. How often that happened while Harry or little Josette was in charge, and the mistress was in the parlor playing lady to some visitor, "'twere vain to tell."

Then, in spite of Monsieur Plante's palisades around the chimney, in a hard shower the rain would come pelting down, and since the hearth unfortunately sloped a little the wrong way, it would put the fire out — while the bark on the roof, failing in its duty, would now and then leave us so completely deluged that there was nothing for it but to snatch up the breakfast or the dinner and tuck it under the table until better times, meaning until the fair weather came back. In spite of all these little setbacks, though, we enjoyed our new quarters enormously.

Our garden was well stocked with vegetables, and even the currant bushes we had brought with us from Chicago, tied in a bundle on the back of the carriage, had given us some fruit.

The Indian women were very faithful in their visits and their presents. Sometimes it was venison, sometimes ducks or pigeons — whortleberries, wild plums, or cranberries, depending on the season — neat, pretty mats for the floor or the table, wooden bowls or ladles, fancy work in deerskin or porcupine quills. They would bring these in and throw them down at my feet. If I was inattentive and failed to look pleased, and to pick the things up off the floor and lay them carefully aside, a look of mortification and the remark "Our mother hates our gifts" showed how badly their feelings had been hurt. A present was always expected to be received graciously, and returned with something worth twice as much.

Meanwhile the weeks went by, one after another, and still "the master's" return was put off.

Since a schooner only rarely turned up at Green Bay to take passage in for Detroit, it was always a matter of guesswork how long a trip across the lakes and back would take — so it wasn't until the very end of August that he reached home again. He was greatly surprised to find us so nicely moved and settled, and under his active supervision the evils we had had to complain about were soon put right.

My husband had met my young brother Julian at Fort Gratiot and brought him along; my parents were sending him, at our request, to live with us. Edwin was overjoyed to have a companion again, having been very solitary up to then. The boys soon had plenty to occupy them, because in answer to a summons sent out to the different villages the Indians very shortly came flocking in for the payment.
