Early in February 1833, my husband and Lieutenant Hunter, with one or two others, set off on a trip to Chicago. The place had grown into so much of a town — it had maybe fifty inhabitants — that the owners of "Kinzie's Addition" needed to lay out lots and open streets through their property. All of that was done during this visit.

While they were out on the ground with a surveyor, my husband's attention was caught by a very bright-looking boy in Indian dress, who went hopping along beside the assistant carrying the chain, mimicking him as he called out "Stick!" and "Stuck!" in the course of the work. He asked who the boy was, and was surprised to learn that he was the brother of the old family servants Victoire, Genevieve, and Baptiste. Tomah — that was his name — had never worn civilized clothes; he was in a blanket and leggings, and had always lived in a wigwam. My husband asked whether he would like to come to Fort Winnebago with him and learn to be a white boy. The idea pleased him very much, and, his mother having agreed to the arrangement, he was packed into a wagon with the two gentlemen and their travelling gear when they set out on the return trip.

Tomah had been fitted out with a jacket and trousers and the other clothes his new sphere and character called for. They were near the Aux Plaines, coming up on the house of Glode (Claude) Laframboise, where Tomah knew he would run into people he knew. He asked leave to get down out of the wagon and walk a little way. The next time the gentlemen saw him, he was in full Pottowattamie costume: bitter winter weather or not, he had put his uncomfortable native clothes back on rather than let his old friends see him in a state of transformation.
