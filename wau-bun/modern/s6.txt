My head was pounding — the aftereffect of a day of being thrown around by the boat — but I did not want to give in to it, so once I had been shown everything the little settlement could show in the time we had, we went on to Mr. Mitchell's.

We were received by Mrs. Mitchell, an extremely pretty, delicate woman, part French and part Sioux, who had spent her early life at Prairie du Chien on the Mississippi. She had been a great belle among the young officers at Fort Crawford — such a belle that when the post surgeon killed himself, people laid it to an attachment he had formed for her and could not get returned. I was struck by her soft, gentle manners and by the musical rise and fall of her voice, which I soon learned is a marked characteristic of women in whom the French and the native blood are joined.

A lady who was then visiting the Mission was one of the party. She insisted I lie down on the sofa and did everything she could for my aching head. Sitting beside me, going on about the new world opening up in front of me, she asked:

"Doesn't it come home to you very strongly, the complete loss of religious privileges you're going to have to bear in that distant home of yours?"

"The loss will be great, I'm sure," I said, "but not complete. I'll have my Prayer-Book, and though we may have no church, we needn't be without a way of worshipping."

How many times afterward, cheered by the comfort of that precious book in the middle of the lonely wilderness, did I think back to that conversation and thank God that as long as I kept it, I could never be without "religious privileges."

We were still at the dinner table when the little steamer's bell rang to call us aboard, and we said a hurried goodbye to all those kind friends and carried away their heartfelt wishes for a safe, prosperous voyage.

There is hardly a finer sight than Mackinac from the water. As we steamed out from shore the whole view opened at once — the sloping beach with its scattered wigwams and the canoes drawn up here and there, the irregular, quaint houses, the white walls of the fort, and beyond it a still higher rise crowned by what is left of old Fort Holmes. Put together, the picture shows the perfect outline that gave the island its original Indian name: Mich-i-li-mack-i-nac, the Big Turtle.

And that water — pure and alive, with fish sliding and darting through it in plain view, and so clear that you can make out something dropped to the bottom fifty or sixty feet down, a dollar lying far below on its green bed and looking no bigger than a half dime. I could hardly blame the enthusiastic lady who cried, "Oh, I could wish to be drowned in these pure, beautiful waters!"

As we came past the far western point of the island, my husband pointed out a promontory away to the northwest and told me it was Point St. Ignace. It has great historical interest as one of the earliest white settlements on this continent. The Jesuit missionaries put a church and a school there as early as 1607 — the same year a white settlement was made at St. Augustine in Florida, and a year before Jamestown, Virginia, was founded.

Of everything those devoted men built, all that is left is the memory of their work, and in most cases the only thing keeping the memory is the name of the place where they spent their love on their savage brethren. The little French church at Sandwich, across from Detroit, stands alone now as a witness to the zeal and self-sacrifice of those pioneers of Christianity.

Passing "Old Mackinac" on the mainland, which makes the southern edge of the straits, we came out shortly into the broad water of Lake Michigan. Every traveler and every reader of our history knows how the old fort there was taken by the Indians in Pontiac's day: how they got up a game of ball outside the walls, apparently in a friendly spirit, and the officers and soldiers came out to watch; how the ball was cleverly tossed over the wall; how the players rushed in after it as if to find it, and had the place, and butchered the garrison.

The little Indian village of L'Arbre Croche glimmered far to the south in the light of the setting sun. Apart from that there was no sign of a living soul along that whole vast wooded shore. The giant forest trees, and here and there a little glade of prairie opening down to the water, made a landscape that would have gladdened any farmer's eye with its promise of good ground — but plainly no man's foot had ever crossed it, and we left it to its solitude and steered west across the water.
