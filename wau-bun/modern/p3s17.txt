We now had to part with our friends Major Twiggs and his family, which we did with heartfelt regret. He gave me a few parting words about our old acquaintance Krissman.

"When I went into the barracks the other day," he said, "about the time the men were taking their dinner, I noticed a great six-foot soldier standing against the window-frame, crying and blubbering. 'Hello,' I said, 'what on earth does this mean?'

"'Why, that fellow there,' said Krissman — for it was him — 'has scrowged me out of my place!' A pretty soldier your protégé will make, madam!" the Major added.

I never heard anything more of my hero. Whether he went off to display his prowess against the Seminoles and the Mexicans, or whether he went back to till the fertile soil of his native German Flats and blow his favorite boatman's horn, some future historian will have to say.

There is one more character to be disposed of — Louisa. An opportunity came up in the spring, and the Major put her in the charge of somebody going to Buffalo, so that she could be sent back to her parents. As a compliment to the new acquaintances she had made, she shortened her skirts, put on a pair of scarlet leggings embroidered with porcupine quills, and took her leave of military life — having handed the gentleman who had charge of her sixty dollars for safe keeping, which she said "she had saved up out of her wages at a dollar a week through the winter."
