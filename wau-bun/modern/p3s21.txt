When the Indians arrived and when they left, my sense of "woman's rights" was often badly outraged. The head of the family, as a rule, came along at his leisure carrying his gun and perhaps a lance; the woman came with the mats and poles of her lodge on her shoulders, her pappoose if she had one, her kettles, her sacks of corn and wild rice, and not infrequently the household dog perched on top of the whole load. If there is a horse or a pony among the family possessions, the man rides and the squaw trudges along behind.

This unequal division of labor doesn't come from any lack of kind, affectionate feeling in the husband. It is more the instinct of his sex to assert its superior position and importance whenever a proper occasion comes up. Out of sight of anyone else, and in no danger of compromising his own dignity, the husband is willing enough to relieve his wife of the burden custom lays on her, and to take a share of her work and her hardships.

The payment hadn't gone by without its due number of complimentary and medicine dances. The medicine dances happen only at rare intervals; the complimentary ones happen whenever an occasion turns up that calls for a show of respect and courtesy.

The custom is to ask the person to be complimented for permission to dance for him. Once that is granted, they prepare by painting their faces elaborately and marking the body — usually bare about the chest and shoulders — in the most approved pattern. Every ornament that can be mustered is added to the hair or the headdress. Happy is the man who, having taken one or more scalps, is entitled to proclaim it with the corresponding number of eagle's feathers.

The less fortunate substitute the feathers of a wild turkey, or, better still, of the first unlucky "rooster" to fall in their way. My poor fowls, during the time of the payment, were always thoroughly plucked.

When their preparations are finished, the dancers gather at some convenient spot and come marching from there to the appointed place, with the music of the Indian drum and the shee-shee-qua, or rattle. They arrange themselves in a circle and dance with violent contortions and gestures, some of them graceful, others merely energetic. The squaws stand a little way off and add their discordant voices to the music of the instruments; they rarely take part in the dance itself. Sometimes, though, when the general gaiety has excited them, a few of them will form a circle outside and perform a kind of ungainly up-and-down movement whose only merit is the perfect time they keep — and the Indians without exception seem to have a natural ear for it. When the dance is over, which is only when the dancers' strength is completely used up, a quantity of presents is brought out and put in the middle of the circle by order of the person complimented. One of their own number makes a fair distribution of them, and then, the whole object of the display having been accomplished, they go away.

The medicine dance is put on mainly to celebrate the "Medicine-man's" skill in curing disease. This functionary belongs to a fraternity that is thought to have, on top of its other powers, some skill in interpreting the will of the Great Spirit about the conduct of his people. He sometimes makes offerings and sacrifices that are regarded as propitiatory. In that sense the word "priest" may be thought to apply to him. He is also a "prophet" in so far as he is, in a limited way, a teacher — but he doesn't claim any gift of foretelling future events.

A man is chosen to join the fraternity of the Medicine-man by those already initiated, mainly because they have noticed some skill or shrewdness in him. Sometimes it happens that a man who has had a severe illness which gave way to the prescriptions of one of the members is considered a proper object of choice, on the strength of a sort of claim established in that way.

When he is about to be initiated, a great feast is made — at the candidate's expense, of course, because the same principle of politics holds good in simple life as in civilized: honors have to be paid for. An animal is killed and dressed, and the people at large share in it; there are dances and songs and speeches in abundance. Then the chief Medicine-man takes the candidate aside and privately instructs him in all the ceremonies and all the knowledge he needs to make him an accomplished member of the fraternity. Sometimes the new member chosen is still a child. In that case the Medicine-man takes him as soon as he reaches a proper age, and qualifies him by instruction and example to be a creditable member of the fraternity.

Among the Winnebagoes there seems to be a considerable belief in magic. Every Medicine-man has a bag or sack that is thought to have some animal shut up inside it, which he addresses in the course of their pow-wows, calling to it in the note that belongs to the species he supposes it to be. And the people seem convinced that the answers announced really are communications from the Great Spirit, delivered in this form.

The Indians appear to have no idea of retribution beyond this life. They have a strong appreciation of the great fundamental virtues of natural religion — the worship of the Great Spirit, brotherly love, parental affection, honesty, temperance, and chastity. Any breach of the Great Spirit's laws, by a departure from these virtues, they believe will rouse his anger and bring down punishment. Those are their principles. That their practice shows more and more of a departure from them, under the debasing influence of living close to the whites, is a melancholy truth, and nobody will admit it with so much sorrow as the people who lived among them, and thought well of them, before this marked change came about.

One of the first improvements that suggested itself about our new home was getting rid of some very unsightly pickets around two or three Indian graves on the esplanade in front of the house. These burial places are held in such reverence, though, that we felt we had to approach the subject with great delicacy and consideration.

My husband eventually ventured to propose to Mrs. "Pawnee Blanc," who was the nearest surviving relative of the person buried there, that the pickets should be replaced with a neat wooden platform.

The idea pleased her very much, because through her intimacy with Paquette's family she had picked up something of a taste for civilization. So a little platform about a foot high, properly finished with a moulding round the edge, was put in place of the worn, blackened pickets — and it was touching to see the mournful satisfaction with which two or three old crones would come regularly every evening at sunset to sit and gossip over the ashes of their departed relatives.

On the fine moonlit nights, too, you could often see a group sitting there, enjoying what is to them a solemn hour, since they hold the poetic belief that "the moon was made to give light to the dead."

The reverence the Indians have for the memory of their departed friends, and their dutiful care in visiting their last resting places and making offerings there to the Great Spirit, is an example their more enlightened brethren would do well to copy. Not so, though, with some of their other customs in relation to the dead.
