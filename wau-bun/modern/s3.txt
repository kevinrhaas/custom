Mr. and Mrs. Robert Stuart took us in with the warmest possible welcome. We had been expected at their house for days.

You can only really appreciate a refuge like that after you have been thrown around and soaked the way we had been. A bright, warm fire, and faces lit up with genuine interest in us, wiped out every trace of exhaustion and irritation.

We talked a while, and then the servants were called in, a chapter of God's word was read aloud, the hymn was sung, the prayer of praise and thanksgiving was offered, and we were shown where to sleep.

I am not going to try to draw a portrait here of those two people I was meeting for the first time. Robert Stuart's biography deserves a better pen than mine. Anyone who had the good fortune to know him — and still more, anyone who ever stayed under his roof — carried the impression of him to the end of their life: the bearing, the easy humor, the benevolence that never ran out, the upright and uncompromising way he held to a principle, the passionate philanthropy, the complete absence of self-interest. Irving in Astoria and Franchere in his Narrative both give striking pieces of his early character, along with episodes out of his history that are thrilling and romantic. But both of them left out the most valuable part — his later life, as a Christian gentleman.

As for his beloved wife, who outlived him and is still walking her bereaved and solitary road, comforted by the memory of her long, useful years as a "Mother in Israel," we will say no more than this: our love goes to her, and our prayers, that her Father in heaven will send her His best blessings.
