These kindly gestures were cut short when another party of the enemy came in, bringing with them the mother of the little prisoners and her youngest child, a baby three months old.

As it happened, the father of the family had gone off early that day with his hired men to a raising a few miles away, so the house had been left with nobody to defend it. The settlers had gone a long stretch of years in peace, with no trouble or alarm from the savages at all, and it had put them completely off their guard. They had lately dropped some of the precautions they had once thought necessary.

These Indians had lain in wait and picked their moment to seize the defenceless family and take them prisoner. From their paint, and the other marks by which the early settlers learned to tell one tribe from another, Mrs. Lytle guessed that the people she and her children had fallen among were Senecas. She was right. It was a party of that tribe who had come down from their village to fall upon some isolated band of their enemies the Delawares; failing at that, they had made it up to themselves by capturing a few white settlers.

That the prisoners in this case escaped the fate of most Americans unlucky enough to fall into the hands of the Iroquois is owing to the generally mild temper of this tribe, and to the generous character of the chief who was with the party.

The children learned from their mother that she had no idea at all what had become of their other brother and sister, a boy of six and a little girl of four. She hoped they had got safely away with the servant-girl, who had also disappeared from the very start.

After stopping a few hours to let the prisoners' worn-out bodies recover, the savages set off marching again, and one of the older Indians offered to relieve the mother of the burden of her baby, which she had been carrying in her arms all this while. Pleased at the unexpected kindness, she handed over her tender charge.

On they went, and the savage carrying the infant hung back a little behind the rest of the party, until he found a spot that suited his purpose. Then he took hold of his innocent victim by the feet and, swinging it once around to add force to the blow, dashed its brains out against a tree. He left the body where it lay and rejoined the others.

The mother, with no idea what had happened, looked hard at him as he came back without the child, then stared wildly around at the rest of the group. Her beloved little one was not there. Its absence told her what had been done to it. But she choked back the scream of agony, because she knew the lives of the ones still with her depended on her holding steady in that terrible hour. She drew them closer to her and went on down that miserable road without a word spoken or a question asked.

Out of the depths of her heart she cried to Him who is able to save, and He comforted her with the hope that the ones left to her would be delivered. She could see that if blood had been the only object, her scalp and her children's would have been taken on the spot where they were captured.

And she read in the eyes of the man who was plainly in command of the party a look more merciful than she had dared hope for. She had particularly noticed how soothing his manner was, and how openly he favored her eldest child, the little girl we have been speaking of, and on those slender foundations she built any number of bright hopes of escape or ransom.
