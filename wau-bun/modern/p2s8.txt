The Indians grew more unruly by the day. They came into the fort in defiance of the sentries and walked into the officers' quarters without ceremony. On one occasion an Indian picked up a rifle and fired it in the commanding officer's parlor, as a gesture of defiance. Some people thought it was meant as a signal to the young men to attack. The old chiefs went back and forth among the assembled groups looking violently agitated, while the squaws rushed about in great excitement, plainly braced for something terrible.

Nothing more came of the ill feeling for the moment, and Captain Heald — strange as it seems — went on believing that he had put the Indians in a friendly enough frame of mind to guarantee the safety of his command on the march to Fort Wayne.

So the time went by until the 12th of August. What the people inside the fort were feeling over those days is easier to imagine than to describe. Every morning that dawned seemed to bring them nearer to the most appalling end there is — butchery by a savage enemy — and at night they hardly dared let themselves sleep, in case they woke to the war-whoop and the tomahawk. Gloom and suspicion were everywhere, and because the officers were not of one mind, they were denied the comfort they might have found in sympathizing with and encouraging each other.

The Indians had been assembled from the villages nearby, and a council was held with them on the afternoon of the 12th. Captain Heald went alone on the military's behalf. He had asked his officers to come with him and they had refused. They had been told privately that the young chiefs meant to fall on the officers and massacre them during the council, but they could not convince Captain Heald that the information was true. So they simply waited until he had left the garrison with Mr. Kinzie, then took command of the block-houses overlooking the esplanade where the council was held, opened the port-holes, and trained the cannon so that they covered the whole assembly. That is probably what kept the white men at the council alive.

At the council the commanding officer told the Indians that the next day he meant to hand over to them not only the goods stored in the United States factory but the ammunition and provisions as well, of which the garrison had plenty. He then asked the Pottowattamies for an escort to Fort Wayne, promising them a liberal reward on arriving there on top of the presents they were about to receive. With a great many professions of friendship and good will, the savages agreed to everything he proposed and promised everything he asked.
