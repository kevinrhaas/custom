Very shortly after my husband left, we had a visit from "the White Crow," the "Little Priest," and several other principal chiefs of the Rock River Indians. They seemed very much put out to learn that their Father was away from home, even though his errand was to fetch "the silver." We sent for Paquette, who interpreted for us what they had come about.

They had come to tell us that the Sauk chief Black Hawk and his band, who under an earlier treaty had moved some time before to the west of the Mississippi, had now come back to their old homes and hunting-grounds, and had declared that they would not give them up, but would drive off the white settlers who had begun to move onto them.

In fact the chief had already done the driving off, and since he was said to have got some of the Pottowattamies to join him, there was reason to fear that he might talk some of the Winnebagoes into following their example.

These chiefs had come to take counsel with their Father, and to assure him that they would do everything in their power to keep their young men quiet. They had heard that troops were being raised down among the whites in Illinois, and they hoped their own people would have the sense to stay out of trouble. They also begged that their Father, when he got back, would see to it that the soldiers did not meddle with them as long as they kept quiet and behaved in a friendly way.

White Crow seemed particularly anxious to make me understand that if any danger came up while Shaw-nee-aw-kee was away, he would come with his people to protect me and my family. I trusted his word, because he had always shown himself an upright and honorable Indian.

All the same, the thought of Indian trouble so close to us, with our guardian and protector gone, gave us a good many anxious moments, and it wasn't until we heard that the Sauks and Foxes had retreated peaceably west of the Mississippi that we were able to lay our fears aside altogether.
