The Mariner did come at last. It was early in the morning. Within an hour of her arrival the frightening news she carried had run the whole length of the settlement: the cholera was in this country. It was in Detroit. It was among the troops on their way to the seat of the war. Whole companies had died of it in the river St. Clair, and the ones still alive had been put ashore at Port Gratiot to save themselves however they could. We were shut in between the savage enemy on one side and the pestilence on the other.

For anyone with friends back East, the news was as bad as it could be. It seemed to break every person who heard it. The first man to bring it to us was an officer who had shown the most conspicuous courage on the battlefield, and in some private undertakings that called for nerve and daring of a rare kind. When he had finished telling us, he laid his head against the window-sill and cried like a child.

Those who simply had to get back to friends near and dear to them left the Bay on the Mariner. Everyone else decided their present home was the safest place to be, and so it turned out, because the dreadful scourge did not come to Green Bay that season.

The heat was intense, and the mosquitoes were so thick that we made no attempt at walking on the parade after sunset unless we were armed with two fans, or with green branches to keep waving without stopping, to drive them off. Which was, by the way, the surest way of drawing them to us. We had somehow forgotten the flat indifference we had so often marveled at in Old Smoker, who would sit there calmly and let his bare arms and body go literally gray with the tormenting insects. Then he would quietly wipe off a handful, the blood following the movement of his hand across his skin, and wait stoically for the chance to do it again. They say that a mosquito left alone until he has had his fill leaves a far less inflamed bite than one brushed away in the middle of his meal.

In the daytime, at that season, the air is full of what people call the Green Bay fly, a kind of dragon-fly. Sometimes the outside walls of the houses are so thickly covered with them that you can hardly tell what color the house is. They live a very brief life, barely more than a day. You see their dead bodies stuck to the walls and windows indoors, and outdoors they fall in such numbers that once a high wind has swept them into rows along the sides of the quarters, you can walk through them and kick them up with your feet like dry leaves in the fall.
