The third day of our journey dawned brilliantly clear, like the two before it, and we set our course more to the north than we had been going, in the direction of Big-foot Lake, now known by the somewhat overused name of Lake Geneva.

That day's ride was free of mishaps and disasters of any kind. The air was mild, the leaves of the forest fresh and fragrant, the little brooks clear and sparkling — everything in nature was speaking the praises of its generous Creator.

It is in scenes like that one, far removed from the bustle, the strife and the sin of civilized life, that we most fully feel the presence of the great Author of the Universe. It is there that the mind can most completely adore his majesty and his goodness, because it is only there that the command is obeyed, "Let all the earth keep silence before Him!"

Anyone can see that the deepest and most solemn devotion lives in the hearts of people who, shut out from the worship of God in temples built by human hands, are brought instead to commune with him in the middle of the boundless magnificence his own power has made.

The day was not entirely without incident, though. We stopped for our noontide food and drink, dismounted, and threw ourselves down on the fresh grass right at the edge of a pleasant thicket — and were startled by a tender bleating close by. In a moment, breaking its way through the low branches, a sweet little dappled fawn came out upon us, plainly looking for its mother. It did not seem in the least frightened at the sight of us. Poor Selkirk might have been parodied here: it was so unacquainted with man, its tameness was charming to us.

But the children's loud delight soon sent it bounding back into the woods, and any hope of catching it for a pet ended right there.

We had travelled well that day and were beginning to feel rather tired when, just before sunset, we came up on a ridge overlooking one of the loveliest little dells imaginable. It was an oak opening, and browsing in the shade of the tall trees scattered around it were the cattle and horses of the soldiers, who had got that far on their march. Two or three white tents were pitched at the bottom of the valley beside a clear stream. The camp-fires were already lit, and the men, alone or in groups, were busy with all their various arrangements for their own comfort or their animals'.

Lieutenant Foster came out delighted to welcome us, and accepted without a moment's hesitation an invitation to join our mess again for as long as we were together.

We soon found a pleasant place to camp, far enough away from the other party to keep us out of each other's way, and our supper was improved by a kettle of fine fresh milk, kindly brought over by Mrs. Gardiner, the hospital matron, who with her little covered cart was no small feature of the military group. We ate our evening meal with a great deal of laughter and enjoyment.

If people are ever good company, it is when they are thrown together in circumstances like these. There has always been enough happening during the day to supply things to talk about and things to laugh about for as long as the company feels like talking — which, to tell the truth, is not an unconscionably long time after supper is over.

The poor Lieutenant looked grave enough when we set off ahead of him the next morning. Nobody in his party knew the road. But after giving him directions both general and particular, Mr. Kinzie promised to blaze a tree or set up a chip as a guide at every place that looked more than usually doubtful.
