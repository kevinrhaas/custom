With the payment over and the Indians scattered, we got ready to settle down quietly in our little home. But now a new source of trouble came up.

My husband's accounts of what he had disbursed as Agent of the Winnebagoes, which he had sent on to the Department at Washington, had never reached there, and he was duly notified of the fact — that is, notified in the way news could reach us at all, by the roundabout and unreliable means of conveyance that kept up our communication with the Eastern world at that time. If the vouchers for the earlier expenditures could not be produced, along with those for the recent payment of $15,000 in annuity money, it might put him in a very awkward position. So he decided to go to Washington at once and carry his duplicate accounts himself.

"How would you like to go and see your father and mother," he said to me one morning, "and show them how the West agrees with you?"

After a year apart it was the most welcome suggestion imaginable, and within a few days everything was ready for us to leave.

We had a visitor staying with us just then, Miss Brush of Detroit, who had come from Green Bay with Mr. and Mrs. Whitney and Miss Frances Henshaw on an excursion to the Mississippi. Our little india-rubber house had somehow managed to stretch itself far enough to hold the whole party through the very pleasant visit they made us.

The arrival of two young ladies had been, as you can imagine, a perfect godsend to the unmarried lieutenants, and when Miss Brush — tired of the trip, or frightened off by the snow, which fell eight inches deep on the fourth of October — decided to give up the rest of her excursion and accept our pressing invitation to stay with us until her friends came back, we were looked on as public benefactors. She was now to go with us to Green Bay, and possibly on to Detroit.

Our voyage down the river passed without incident, and we reached Green Bay just as the whole place was stirring in expectation of one of Mr. Newbery's schooners. That was an important event, and it interested the entire community, from Fort Howard to "Dickenson's." For some it would bring friends, for some supplies; for the ladies it brought the fashions and for the gentlemen the news, since it was the happy carrier of the mails — not for that place only, but for the whole "upper country."

In a few days the vessel came in. She had a mail aboard for Fort Winnebago; it was only in the winter that letters were carried overland to that place, by way of Niles's Settlement and Chicago.

As Postmaster, my husband opened the mail-bag and took out his own letters. One of them told him that the missing accounts had turned up at the Department after all, to everyone's satisfaction — but, oh, the bitter disappointment: another brought word that my parents had gone to Kentucky for the winter. Not to any city, or to anywhere you could easily get to, but "up the Sandy," and off among the mountains of Virginia, hunting up old land-claims belonging to my grandfather's estate.

There was no hope of following them. We could hardly expect to find them in the short time we could be away from home — not even with the help of the clear directions an old settler had once given my father while he was exploring that wild country.

"You must go up Tug," the man told him, "and down Troublesome, and fall over on to Kingdom-come." We did not think it advisable to attempt an expedition like that, and so we made up our minds to turn around and go back to Fort Winnebago.
