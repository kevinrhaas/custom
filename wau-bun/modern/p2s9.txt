After the council, Mr. Kinzie, who understood not only the Indian character but the exact temper of feeling among them just then, had a long talk with Captain Heald, hoping to open his eyes to how things actually stood.

He reminded him that ever since the troubles with the Indians on the Wabash and around it, there had been a settled plan of hostility toward the whites, and that because of it American policy had been to keep out of their hands anything that would help them make war on the defenceless people of the frontier.

Mr. Kinzie also reminded Captain Heald what he himself had done the previous autumn. He had set out from home for Detroit, and when he had got as far as De Charme's the news of the battle of Tippecanoe reached him. He turned straight back to Chicago so that he could send orders to his traders to supply the Indians with no ammunition at all. As a result, everything the traders had on hand was hidden away, and those of them who had not already left for their wintering-grounds took neither powder nor shot with them.

Captain Heald was struck by how bad a policy it would be to arm the enemy — for that is what they now had to consider their old neighbors — against himself, and he decided to destroy all the ammunition except what his own troops would need.

On the 13th the goods — blankets, broadcloths, calicoes, paints and the rest — were handed out as agreed. That same evening the ammunition and the liquor were carried off: part of it into the sally-port and dumped down a well that had been dug there to supply the garrison with water in an emergency, and the rest taken out as quietly as possible through the north gate, where the barrel-heads were knocked in and the contents poured into the river.

A large quantity of alcohol belonging to Mr. Kinzie went the same way. It had been stored in a warehouse near his house, across from the fort.

The Indians suspected what was going on and crept up, snake-like, as close to the work as they could get; but a sharp watch was kept and nobody was allowed near except the men doing the job. All the muskets not needed by the command on the march were broken up and thrown down the well, along with the bags of shot, the flints, the gunscrews — in short, everything to do with weapons of offense.
