Christmas and New Year's brought our Indian friends back to us. They had picked up something about how these holidays are kept from their French neighbors, and I had been warned in advance that I would see the squaws kissing every white man they came across. I did not quite believe that, but I could easily believe that each of them would expect a present as a compliment of the season, so I laid in a proper supply of beads, ribbons, combs, and other trinkets. Knowing how fond they are of anything sweet, I also had a quantity of crullers and doughnuts made up the day before, as a treat for them.

To my great surprise and annoyance, only a moderate share of the cakes — the frying of which had been left to Louisa — came upstairs to be put away in the "Davis."

"Where are the rest of the cakes, Louisa?"

"That great fellow Hancock came in with the fatigue-party to fill the water-barrels, and while I had just stepped into the store-room to get more flour, he carried off everything I had cooked."

And Louisa pulled a face and whined about it, as though she had not herself been treating every soldier who ever set foot on the place.

Early the next morning I had quite a levee of Ho-tshung-rah matrons. They seated themselves in a circle on the floor, and I was sorry to see that applying a little soap and water to their blankets had formed no part of their holiday preparations. There was nobody on hand to interpret, so I thought I would open the conversation in a way they would understand. I brought out of the sideboard a china dish filled with the nice brown crullers, over which I had grated, as one does, a good quantity of white sugar. I handed it to the first woman in the circle. She took the dish out of my hand, poured every cake into the corner of her blanket without the least hurry, and handed the dish back to me empty. She must be a most voracious person, I thought, but I will manage better next time. I filled the dish again and went to the next one, this time keeping a firm grip on it as I offered her the contents, of which I supposed she would modestly take one. Not at all. She scooped the whole lot out with both hands and stowed them away in her blanket exactly as the first had done. My sense of politeness rebelled at handing them out one at a time, the way you do with children, so I sat down to work out what was to be done — clearly the supply would not hold out long against demand on that scale, and there would be more visitors along shortly.

While I was puzzling over it, the ones who had got the cakes began handing them round, and the whole number was fairly divided among the company. But I noticed that they did not eat them. They ran their fingers over the grated sugar, looked into each other's faces, and muttered under their breath — there was plainly something here they did not understand. Then one of them, bolder than the rest, wet her fingers, picked up a few grains of the sugar, and put it cautiously to her mouth.

"Tah-nee-zhoo-rah!" (Sugar!) she exclaimed, delighted, and they all burst out laughing. I need hardly say that the cakes disappeared as fast as they judged good manners would allow. They had never seen any sugar but the brown or yellow maple kind, so they had taken the white substance for salt, and that was why they had hesitated to taste it.

They stayed on until Shaw-nee-aw-kee made his appearance, and then, made happy by their various gifts, they all took their leave.
