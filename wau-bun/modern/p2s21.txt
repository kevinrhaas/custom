Along with Mr. Kinzie's party was a non-commissioned officer who had escaped in an unusual way. As the troops were about to leave the fort, it was discovered that the surgeon's baggage-horses had wandered off. The quartermaster-sergeant, Griffith, was sent to round them up and bring them along. Getting them back was absolutely necessary, since their packs held part of the surgeon's apparatus and the medicines for the march.

This man had been on the sick report for a long time, which is why he had been given charge of the baggage instead of being put with the troops. His efforts to recover the horses came to nothing, and he was hurrying to rejoin his party, alarmed by signs of disorder and hostility among the Indians, when To-pee-nee-bee met him and took him prisoner.

The chief took his arms and accoutrements away from him, put him into a canoe, paddled him across the river, and told him to head for the woods and hide himself. He did. The next day, in the afternoon, seeing from his hiding place that everything looked quiet, he risked stealing carefully into Ouilmette's garden, where he hid for a while behind some currant bushes.

Finally he decided to go into the house, and climbed in through a small back window into the room where the family were. This was just as the Wabash Indians were leaving Ouilmette's house for Mr. Kinzie's. The sergeant was now in immediate danger. The family stripped him of his uniform and dressed him in a suit of deerskin, with belt, moccasins, and pipe, like a French engagé. His dark complexion and big black whiskers helped the disguise along. Everyone in the family was ordered to speak to him in French, and although he did not know a word of the language, he managed to pass for a Weem-tee-gosh, and in that character to travel with Mr. Kinzie and his family, undetected by his enemies, until they reached a place of safety.
