There was one among them that year whom I had never seen before — the mother of the elder Day-kau-ray. Nobody could tell her age, but everyone agreed she must have seen more than a hundred winters. Her eyes were dim and almost white with age; her face was dark and withered, like a baked apple; her voice was tremulous and feeble except when she raised it in fury to scold her graceless grandsons, who loved playing every kind of mischievous trick on her. Everything about her showed the very great age she must have reached.

She usually went about on all fours, not having the strength to hold herself upright. On the day of the payment, once she had her portion, which she hid carefully in a corner of her blanket, she came crawling along and settled herself on the doorstep to count her treasure.

My sister and I were watching her from the open window.

Soon, just as she had spread out her silver in front of her — unobserved, as she thought — two of her descendants came on her suddenly. At first they seemed to be begging for a share, but she pushed them off with angry gestures, and then one of them made a sudden swoop and got away with a handful.

She tried to get up and go after him, but she could do no more than clutch what was left and let out the most unearthly screams of rage. Just then the boys looked up and saw us watching them. They burst out laughing, threw her the half-dollars with a sort of mocking gesture, and ran back to the pay-ground.

For all their vexatious tricks, she seemed very fond of them, and she never failed to beg something from her Father that she could give them.

She crept into the parlor one morning, then straightened herself up, held on to the frame of the door, and cried out in the most piteous tone, "Shaw-nee-aw-kee! Wau-tshob-ee-rah Thsoonsh-koo-nee-noh!" (Silver-man, I have no looking-glass.) My husband, smiling and picking up the same little tone, called back, "Do you want to look at yourself, mother?"

The idea struck her as so irresistibly comic that she laughed until she was fairly obliged to sit down on the floor and give herself up to it. She then admitted that the little mirror was for one of the boys. Once her Father had given her that, she found that she had "no comb," and then that she had "no knife," and then that she had "no calico shawl," until it ended, as it generally did, with Shaw-nee-aw-kee paying pretty dearly for his joke.
