The morning of the 15th came. Everything was ready, and nine o'clock was the hour named for starting.

Mr. Kinzie had volunteered to go with the troops on their march, and had put his family in the care of some friendly Indians, who promised to take them by boat around the head of Lake Michigan to a point on the St. Joseph's River, where the troops would join them if they were allowed to get that far.

Early that morning Mr. Kinzie had a message from To-pee-nee-bee, a chief of the St. Joseph's band, telling him that the Pottowattamies who had agreed to escort the detachment meant mischief, and urging him to give up the idea of going with the troops overland. He promised that the boat carrying Mr. Kinzie and his family would be allowed through to St. Joseph's safely.

Mr. Kinzie refused the offer. He believed his being there might act as a restraint on the fury of the savages, since so many of them were warmly attached to him and his family.

The party in the boat was Mrs. Kinzie and her four younger children, their nurse Josette, one of Mr. Kinzie's clerks, two servants and the boatmen, along with the two Indians acting as their protectors. The boat set off, but it had barely reached the mouth of the river — which, remember, was half a mile below the fort — when another messenger from To-pee-nee-bee came to hold them where they were. There was no mistaking the reason for it.

The wife and mother sat there hardly breathing. She was a woman of unusual energy and force of character, but her heart failed her as she folded her arms around her helpless babies and watched her husband and her eldest child march off to certain destruction.
