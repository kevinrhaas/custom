Now we have to go back to the place and the moment where our story began.

Late that evening the father came home. Everything inside the house and around it was silent and desolate. There was no trace of a living creature anywhere on the place or the grounds. His nearest neighbors lived a long way off, but he rushed to them anyway, frantically demanding news of his family.

He roused them out of their beds, and one after another they joined the search. At last, at the house of one of them, they found the servant-maid, who had managed to escape. Her first hiding place, she said, had been a large brewing-tub in an outer kitchen. She had crawled under it at the first alarm and stayed there until the Indians left — they were clearly in a hurry — which gave her a chance to run somewhere safe. She could tell them nothing about her mistress and the children except that they had not been killed where she could see or hear it.

Finally, having combed the neighborhood without any luck, Mr. Lytle remembered an old settler who lived alone far up the valley. He and his friends went straight there, and the old man told them that he had been at work in his field just before sunset and had seen a party of strange Indians go by not far from his cabin. As they wound along the brow of the hill he could make out that they had prisoners with them — a woman and a child. He knew the woman was white, because she carried her baby in her arms instead of on her back, the way the savages do.

Day was breaking by now, since the whole night had gone in fruitless searching. The agonized father talked it over with his kind friends and neighbors and accepted their offer to go with him to Fort Pitt and ask the commandant and the Indian Agent there for advice and help.

Going back down the valley, as they came near a hut that they had found apparently deserted the night before, they were startled to see two children standing on the high bank in front of it. The delighted father recognized two of his missing flock — but they could tell him nothing about their mother or the others who were lost. Their story was simple and touching.

They had been playing in the garden when they were frightened by the sight of the Indians coming into the yard near the house. Without being seen, the brother, who was only six years old, helped his little sister over the fence into a field grown over with blackberry and wild raspberry bushes. They hid among them for a while, and then, when everything seemed quiet, they tried to push their way through to the side of the field farthest from the house. Unluckily, the little girl had pulled off her shoes and stockings while playing in the garden, and the briers tore and cut her tender feet so badly that she could hardly keep from crying out. Her brother took off his own stockings and put them on her feet. He tried to protect them with his shoes too, but they were too big and kept slipping off, so she could not wear them. For a while they kept at it, believing they were escaping certain death — because, as I have said, the tales these children had grown up hearing had taught them to think of every strange Indian as an agent of torture, and of horrors worse than dying. Worn out with pain and exhaustion, the poor little girl finally said she could go no farther.

"Then, Maggie," her brother said, "I'll have to kill you, because I can't let the Indians kill you."

"Oh, no, Thomas!" she begged. "Don't — please don't kill me! I don't think the Indians will find us."

"Oh, yes they will, Maggie. And I could kill you so much easier than they would."

He kept trying to talk her into it for a long time, and even looked around for a stick big enough to do it with. But despair gave the little creature strength, and she promised her brother that she would not complain and would not falter, if he would only help her get out of the field.

That a little boy could think of saving his sister from savage cruelty by taking her life himself shows you the kind of horror stories the children of the early settlers grew up on.

After a few more efforts they got out of the field and into an open pasture, where to their great delight they saw some cows feeding. They recognized them as belonging to Granny Myers, an old woman who lived some little distance off — though in what direction from where they now were, they had absolutely no idea.

With a shrewdness well beyond his years, the boy said, "Let's hide until sunset. The cows will go home then, and we'll follow them."

They did, but when they reached Granny Myers's they were dismayed to find the house empty. The old woman had been called away on some business down the valley and did not come back that night.

Tired and hungry, they could go no farther. After an almost fruitless attempt to get some milk out of the cows, they lay down to sleep under an old bedstead that stood behind the house. Their father and his party gave them one more fright in the night: the shouts and calls meant to wake up whoever was in the house sounded to them like the whoop of the Indians, and since they could not tell friends from enemies, they crept close together and got as far out of sight as they could. When they were found the next morning, they were debating which way to go next to be safe.
