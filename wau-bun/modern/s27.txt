The Winnebago Indians were in two divisions. One was paid by the Agent at the Portage, the other at Prairie du Chien by General Street. The first, numbering between four and five thousand, received under the terms of the treaty fifteen thousand dollars a year, along with a considerable quantity of presents and a certain number of rations of bread and pork, to be issued in emergencies throughout the year.

The principal villages of this division of the tribe were at Lake Winnebago, Green and Fox Lakes, the Barribault, Mud Lake, the Four Lakes, Kosh-ko-nong, and Turtle Creek. When the annuity money arrived, or a little before, messengers were sent out to all these villages to tell the heads of families or lodges to gather at "the Portage."

Once there, the heads of families, under their several chiefs, give in their names and the number of people in their lodges to be registered. Since a set sum of money is allotted for each individual, it is naturally in the interest of the head of a lodge to make the registered number as large as he can. Each man brings his little bundle of sticks and hands it to the Agent to register. Sometimes the conversation goes like this:

"How many have you in your lodge?"

The Indian counts his bundle of sticks carefully and with great ceremony. "Fifteen."

"How many men?"

"Two." The Agent lays two sticks aside. "How many women?"

"Three." Three more sticks are separated out.

"How many children?"

"Eight." Eight sticks are added to the pile.

"Then what do these two sticks that are left over mean?"

The culprit, whose arithmetic has not been equal to carrying off his deception, disappears amid the shouts and jeers of his companions, who are always delighted to see roguery found out when they have had no share in it.

The young officers usually helped count out and hand over the money at these payments, and it was nothing unusual, as the last band came up, for the chiefs to take a quantity of silver out of the box and ask their Father to pay his friends for their trouble — they seemed genuinely upset when he refused. Here, as in almost every case, you see the native courtesy and politeness they never lose sight of. If a party comes to their Father to beg for provisions and food is set before them, then however hungry they are, each man waits patiently until one of the company has divided the whole into equal shares; then he takes his own portion and eats it quietly and with the greatest moderation. I never saw this rule broken but once.

Our friend Pawnee Blanc, the Old Dandy, came one day with a party of Indians asking leave to dance for us in the open space in front of the door. It was a warm, dusty afternoon, and as our friends grew hot and tired with such violent exercise kept up so long, a pitcher of raspberry negus was made and sent out to them. Pawnee took the pitcher and the tumbler, filled the tumbler about half full, and gave it to the first man in the circle; then filled it the same way for the next, and so on — until it suddenly occurred to him to look inside the pitcher. What he saw there settled his course of action. He set the tumbler down on the ground, raised the pitcher to his lips with both hands, and took a hearty pull; after which he went on serving, less and less each time, until he was called on to have the pitcher refilled. Everyone present agreed it was the only case any of them had ever seen of an Indian appearing afraid of getting less than his share of anything.

During the payment a good many kegs of whiskey find their way into the Indians' lodges, in spite of the watchfulness of the officers and the Agent both. Where there is a demand there will always be a supply, whatever the law forbids. The last day of the payment is invariably a day of general carousing.

When the men begin their frolic, the women carefully collect all the guns, knives, tomahawks, and weapons of every sort and hide them, so that as little harm as possible may be done while there is no restraint and no reason left. I am sorry to have to record that our little friend Pawnee Blanc was much given to the pleasures of the bottle.

Among the presents for the chiefs that Shaw-nee-aw-kee had brought from the East were a trunk of blue cloth coats trimmed with broad gold lace and a box of round black hats decorated in the same way. Anyone who knows Indians, of whatever tribe, will have noticed that the first step toward civilization, in a man or a woman, is putting on a man's hat trimmed with tinsel, ribbons, or feathers. Pawnee was among the lucky ones remembered in the distribution. He put on his new costume at once, tied a few extra bunches of gay-colored ribbon to the long spear that always served him as a baton of ceremony, and came straight over, followed by an admiring train made up mostly of women, to pay me a visit of state.

The solemn gravity of his face as he waved off anyone who came near enough to finger his new finery, and the dignity with which he strutted along, edging this way and that to avoid the slightest contact with homely everyday wardrobes, promised well for a lasting reign of propriety and self-respect, and a proper regard for the good opinion of everyone around him. But alas for Pawnee! Late in the day we saw him being helped toward his lodge by two stout young Indians who had pulled him out of a ditch, his fine coat covered with mud, his hat battered and bruised, his spear stripped of its gay streamers, and poor Pawnee himself weeping and pouring out all the doleful lamentations of a tipsy Indian.
