Agathe was the daughter of an Indian known by the name of Rascal Day-kau-ray. Whether he deserved the name is something the rest of this will have to settle. He was the brother of the grand old chief of that name, and as unlike him as two men of the same blood can possibly be.

The Day-kau-rays were a very handsome family, and this daughter was remarkable even among them for her looks. A tall, well-made figure, a round, sweet face, and that particularly soft, musical voice the women of her people have would have caught a stranger's eye anywhere; and the sad look on her face drew everyone's heart toward her and made you want to know more of her history. As I had it from her friend Mrs. Paquette, it was a touching one indeed.

A young officer at the Fort had seen her and had set — I will not say his heart, since it is doubtful whether he had one — but his mind on her. He applied to Paquette to negotiate what he called a marriage with her. I am sorry to say that Paquette let himself be talked into the scheme. He knew perfectly well how wrong it was to misrepresent things to Agathe's family, and he knew what misery he was about to bring on her.

The poor girl was already engaged to a young man of her own people, and, as is usually the case, the attachment on both sides was very strong. Among these simple people, who have few things to think about or speculate on beyond the business of daily life, their affections and their hatreds are the warp and woof of their character. All their feelings are intense, because they are concentrated on so few objects. Family ties, with the women especially, take up the whole of their capacity for feeling.

Marriage is a sacred bond among them, and cannot be dissolved. I have read, in a recent report to the Historical Society of Wisconsin, that in earlier times a temporary marriage between a white man and a Menomonee woman was nothing unusual, and that such an arrangement brought no scandal with it. I am afraid that if such cases were looked into, a great deal of deceit and misrepresentation would be found to have been added to the other sins of the business, and that the woman would turn out to have been a victim of such a connection, and not a willing party to it.

In any case, no system of the kind exists among the Winnebagoes. The strictest sense of propriety in women is one of the things that marks them out. A woman who breaks it is said to have "forgotten herself," and she is certain to be cast off and "forgotten" by her friends.

The marriage proposed between the young officer and Day-kau-ray's daughter was understood to be meant as a true and lasting one. The father would never have laid himself open to the contempt of his whole nation by selling his daughter to be any man's mistress. The Day-kau-rays, as I have said elsewhere, were not a little proud of the distant strain of French blood that ran in with the native stream in their veins, and probably when Agathe's father agreed to the match he was moved as much by the honor he thought it did him as by the quantity of valuable presents that came with the offer.

However that may be, the poor girl was torn away from the man she loved and moved out of her father's lodge into the young officer's quarters.

There were no ladies in the garrison at that time. If there had been, such a step would hardly have been risked. Far off in the wilderness, shut out from the wholesome influences of religion and society, is it any wonder that the moral sense goes blind now and then, and that a man makes the choice, "Evil, be thou my good"?

The first step in the wrong was followed by another one, crueler still. The young officer left the post on furlough, he said — and never came back. Word came after a while that he was married, and when he rejoined his regiment it was at another post.

There was a natural feeling in the force of the "woe pronounced against him" by more tongues than one. "He will never," my informant said, "dare to show himself in this country again! There is not an Indian who knows the Day-kau-rays who would not take his life if he met him!"
