Before long we had to keep the doors and windows shut, to shut out a sight of misery we could do nothing about. If a door was opened to let one of the family in, some desperate mother would rush in, take hold of my baby's hand, put her own starving child's hand into it, and tell us pleadingly that he was begging "his little brother" for food. The strongest man alive could not have watched that heart-rending scene dry-eyed, and it was in front of us constantly. It was no use screening the lower half of the windows with curtains: they climbed up on the outside, and row upon row of gaunt, wretched faces would look in over the top, watching us, to see whether we really were as badly off as we said we were.

The noble old Day-kau-ray came one day from the Barribault to tell us how things stood in his village. More than forty of his people, he said, had now gone many days with nothing to eat but bark and roots. My husband went with him to the commanding officer, so he could tell his story and find out whether any quantity of food could be had from that quarter. The upshot was a promise of a small allowance of flour, enough to take the edge off his own family's hunger.

When this was explained to the chief, he turned away. "No," he said. "If his people could not be relieved, he and his family would starve with them!" And he refused the help that was offered, even for those nearest and dearest to him, until everyone could share alike.
