There was nobody at Barney Lawton's when we got there except a Frenchman and a small number of Indians. My sister and I got down off our horses and went into the house, which had its door standing open. Two Indians were sitting on the floor, smoking. They raised their eyes as we appeared, and I shall never forget the look of wonder and horror on both their faces. Their lips went slack until one of them dropped his pipe on the floor. Their eyes seemed to be starting out of their heads, and they lifted their hands with the palms spread out, as if to wave us away from them, and slowly got out the word: "Manitou!" (a spirit.)

When we raised our masks and came forward smiling to shake hands with them, they sprang to their feet and fairly let out a cry of delight at the sight of our familiar faces.

"Bon-jour, bon-jour, Maman!" was their greeting, and then they plunged out of doors to tell their companions what had happened.

Our afternoon's ride took us across a prairie that stretched away to the northeast. Not a living creature was to be seen on its whole broad expanse, but flying and circling over our heads were countless flocks of curlews, "Screaming their wild notes to the listening waste."

Their strange, shrill cry — "crack, crack, crack — rackety, rackety, rackety," repeated from dozens of throats at once, and sometimes delivered a few inches above our ears — eventually became almost unbearable. They seemed to have lost their senses entirely in the excitement of so unusual and splendid a procession through what had until then been their own desolate kingdom.
