After crossing the river at what is now Depere and getting into the wild, unsettled country on the west side, we came to one wooded hill after another, separated by ravines so narrow and so steep that it seemed impossible for any animal but a mule or a goat to get through them.

Wish-tay-yun went in front. The horse he rode was used to the country and well trained for that kind of road. As for Charlie, he was perfectly admirable. When he came to a sheer drop, he would put his forefeet out in front of him and slide down on his haunches in the most scientific way, while the only method I had of keeping my balance was to hold tight to the bridle and lay myself back almost flat against him, braced. Then our position would suddenly reverse, and we would be scaling the opposite bank at the very real risk of falling over backward into the ravine below.

It was funny to watch Wish-tay-yun scrambling on ahead of us and turning half round now and then to see how I was getting on. And when I finally got to the top, panting and laughing, he would throw up his hands and shout, with the greatest glee, "Mamma Manitou!" (My mother is a spirit.)

Our old acquaintances the Grignons seemed very much surprised that I had ventured on such a trip. They had never tried it themselves, even though they had lived so long at the Kakalin — but then there was no reason why they should have. They could always get a canoe or a boat whenever they wanted to visit "the Bay."

As we expected, our gentlemen caught up with us at supper. "They had stayed behind to have dinner with Colonel Stambaugh — had had a delightful gallop up from the Bay — had seen no ravines, nor anything but fine smooth roads — might have been asleep, but if so they weren't aware of it." That was the account they gave of themselves, and it amused us a good deal.
