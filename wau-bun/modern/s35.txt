March 9th. Today's ride took us past the first of the Four Lakes. Scattered along its banks was a Winnebago encampment. They greeted their Father with noisy delight — "Bon-jour, bon-jour, Shaw-nee-aw-kee," "Hee-nee-kar-ray-kay-noo?" (how do you do?)

Then came the usual announcement: "Wys-kap-rah tshoonsh-koo-nee-noh!" (I have no bread.)

That is how they ask for something. But we could not afford to be generous. There was no certainty of getting more if our own supply ran out, and that forced us to keep to the strictest economy.

How beautiful that camp looked in the morning sun! The matted lodges with blue smoke curling off their tops, the trees and bushes dusted with a light snow that had fallen in the night, the lake shining and sparkling almost at our feet — even the Indians in their distinctive dress adding to the picture.

I was sorry to leave it, and we had to leave it in a great hurry, because Souris, the pack-horse, had taken it into his head to decamp while we were talking with our red friends. He had very sensibly decided to carry on the trip in the right direction, so we were lucky enough to catch up with him after a short chase, and once young Roy, whose particular charge he was, had given him a good scolding and a few blows, he was put in the middle of the cavalcade in disgrace for neglecting his duty.

After we left the lake our road ran over rolling prairie, bare and desolate enough now. The hollows were full of snow, half thawed, which gave the horses very uncertain footing, and I could not help joining in the ringing laughter of our Frenchmen whenever Brunet and Souris, the two ponies, went floundering almost up to their bellies through the yielding stuff. Even the boastful Plante, who prided himself on his horsemanship, was nearly unseated once or twice through picking his road badly. Some of the rises were covered with thicket or copse, and in these our dogs would usually start up a deer or two. The first bound, or "lope," was the signal for a chase; the horses seemed to catch the spirit of it as one halloo answered another. But we never had the luck to get a shot at one, because although the dogs did catch a deer once or twice, they were not strong enough to hold it. It was about the middle of the afternoon when we reached the Blue Mound. I was very glad to have got that far, because I was badly tired and every mile now felt like two to me. The miles in this country really are unconscionably long. When I was told we still had seven of them to do before we got to "Morrison's," where we meant to spend the night, I was almost in despair. It was my first trip on horseback, and I was not yet hardened to the exercise.

By the time we reached Morrison's I was so worn out that when my husband tried to lift me down from the saddle I fell into his arms.

"This will never do," he said. "Tomorrow we must turn back toward Fort Winnebago."

The door opened hospitably to take us in. We were welcomed by a lady with the sweetest, kindest face, and by her companion, some years younger. The first was Mrs. Morrison; the other, Miss Elizabeth Dodge, General Dodge's daughter.

My husband laid me on a small bed in the room where the two ladies had been sitting at their work. They took off my bonnet and riding-dress, chafed my hands, and made me some warm wine and water, which soon revived me. Half an hour's rest refreshed me enough that I could talk with the ladies and set my husband's mind at ease about me. Tea was announced shortly after, and we went across to another building — for Morrison's, like every settler's establishment of that period, was a group of separate log houses or cabins, each with one room, or at most two.

The table groaned with good things, and put me in mind of some I had seen among the old-fashioned Dutch families on the banks of the Hudson.

My spirits had come back and we made quite a cheerful party. Mrs. Morrison told us that in her first eighteen months in this country she had not spoken to a white woman: the only company she had was her husband and two black servant-women.

A Tennessee woman had dropped in with her small son just before tea, and we entertained Mr. Kinzie with a description of the pair. The mother's visit was purely a courtesy call. She was a little dumpy woman, her complexion burned perfectly red by the sun, her hair an exact tow-color, braided up from her forehead in front and from her neck behind. The two tails met on the top of her head and were fastened with a small tin comb. Her dress was checked homespun and a very tight fit, and since she wore no ruff or handkerchief round her neck, she looked as though she had just been got ready for execution. She was plainly awestruck at finding visitors there and seemed ready to leave on the spot, but the boy was not so easily cowed. He refused to understand her signs and pinches until he had sidled up to Mrs. Morrison and, pulling his old hat even further down over his eyes, asked her for a whang — meaning a narrow strip of deerskin. The lady very obligingly cut him one from a large smoked skin, which she produced from wherever it was kept, and mother and son took their leave, smiling but looking rather scared.

After tea we went back to Mrs. Morrison's parlor, where she kindly insisted that I lie down again on the little bed to recruit me, as she put it, for the next day's ride. My husband meanwhile went off to see about accommodation for his men and horses.

In the conversation that followed I learned that Mrs. Morrison had spent a good deal of time near my recent home in Oneida County, that many of the friends I had loved and valued were her friends too, and that on hearing of my arrival at Fort Winnebago she had actually thought of coming to visit me there, to begin an acquaintance that had now come about by quite other and unexpected means.

We talked long and happily together until it was late, and we both took great satisfaction in going over old friends and past events — much to the edification of Miss Dodge, and of the gentlemen once they rejoined us.
