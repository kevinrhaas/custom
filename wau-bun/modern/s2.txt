For a while the run across Thunder Bay was lovely. Thunder Bay did not intend to let that stand. A storm came down on us fast and hard, and worse, it settled in and stayed. The pitching and rolling of that small boat, the closeness of the air below, even the seasickness — we took all of it the way we were supposed to. We had expected it. We were ready for it. What we were not ready for was the water.

The rain came down in sheets and found every seam and pore in the deck and the moulding. It came down the stairway. It came through the joints and the cracks. It soaked the carpet first, then the bedding, and finally it drove us — "by stress of weather" — into the Gentlemen's Cabin. The men gave way for us very gallantly and did everything they could think of to make us comfortable, and we were just congratulating ourselves on having found shelter when the seams overhead opened and dumped such a flood on our devoted heads that umbrellas were no protection at all. There was nothing left for the women and children but the berths, which in that cabin at least stayed dry, and there we stayed, tucked in, the whole live-long day. Our dinner was handed to us on our pillows. The men picked the driest spots they could find, put up their umbrellas, sat under them, and told funny stories to keep our spirits up, and went on doing it until the rain finally quit. At nine that evening came the word we wanted: we had reached the pier at Mackinac.
