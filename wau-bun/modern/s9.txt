The next morning it was still raining. Rain, rain, nothing but rain. The men went out in it anyway, taking a small boat out to the steamer to bring up the luggage — and not the least important part of our share of it was several boxes of silver for paying the Winnebago annuities at the Portage.

I went out onto the piazza with some of the others to watch them go. A gentleman pointed out Fort Howard for me, on a point of land across the water about three-quarters of a mile off: the old barracks, the picketed enclosure, the walls, all of it quaint-looking and, considering how recently it had gone up, genuinely ancient and venerable. Then we turned our attention to the boat, which by that time had reached midstream. One of the passengers was standing up in the stern, apparently giving directions.

"That's a bold fellow," somebody said. "If he's not careful he'll lose his balance." And at that exact moment we watched him turn a complete backward somersault and vanish into the river.

"Oh!" I cried. "He'll drown!"

The men laughed. "No, there he is. They're pulling him back in."

The boat turned around at once and came back to shore. It was not until one of them stepped out and came toward me, dripping and laughing, that I recognized him as my own personal property. He chose to treat the whole thing as a joke. I thought it was a rather sad start to my Western experience.

He let himself be talked into leaving his belongings to his friends, changed his clothes, and was settling in to stay quietly with me when a vehicle pulled up at the door and we saw the pleasant, familiar face of our old friend Judge Doty.

He had heard we were in and had come to carry us straight off to his house. We were only too glad to gather up our bags and travelling-baskets and go with him without any further ceremony.

The drive took us first along the edge of Navarino, then through Shanty-town — much the better-named of the two — through mud and mire, over bad roads, up and down hilly, break-neck stretches, until we came to our friends' little brick house. Mrs. Doty received us with such genuine, sisterly kindness, and the whole place felt so full of welcome, that we were at home in no time.

We discovered that in expectation of our arrival, invitations had already gone out to gather the entire circle of Green Bay society to meet us at an evening party. In a new country, that is the established way of doing honor to guests and strangers.

We asked, and learned that Captain Harney — who had generously offered to come down with a boat and a crew of soldiers from Fort Winnebago to carry us up to that place, our destined home — had not arrived yet. So we felt free to arrange a few days of enjoying ourselves at the Bay.

For people that cut off from the world at large, it was a pleasure to hear all the news we had brought: every particular of how life was being lived, how people behaved, the thousand small items no newspaper of that day would have dreamed of printing, the fashions, and in general the gossip a lady is wrongly assumed to be better informed about than a gentleman.

I remember perfectly that the day went by in an almost unbroken stream of talk, given and received. Everyone in the party but me had made the trip — the voyage, rather — up the Fox River and down the Wisconsin to the Mississippi.

There were no end of stories about one particular journey the three of them had made in company with a French trader and his two sisters, who were then making their debut as Western travelers. How Mademoiselle Julie would borrow a fine damask napkin or two, without asking, to wipe out the ducks before cooking them. How impossible it was to persuade either sister that the table things ought to be washed and rinsed properly before being packed away in the mess-basket — the result being that another good napkin had to be whisked out on the sly to wipe the dishes when mealtime came. And the young gentleman's amusement in hunting his misappropriated property out of the nooks and corners of the boat, tying it all together with a cord, and hanging it over the stern to make its own way down the Wisconsin to Prairie du Chien.

Then there was a first-rate story about M. Rolette himself. At one point on the route — crossing Winnebago Lake, I think — the travelers met one of the Company's boats heading down to Green Bay for supplies. M. Rolette was one of the Company's agents and the men in the boat worked for him. Naturally, after weeks away from home, meeting on that lonely water and trading news was a great occasion.

The boats stopped. Greetings were exchanged in earnest. One question came after another.

"Eh bien — have they finished the new house?"

"Oui, Monsieur."

"Et la cheminée, fume-t-elle?" (Does the chimney smoke?)

"Non, Monsieur."

"And the harvest — how's that?"

"Very fine indeed."

"Is the mill working?"

"Yes, plenty of water."

"How's Whip?" (his favorite horse.)

"Oh, Whip is first-rate."

Everything about the store, the farm, and the business in all its branches having been gone over to his satisfaction, there was no reason to stay any longer. Time to move on.

"Eh bien — adieu! bon voyage!"

"Arrachez, mes gens!" (Go ahead, men!)

Then, suddenly — "Arrêtez! arrêtez!" (Stop, stop!)

"Comment se portent Madame Rolette et les enfans?" (How are Mrs. Rolette and the children?)

That day and all its excitement finally ended, and we went to bed grateful that General Root and his secretary were not stationed at the head of it with their budget of political news.
