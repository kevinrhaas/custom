The farm at Lee's Place was worked by a Mr. White and three men he employed to help him look after it.

On the afternoon of the day this narrative begins, a party of ten or twelve Indians, dressed and painted, arrived at the house and — as is the custom among savages — walked in and sat down without any ceremony.

Something about the way they looked and behaved made one of the household, a Frenchman, suspicious. He said, "I don't like the look of these Indians. They are none of our people. I can tell by their dress and their paint that they aren't Pottowattamies."

Another of the household, a discharged soldier, then said to the boy who was there, "If that's so, we had better get away from them while we can. Don't say anything — just do what you see me do."

The afternoon was well on by then, so the soldier strolled unhurriedly down toward the canoes, two of which were tied up near the bank. Some of the Indians asked where he was going. He pointed at the cattle standing among the haystacks on the opposite bank and made signs that they had to go and feed them, and would come back afterward for their supper.

He got into one canoe and the boy into the other. The stream was narrow and they were across in no time. Once on the other side they pulled some hay for the cattle, made a show of rounding them up, and gradually worked their way around until the haystacks hid what they were doing — then took to the woods, which were close by, and ran for the fort.

They had gone about a quarter of a mile when they heard two guns fired, one after the other, and they took it that the shots had been aimed at the companions they had left behind.
