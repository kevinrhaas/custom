The Butte des Morts — the Hillock of the Dead — was the site, a long time back, of an extremely bloody battle between the French and the Mis-qua-kees, or Foxes. The slaughter in that fight was so great that its memory has been kept alive in the grim name given to the mound where the dead were buried. Up to that time the Foxes had lived along the shores of the river they had given their name to, but being completely overwhelmed and beaten, they pulled back toward the Mississippi and took refuge with their allies the Saukies — the Sauks, as they are called now — and gradually merged with them, until the combined tribes came to be known by the name they carry today: the Sauks and Foxes.

Among the French living in the upper country, every tribe of Indians has its own nickname, describing some peculiarity of their habits or their looks. The Chippewas, for their quickness, are called Sauteurs, Jumpers. The Ottawas are the Courtes-oreilles, the Short-ears. The Menomonees, after the wild rice that grows so thick in their country, are the Folles Avoines. The Winnebagoes, from their habit of wearing polecat fur on their legs when they go out equipped for war, are les Puans. The Pottowattamies, for their uncleanly habits, are les Poux. The Foxes are les Renards, and so on down the list.

The result is that you will never hear a French or half-breed resident of that country refer to an Indian any other way. "That man is a Court-oreille." "Is that woman a Winnebago?" "No, she's a Folle Avoine." It leaves a stranger a little confused at first about how to classify the people he is meeting.

Every native friend gathered around us here was one of les Puans — or to use their own much better-sounding name for themselves, the Ho-tshung-rahs.

Having said a very regretful goodbye to our friend Judge Doty, whose company had done so much for the pleasure of the trip and whose example had taught us the valuable lesson of taking things as we find them, we said goodbye early after breakfast to our kind hosts and started on.

From Butte des Morts to the Portage is about seventy miles by land. By water it is not less than a hundred and thirty, so winding is the river's course through the low swampy prairies that cover a great part of this country.
