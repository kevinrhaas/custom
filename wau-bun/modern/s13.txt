The arrangement was that Judge Doty would ride in our boat as far as the Butte des Morts, where his man would be waiting with horses to take him on to Mineral Point, where he had court to hold.

It was a bright, beautiful morning when we left his pleasant house to begin the passage up the Fox River. Captain Harney meant to stay a few days longer at the Bay, but he came down to walk us to the boat and see us settled in comfort.

Helping me over the ploughed ground and the other rough places between the house and the riverbank where the boat lay, he told me how impatiently Mrs. Twiggs was waiting for me. She was the commanding officer's wife, and since the previous spring she had been the only white woman at Fort Winnebago. We had met in New York not long after her marriage, so we were not quite strangers. For my own part I felt certain that once there were two of us, once my piano was safely up there, once the Post Library we had bought was unpacked, once everything was properly arranged and settled, we would be — far off in the wilderness or not — the happiest little circle you could imagine. Every expectation I had was cheerful and confident.

What was waiting for us was a medium-sized Mackinac boat with a crew of soldiers and our own three voyageurs besides: a dark hull about thirty feet long. Amidships stood a framework of light posts holding up a canvas roof, with canvas curtains that could be dropped along the sides and ends like the blinds on a country stagecoach, or rolled up to let in the light and air.

In the middle of that little cabin sat the box with my piano in it, and on top of the box a mattress, which would be our divan by day and our bed at night if the weather ever turned too wet or too foul for camping. Next to it were stowed the boxes of silver my husband would use to pay the annuities owed his red children by treaty. The mess-basket was within easy reach, and we had bought a pair of big square covered baskets from the Waubanakees — the New York Indians — to hold our outer clothes and bedding and to double as handy little work tables or dinner tables.

Being a true daughter of New England, I need hardly say I had not forgotten to bring knitting and embroidery. Books and pencils went without saying.

Most of our furniture, along with the housekeeping goods we had laid in at New York and Detroit, was to follow in another boat under men whose stated business was getting cargo safely up the rapids and on to Fort Winnebago. That was a job requiring some three weeks and an enormous amount of labor, and the owners of the goods could count themselves lucky to get them at all, however wet, broken and battered they turned out to be. That was exactly why we kept our best possessions with us, even at the cost of being a little crowded.

Until that day I had never seen a gentleman in a colored shirt. A spotless white collar and shirt-front was one of those notions Boston, and therefore New England folks in general, held about what was becoming in a man's dress. Mrs. Cass had warned me, laughing, that calico shirts and patchwork pillowcases were an indispensable part of a traveling outfit — and sure enough, thanks to the taste and skill of some tidy little Frenchwoman, our divan pillows turned out to be dressed in the brightest, most variegated covers imaginable.

The Judge and my husband were gorgeous in the deepest blue and pink. Each of them also had a bright red cap — a bonnet rouge, or tuque, as the voyageurs call it — which, out of respect for the lady present, was only to be put on when a heavy dinner, a dull book, or a shortage of exercise made an afternoon nap unavoidable.

The Judge was an admirable traveling companion. He had lived in that country for years, had gone with General Cass on his expedition to the head-waters of the Mississippi, and carried an enormous stock of anecdotes about the early days, the customs and the people.

A few examples of how justice was administered back then come back to me.

There was an old Frenchman at the Bay named Réaume, hugely ignorant and grasping, though otherwise reasonably good-natured, who was made a justice of the peace. Two men came before him once, one as plaintiff and one as defendant. The justice listened patiently to the complaint and then to the defense, then rose with dignity and delivered his decision:

"You are both wrong. You, Bois-vert" — this to the plaintiff — "you bring me one load of hay. And you, Crély" — to the defendant — "you bring me one load of wood. And now the matter is settled." There is no record of anybody objecting to the verdict.

That story led to another one, set at Prairie du Chien on the Mississippi.

There was a Frenchman there, another justice of the peace, universally known as Old Boilvin. His office stood just outside the walls of the fort, and it was very much the fashion among the officers to drop in of a morning to find some sport for an idle hour and take a glass of brandy and water with the old gentleman, which he called taking a little quelque-chose.

A soldier named Fry had been accused of stealing and butchering a calf belonging to M. Rolette, and the constable — a bricklayer named Bell — had been sent to arrest the culprit and bring him in for trial.

While the gentlemen were paying their usual morning call on the justice, there was a noise in the entry and a knock at the door.

"Come in," called Old Boilvin, getting up and going toward it.

Bell: Here, sir, I've brought Fry to you, like you ordered.

Justice: Fry, you great rascal! What for you kill M. Rolette's calf?

Fry: I did not kill M. Rolette's calf.

Justice (shaking his fist): You lie, you great —— rascal! Bell, take him to jail. Come, gentlemen, come, let us take a leetle quelque-chose.

The Canadian boatmen always sing while they row or paddle, and nothing spurs them on like hearing the bourgeois take the lead in the music. If the passengers join in the refrain — especially the female ones — the compliment counts for even more.

Their songs are light and cheerful, usually built around some little bit of satire or wit, and shaped to produce a spirited and sometimes uproarious chorus.

Verse and refrain run about like this:

BOURGEOIS. — Par-derrière chez ma tante, Par-derrière chez ma tante.

CHORUS. — Par-derrière chez ma tante, Par-derrière chez ma tante.

BOURGEOIS. — Il y a un coq qui chante, Des pommes, des poires, des raves, des choux, Des figues nouvelles, des raisins doux.

CHORUS. — Des pommes, des poires, des raves, des choux, Des figues nouvelles, des raisins doux.

BOURGEOIS. — Il y a un coq qui chante, Il y a un coq qui chante.

CHORUS. — Il y a un coq qui chante, and so on.

BOURGEOIS. — Demande une femme à prendre, Des pommes, des poires, des raves, des choux, and so on.

CHORUS. — Des pommes, des poires, and so on.

BOURGEOIS. — Demande une femme à prendre, Demande une femme à, and so on.

And on it goes, with advice offered one piece at a time:

Ne prenez pas une noire, Car elles aiment trop à boire. Ne prenez pas une rousse, Car elles sont trop jalouses.

By the time every possible qualification has been run through and rejected, stretched out by that endless repetition of the chorus, you hear the bourgeois shout:

"Whoop la! à terre, à terre — pour la pipe!"

The voyageurs stop every five or six miles without fail to rest and smoke, and that used to be how distance was measured out there — so many pipes, not so many miles.

The Canadian melodies are sometimes very beautiful, and it is hard to imagine a more exhilarating way to travel than over that water, surrounded by all the wild magnificence of the country, with the measured stroke of the oars keeping time to "Le Rosier Blanc," "En roulant ma Boule," or "Lève ton pied, ma jolie Bergère."

The high point of the fun was a comic piece that never seemed to go stale no matter how often they sang it. It went something like this:

BOURGEOIS. — Michaud est monté dans un prunier, Pour treiller des prunes. La branche a cassé —

CHORUS. — Michaud a tombé?

BOURGEOIS. — Ou est-ce qu'il est?

CHORUS. — Il est en bas.

BOURGEOIS. — Oh! reveille, reveille, reveille, Oh! reveille, Michaud est en haut!

Etiquette required you to look astonished at Michaud's luck in staying up in the tree even though the branch broke, and the joke had to be run through every kind of fruit tree Michaud might conceivably climb.
