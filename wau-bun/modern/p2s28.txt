In 1816, Mr. Kinzie and his family came back to Chicago again. The fort was rebuilt on a somewhat larger scale than the old one. It was not until the troops returned that the bones of the unfortunate Americans killed four years before were gathered up and buried.

An Indian Agency was set up under the charge of Charles Jewett, Esq., of Kentucky. He was succeeded in 1820 by Dr. Alexander Wolcott of Connecticut, who held the post until his death in 1830.

The troops were withdrawn from the garrison in 1823, but brought back in 1828, after the Winnebago war. That was a disturbance between the Winnebagoes and the white settlers on and near the Mississippi. After several murders had been committed, the young chief Red Bird was taken and imprisoned at Prairie du Chien to wait for his trial, and there he killed himself out of chagrin and the misery of confinement. It was feared that the Pottowattamies would make common cause with the Winnebagoes and start a general campaign of havoc and bloodshed along the frontier. What probably kept them from it were the efforts of Billy Caldwell, Robinson, and Shaw-bee-nay, who made an expedition among the Rock River bands to argue and persuade them into staying quiet.

The few citizens of Chicago in those days led, for the most part, a very quiet and unvaried life. Game was so plentiful and the land they farmed so immensely fertile that they had far more than they could use of every luxury of garden, corn-field, and dairy. A friend back in the "East countrie" once asked the question,

"What do you do with all the good things you raise? You have no market?" "No." "And you can't eat it all yourselves?" "No." "Then what do you do with it?"

"Why, when a vessel comes in, we manage to talk the captain into accepting a few kegs of butter and a stock of corn and vegetables as a present, and that helps us get rid of some of our surplus."

The mails came in, as you might expect, at very rare intervals. They were brought sometimes from Fort Clark (Peoria), but more often from Fort Wayne, or across the peninsula of Michigan, which was still a wilderness peopled with savages. The hardy adventurer who acted as express-rider often had to take a leaf from the birds of the air and "lodge among the branches," to keep himself and his charge safe.

Visitors were very rare, unless it was a friend who came to stay for several months and share the life of the wilderness. Now and then, though, a traveler found his way to the place while passing to or from "parts unknown," and a man like that was certain of a hospitable and hearty welcome.

A gentleman travelling up from the southern settlements arrived late one evening at Wolf Point, where George Hunt and a Mr. Wallace then had their small trading establishment. He stopped and asked whether he could get accommodation for the night for himself and his horse. The answer was that they were badly set up for entertaining a stranger — the house was small, and they were keeping "bachelor's hall."

"Is there nowhere," the traveler asked, "that I can get a lodging?"

"Oh yes — you'll find a very comfortable house, Mr. Kinzie's, about half a mile below, near the mouth of the river."

The stranger turned his horse's head and took the road they had pointed out.

When he got to the spot, his first question was, "Is this Mr. Kinzie's house?"

"Yes, sir."

"I'd be glad of accommodation for myself and my horse."

"Certainly, sir — walk in."

The horse was taken off to the stable while the gentleman was shown into a parlor where two ladies were sitting. The usual opening questions and answers were gone through — in a new country people get acquainted quickly — and before long the gentleman found himself sitting down to a comfortable hot supper. We will venture to say a fine supper, since the table in this household has always been somewhat famous.

He evidently enjoyed it, because he made himself thoroughly at home. He even called for a boot-jack after tea and pulled off his boots. The ladies were a little surprised, but they had been out of the world a good while, and there was no telling what changes in etiquette might have come in during their retirement.

Before saying good night, the traveler let them know what he would like for breakfast, and it was duly prepared. The next day turned out stormy. The gentleman was satisfied with his quarters, and having taken care to satisfy himself that there was no neglect or shortfall in the accommodation as far as his horse was concerned, he got through the day very comfortably.

Every so often, when he got tired of reading, he would talk with the family, and on the whole he seemed not at all inclined to hold himself aloof, but to allow himself a little proper sociability, seeing they were all off there together in the woods.

On the second day the weather cleared. The traveler announced that he meant to leave. He ordered his horse brought to the door — and then he called for his bill.

"My house is not a tavern, sir," came the astounding reply.

"Not a tavern! Good heavens — have I been making myself at home like this in a private family?"

He was profuse with his apologies, which were quite unnecessary, since the family had seen from the first what mistake he had fallen into, and had entertained themselves all through his visit by looking forward to the consternation of their guest when he should be undeceived.

It was in the year 1816 — the year the fort was rebuilt, after its destruction by the Indians — that the tract of land Chicago stands on, together with the surrounding country, was ceded to the United States by the Pottowattamies. They stayed on it as its peaceful occupants, though, for twenty years more. It was not until 1836 that the Government removed them to lands set aside for their use on the Upper Missouri.

In the year 1830 the town of Chicago was laid out into lots by Commissioners appointed by the State. At that time the prices of these lots ran from ten dollars to sixty.

Mr. Kinzie, who because of this place's geographical position and the vast fertility of the country around it had always predicted its eventual prosperity and importance, was not allowed to see his predictions come true. He closed his useful and energetic life on the 6th of January, 1828, having just completed his sixty-fifth year.
