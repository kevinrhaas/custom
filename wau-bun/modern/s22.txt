Major and Mrs. Twiggs, a few of the younger officers — nearly all the older ones were away — and our brother Robert, or "Bob," as he is called throughout all the Indian tribes, gave us a warm welcome. Just how warm, only those can know who have arrived, as we had, at a remote, isolated home in the wilderness. The Major insisted that we take possession at once of the vacant quarters inside the fort, instead of going out to "the Agency," as had been proposed.

"No — you must be under the same roof with us. Mrs. Twiggs has been more than four months without another woman for company, and she will certainly not hear of a separation now. But you must be our guests until the boats with your furniture arrive." Those boats, in the care of our old acquaintance Hamilton Arndt, were making their way slowly up from Green Bay.

A dinner had been prepared for us. That is one of the advantages of the zigzag approach by the Fox River: travelers never take their friends by surprise. When the whole circle sat down at that hospitable table, we were a genuinely merry company.

After dinner Mrs. Twiggs showed me the quarters we had been given, on the other side of the wide hall. They came to two large rooms on each of the building's three floors. On the ground floor the front room was empty. The one behind it was plainly meant to be the bedroom, as was obvious from a huge, unwieldy bedstead of dimensions ample enough to have held Og, the King of Bashan, with Mrs. Og and the children into the bargain. We could not keep from laughing. But the bedstead was nothing next to the other structure that took up a second corner of the room.

This edifice had been built under the personal supervision of one of our young lieutenants, and you could see at a glance that he and the soldiers who put it together had used up every scrap of architectural skill they had between them. The timbers it was made of had been grooved and carved; the pillars holding up the front swelled in and out in the most fanciful way; the doors were not merely panelled but radiated in a pattern calculated to impress any unsophisticated eye. One of these had been erected in every set of quarters, to make up for the lack of closets — an inconvenience that never occurred to the bachelors who planned the building until it was too late. The three compartments each structure contained were unquestionably designed for a clothes-press, a store-room, and a china-closet; at any rate, that is what Mrs. Twiggs was using hers for. There was one slight difficulty: in the china-closet the shelves were set too close together to admit so much as a gravy-boat, but they made up in number what they lacked in space. In honor of the man who designed it, we christened the whole affair a "Davis" — laying the first laurel on the brow of one who was afterward to distinguish himself at Cabinet making of quite another kind.

The bold promontory Fort Winnebago was built on looked down on one side over the wide prairie and the Fox River, and on the other ran off into the thickly wooded ridge that led away to Belle Fontaine and Lake Puckaway.

In front lay a stretch of meadow, and across it ran the Portage road, about two miles long, between the Fox and the Wisconsin Rivers. The Government kept teams of oxen and a driver at the Agency to haul the Indians' canoes across this ground, which in many seasons was wet, miry, and almost impassable.
