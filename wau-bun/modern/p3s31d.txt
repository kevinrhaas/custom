Since we had to find out how badly the cargo of furs had been damaged, somebody proposed an early start. The people in the cottages had apparently grown tired of doing good, because they refused to make us any breakfast, even after we promised they would be well paid for the trouble. So we found ourselves obliged to set off with very thin prospects of a morning meal.

When we got down to the boat, what a sight it was. Bedclothes, cloaks, trunks, mess-basket, packs of furs — everything showing the marks of a complete flood. The boat was ankle-deep in water, and there was literally nowhere on board to stand or to sit. We bailed for a while, and tried arranging the packs of furs that had suffered least into a sort of divan in the middle of the boat, and after that nothing better suggested itself than to get back in and put up with what could not be helped.

Our situation was not an enviable one. Wherever you put a hand or a foot, the water came bubbling up around it — and oh, the state of the bandboxes and the work-baskets. Breakfast there was none, because when we looked into the mess-basket everything in it had run together into one unidentifiable mass. Tea, pepper, salt, short-cake, all floating in company. It was hopeless.

But that was not the worst of it. As the blazing July sun climbed higher, the steam coming off every object on board was almost suffocating. The boat was old, the packs of skins were old, and being near them on a dry day had been anything but pleasant. Now it was unbearable. There was no getting away from it, so we encouraged the children to arm themselves with patience for the short part of the voyage that was left.

Sitting on our fragrant couch, under the shade of a single umbrella that had to protect the whole party from the scorching sun, we drifted wearily down the stream through that long, tedious day. As we passed the Kakalin, then the Rapids, then Dickenson's, then the Agency, how longingly we looked at the houses where other people were enjoying a roof over their heads and something to eat — and how eagerly we counted the hours that still had to pass before we could reach Fort Howard.

There were no songs from the poor Frenchmen that day. Music and fasting do not go together. At last we stopped at Shanty-town, where the boat was to be unloaded. Everyone set to work carrying the cargo into the Fur Company's warehouse, which stood near the landing. It did not take long, because everyone worked with a will. That done, the voyageurs, every one of them, got ready to leave. Mâtâ stormed and raved, and Arthur argued with them, and neither had the least effect.

"No," they said. They had brought the boat and the cargo to the warehouse, and that was the whole of their job. And they turned to go.

"Guardapié," I said, "do you mean to leave us here?"

"Bien, madame! It is the place we always stop at."

"Does Monsieur John pay you for bringing his family down?"

"Oh, yes, Monsieur John has given us an order on the sutler, at the Fort down below."

"To be paid when you deliver us safely at the Fort down below. It looks as though I shall be there before you are, and I will take care of that matter myself. Monsieur John never imagined for a moment that you would behave like this."

The Frenchmen talked it over among themselves, and the upshot was that Guardapié and two others climbed into the boat, took up their oars, and rowed us, rather sulkily, the remaining two miles to Fort Howard.
