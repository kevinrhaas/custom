We said a fond goodbye to our friends, and on the morning of the 8th of March we were mounted and equipped for the road. The weather was fine. The streams were already fringed with green and sparkling in the sun, and everything promised an early, mild season. When we got down to the ferry at the foot of the hill the fort stood on, Major Twiggs tried again and again to talk me out of starting on a trip that he assured me would be more dangerous than I had any idea of. It was no use. My mind was made up.

Our party was swelled by an escort of all the young officers, who politely insisted on coming with us as far as Duck Creek, four miles out. In fact there were some who would gladly have gone the whole way with us, to get out of the monotony of their solitary, uneventful lives. Behind us came an ox cart with a canoe perched on it, meant to carry us over the creek and also over a wide marsh on the far side, which at this time of year was always flooded to a considerable depth. We got a great deal of entertainment out of watching this vehicle bump and thump along a road that had never before known the dignity of a wheeled carriage.

Our little shock-headed, sunburnt, thick-lipped Canadian — who by some miracle happened to be the husband of my pretty servant, Mrs. Pillon — bellowed away at the animals whenever they slowed down or jolted against a stump: "Marchez, don-g," "regardez," "prenez garde," to our endless amusement. My spirits were high. I foresaw no hardship or danger of any kind, and rather pictured myself setting out on a pleasure excursion across the prairies. It had not so much as crossed my mind that a straw bonnet and kid gloves were no sort of equipment for an expedition like this. Having never traveled at such a harsh season, I was carelessly ignorant of how one prepares for it, and I had brushed aside or laughed at my husband's suggestions that I provide myself with blanket socks and a woollen capuchon for my head and shoulders. So now, although the wind gave my headgear a rude lift now and then, and my hands were soon swollen and stiff with cold, I told myself these were trifles I would get used to soon enough. I was too pleased with the novelty of my outfit — my hunting-knife in its gay scabbard hanging from my neck, my tin cup at my saddle-bow — to give minor discomforts much thought.

When we got to Duck Creek we said goodbye to our young friends, who stayed on the bank long enough to see us across — us in the canoe, and the poor horses swimming a stream that was now full of floating cakes of ice.

Beyond the rise of ground that made the far bank there was a marsh perhaps three hundred yards across. The men carried the canoe over to it to ferry us across. The water was not deep, so our attendants simply took the pack off Brunet and my side-saddle off Le Gris to avoid any accidents, then mounted their own horses and led the two spare ones. My husband put the pack-horse's load and my saddle in the middle of the canoe, which he was going to paddle across.

"Now, wifie," he said, "get in, and sit down flat in the bottom of the canoe."

"Oh, no," I said. "I'll sit on the little trunk in the middle. I'll be so much more comfortable, and I can balance the canoe exactly."

"As you like — but I think you'll find it isn't the best way."

One good push sent us a few feet out from the bank. Then the two favorite greyhounds we had brought with us, who had been standing on the bank whining, unwilling to take to the water when they were told to, made a sudden leap and landed square on top of me. The canoe hung in the balance a moment — then gave up — and quicker than you could think it, dogs, furniture, and lady were all in the deepest part of the water.

My husband, who had been on the point of stepping into the canoe himself when the dogs so rudely went ahead of him, was at my side in a moment. He grabbed me by the collar of my cloak and told me not to be frightened. I was not in the least frightened, and only laughed as he lifted me out and set me back on the bank.

The unlucky saddle and little trunk were fished out next, but not before they had got a pretty thorough soaking. Our merriment was increased still further by the sight of the clumsy Pillon, who was trying to ride my spirited Jerry across the marsh. He was clinging to the animal's neck, his face twisted with terror, roaring out every French curse he had. Jerry pranced and curveted, and finally shot his rider — or rather his burden — headfirst several feet into the water.

A general howl of laughter greeted the unfortunate Frenchman, and it doubled as he hauled himself puffing and snorting out of his watery bed and waddled back to where he had started, while the horse very sensibly went off to join his companions, who had already reached the far bank.

"Well, wifie," said Mr. Kinzie, "I can't trust you in the canoe again. There's nothing for it but to carry you over the marsh like a pappoose. Will you take a ride on my shoulders?"

"With all my heart, if you'll promise to get me across safely." And I was up in no time.

I have to admit that the gentleman staggered once or twice under his load, which was no light one, and more than once I was badly afraid I was going to end up like old Pillon — but happily we reached the other side without mishap.

There my husband insisted that I put on dry shoes and stockings and — must I own to it? — drink a little brandy, to head off the effects of my icy bath. He would have liked to halt and build a fire and dry my clothes and belongings properly, but I would not hear of it. I argued that the delay would expose me to more cold than riding on in my wet habit and cloak, and that may well have been true. But mixed in with my convictions on the subject there was a certain reluctance to give our friends at the fort the chance — which they certainly would have taken — to laugh at the inauspicious way we had begun.

The horses were soon sorted out and we set off again. It was a fine day for the season. My wet clothes gave me no trouble; the exercise of riding took away any feeling of chill. It was a new way of travelling for me, and I enjoyed it all the more for having been shut up more than five months inside the walls of the fort, with hardly any variation in our days beyond the occasional half-mile walk into the woods around us.

We were held up once more on the road when we met Lapierre, the blacksmith from Sugar Creek, going up to the Portage for supplies with one of his associates, so we had covered no more than twenty-three miles when we reached the ground we had picked to camp on. It lay on a beautiful stream, a tributary of one of the Four Lakes — that chain whose banks have no rival for romantic loveliness.

I could not help admiring the shrewdness of the horses, who seemed to work out with almost human intelligence that we were nearing the spot where their labors would end. While we were still well short of the point of woods that meant a halt, they pricked up their ears, picked up their pace, and came in at last at a full gallop.

We dismounted in an open space just inside the edge of the wood — or "the timber," as Western travelers call it. My husband advised me to walk about until a fire could be made, which our active and experienced woodsmen managed quickly, felling a large tree in a matter of minutes. The dry grass around us made excellent tinder; lit by sparks from the flint (there were no loco-focos in those days) and helped along by broken branches and bits of lightwood, it soon produced a cheering blaze. Meanwhile "the bourgeois" busied himself putting up the tent, careful to set it facing the fire but at such an angle that the wind would carry the smoke and flame away from the opening. Inside, on the ground, were spread first a bear-skin, then two or three blankets (each rider carried two, one under the saddle and one on top of it), and after that the rest of the luggage was brought in and I was able to strip off all my wet clothing and put on dry. You can get some idea of where the thermometer stood from this: my riding-habit, laid over the end of the huge log our fire was built against, froze stiff within a few minutes and stood up on its own, so that it looked exactly like a dress out of which a lady had vanished in some unaccountable way.

Describing the ham broiled on the "broches," the toasted bread, the steaming coffee and the primitive table-furniture would only be repeating our experience on the Fox River. There is this difference, though: travelling on horseback we carry only a coffee-pot, a tea-kettle, and for each rider a tin cup and a hunting-knife. Manners at table are marked by a total absence of ceremony. The knife is drawn from its scabbard — those who think of it grant it a wipe on the napkin. Its first duty is to stir the cup of coffee; its next, to cut up the piece of ham that is laid on half a travelling biscuit, held in the left hand to serve as a plate. Cutting the meat skillfully enough not to destroy the dish at the same time is an art you can only acquire by long practice.

We take our places around the mat to enjoy what, after a tiring ride, we find delicious food. The Frenchmen sit a little apart, getting their share of coffee, meat, and bread, and sometimes trading jokes with the bourgeois, who is their demigod, and whose affability and condescension never diminish their respect and devotion by a hair.

The meal over, the table things are rinsed in hot water and set aside till morning. A wisp of dry prairie-grass is usually reckoned enough to make the knife fit to go back in its scabbard, and since at this season there is no entertainment beyond watching the awkward progress of the hobbled horses from one patch of pasture to the next, we are generally ready before long to arrange our blankets and turn in.

At daybreak we are woken by the bourgeois shouting, "How! how! how!"

Everyone starts up out of sleep. The fire, which has sometimes been fed during the night, is soon blazing again. The horses are caught and saddled while a breakfast much like the previous evening's meal is got ready. The tent comes down, the pack-horse is loaded — "tout démanché," as the Canadian says. Breakfast finished, we rinse our kettles and cups, tie them to our saddle-bows, and then mount and away, leaving our fire, or rather our smoke, to tell of our visit.
