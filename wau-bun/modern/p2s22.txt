On the third day after the battle, Mr. Kinzie's family, along with the clerks of the establishment, were put into a boat in the care of François, a half-breed interpreter, and taken to St. Joseph's. They stayed there until the following November, under the protection of To-pee-nee-bee's band. They were then taken on to Detroit, escorted by Chandonnai and their trusty Indian friend Kee-po-tah, and handed over as prisoners of war to Colonel McKee, the British Indian Agent.

Mr. Kinzie was not allowed to leave St. Joseph's with his family. His Indian friends insisted that he stay and try to save some remnant of his scattered property. On the trips he made with them for that purpose he wore the dress and the paint of the tribe, so as not to be captured and perhaps killed by men who were still thirsting for blood. In time, though, his anxiety about his family made him follow them to Detroit, where in the month of January he was received and paroled by General Proctor.

Captain and Mrs. Heald were sent across the lake to St. Joseph the day after the battle. He had been wounded twice in the engagement, and she had been wounded seven times.

Lieutenant Helm, who had also been wounded, was carried by some friendly Indians to their village on the Au Sable, and from there to Peoria, where he was set free through the intervention of Mr. Thomas Forsyth, Mr. Kinzie's half-brother. Mrs. Helm went with her parents to St. Joseph, where they lived in the family of Alexander Robinson and were shown every possible kindness and hospitality for several months.

After they got to Detroit, Mrs. Helm was joined by her husband, and then both of them were arrested by order of the British commander and sent on horseback, in the dead of winter, through Canada to Fort George, on the Niagara frontier. When they reached that post, no officer had been appointed to receive them; and despite their long, exhausting journey in the coldest and harshest weather, Mrs. Helm — a delicate woman of seventeen — was left sitting in her saddle outside the gate for more than an hour before anyone offered them fire or food, or even the shelter of a roof. When Colonel Sheaffe, who had been away at the time, was told of this brutal inhospitality, he expressed the greatest indignation. He went to Mrs. Helm at once, apologized in the most courteous manner, and treated both her and Lieutenant Helm with the most considerate kindness, until an exchange of prisoners set them free and they found the means to reach their friends in Steuben County, New York.

Captain Heald had been taken prisoner by an Indian from the Kankakee who had a strong personal regard for him, and who, when he saw how badly wounded and weakened Mrs. Heald was, released her husband so that he could go with his wife to St. Joseph. They were carried there, as has been told, by Chandonnai and his party. Meanwhile the Indian who had so nobly released his prisoner went back to his village on the Kankakee, and had the mortification of finding that what he had done had caused great dissatisfaction in his band. The displeasure shown was so strong that he made up his mind to travel to St. Joseph and reclaim his prisoner.

Word of what he intended reached To-pee-nee-bee and Kee-po-tah, who had charge of the prisoners. They held a private council with Chandonnai, Mr. Kinzie, and the principal men of the village, and the upshot was a decision to send Captain and Mrs. Heald to the island of Mackinac and hand them over to the British.

So they were put into a bark canoe, and Robinson and his wife paddled them the whole distance of three hundred miles along the coast of Michigan, where they were surrendered as prisoners of war to the officer commanding at Mackinac.

As an example of Captain Heald's habit of putting things off, it is worth mentioning that even after he had certain word that his Indian captor was on his way from the Kankakee to St. Joseph to retake him, he would still have spent another day there getting ready for a more comfortable trip to Mackinac.

The soldiers, with their wives and their surviving children, were scattered among the various Pottowattamie villages on the Illinois, the Wabash, Rock River, and at Milwaukie, until the following spring, when most of them were carried to Detroit and ransomed.
