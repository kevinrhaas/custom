Black Hawk and a few of his warriors got away to the north, where the One-eyed Day-kau-ray and his party captured them soon afterward and brought them as prisoners to General Street at Prairie du Chien. The women and children of the band had been put into canoes and sent down the Mississippi, in the hope that they would be allowed to cross and join the rest of that tribe.

The canoes had been tied together, and a great many of them tipped over and the children drowned, their mothers being too weak and worn out to save them. The ones who lived were taken prisoner and brought, starving and miserable, to Prairie du Chien. Our mother was at the Fort when they arrived. She said their condition was more wretched and more reduced than anything she had ever seen in her life. One woman who spoke a little Chippewa told her what they had suffered and been through, and it was truly appalling.

After eating whatever horses they could spare, they had lived on acorns, elm-bark, even grass. Many had starved to death, and the pursuing whites found their bodies lying along their trail. This poor woman had lost her husband in battle and all her children when their canoe went over, and the only thing she wanted now was to go and join them. Poor Indians! Who can wonder that they do not love the whites?
