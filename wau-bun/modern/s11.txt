Our evening party went the way parties go anywhere. The part that interested me most, because it was the newest, was talking with some young ladies I was introduced to, all of them born at Green Bay or nearby. Their mother was a Menomonee; their father was a Frenchman, descended from a settler several generations back who there is reason to believe belonged to a branch of the same Grignon family that Madame de Sévigné's daughter married into. At any rate, the family is said to hold a quantity of old papers and records that give that impression, though the spelling of the name has shifted a little. However that may be, the Miss Grignons were strikingly dignified, well-bred young women, and there was a charm in their soft voices and their original, unstudied remarks that a stranger found very attractive.

They also opened up a brand-new field of anxiety for me. When I said how impatient I was to see my new home, they looked astonished and said:

"Vous n'avez donc pas peur des serpens?"

Snakes! Was it possible there were snakes at Fort Winnebago?

"At the Portage! Oh, yes — you can hardly go out walking for them. Rattlesnakes. Copperheads. Every kind!"

I am not naturally a timid woman, but I have to admit the thought of the serpens sonnettes and the siffleurs was not a matter I could be indifferent about.

One of those young ladies had a tall, graceful figure, a rich, blooming complexion and a dark, flashing eye that would have made her stand out in any drawing room. Another had such gentle sweetness and such cultivated taste that everyone regretted it when she was later persuaded to shut herself away in a convent.

Captain Harney and his boat arrived on schedule, and the serious business of making our journey comfortable began under Mrs. Doty's kind supervision. The mess-basket was packed with good things of every description — ham and tongue, biscuit and plum-cake — to say nothing of the solid necessities: crackers, bread, and boiled pork. A lady, of course, was supposed to be far too fastidious to touch that last item even if she were starving in the woods.

We had hired three Canadian voyageurs to take charge of our tent, the mess-basket, and matters and things in general. Their work would be to cut wood for our fires, cook our meals, and lend a hand with whatever else was going on. A messenger had also been sent up to the Kakalin, the rapids twenty-one miles above us, to tell Wish-tay-yun — the most accomplished guide there is through the hard passages of that river — to be ready for us on a set day.
