Among so many painful events, there were sometimes some that came close to being ridiculous.

One evening, while we were sitting at tea, we were startled by the sound of guns firing off in the direction of the Wisconsin. Everyone jumped up and got ready, by pure instinct, to run for the garrison. Coming out of the house, we found the whole bluff and the meadow below it in an uproar: Indians running toward the noise with their guns and spears over their shoulders; squaws and children standing in front of their lodges, looking anxiously toward the strange, unexplained sounds; knots of French and half-breeds, like ourselves, hurrying to reach the bridge and get inside the pickets that had gone up so recently.

As one group of Indians rushed past us, a weapon that somebody was carrying carelessly struck one of our party on the side of the head. "Oh!" she shrieked, "I am killed! An Indian has tomahawked me!" — and the only thing that reassured her was discovering that she could still run as fast as the best of us.

When we reached the parade-ground inside the Fort, we could not help laughing at what a spectacle we made. Some of us had no hats or shawls. Others were clutching bundles of valuables snatched up at the last second. One still had her piece of bread-and-butter in her hand, not having had the presence of mind to put it down before she ran.

The alarm turned out to have come from a party of Winnebagoes from one of the Barribault villages. They were about to leave home for a while, and were going through the ceremony of burying the scalps that they and their fathers had taken.

Like military funerals among civilized nations, their ceremony ended on this occasion with several volleys fired over the grave of their trophies.
