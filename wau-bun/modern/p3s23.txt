The Indians, whatever the tribe, are extremely fond of telling stories and of listening to them, historical or invented. They have professional storytellers, as the Oriental nations do, and these men travel from village to village and collect an admiring, attentive audience, even though what they recite is well-worn and familiar.

That is how their traditions are kept and passed down whole from one generation to the next. Their knowledge of the geography of their country is wonderfully exact. I have watched an Indian sit in his lodge and draw a map in the ashes of the Northwestern States — not their statistics but their geography, the lakes, the rivers, the mountains — with the greatest accuracy, giving the relative distances in days' journeys without stopping to think, and even carrying his drawing and his explanations as far as Kentucky and Tennessee.

In biography they preserve not only the main events of a person's life but his face, his appearance, the way he carried himself, his manners, and any small trait or peculiarity that marked him out.

The women are fonder of fiction, and some of their stories mix humor and pathos in a strange way. I give the two that follow as samples. The Indian names in them are in the Ottawa, or "Courte-Oreilles," language, but the same tales are told in all the different tongues and dialects.

A great many peculiarities are attributed to this animal. He is said to be like the jackal in his habit of disturbing the graves of the dead, and the Indians have a superstitious dread of hearing him bark at night, believing that it foretells disaster and death. They say, too, that he was originally one uniform reddish-brown all over, and that his legs turned black in the way the story tells.

There was a chief of a certain village who had a beautiful daughter. He decided one time to give a feast and invite all the animals. When the invitation reached the red fox, he asked, "What are you going to have for supper?"

"Mee-dau-mee-nau-bo," was the answer. (This is a porridge made of parched corn, slightly cracked.)

The fox turned up his sharp little nose. "No, thank you," he said. "I can get plenty of that at home."

The messenger went back to the chief and reported how contemptuously the fox had refused.

"Go back to him," said the chief, "and tell him we are going to have a nice fresh body, and we will have it cooked in the most delicate manner possible."

Pleased at the prospect of a treat like that, the fox accepted the second invitation very heartily indeed.

The hour came, and he set off for the chief's lodge to attend the feast. The company were all ready for him, because they had made common cause with their friend who had been insulted. As the fox came in, the guest nearest the door rose from his place with great courtesy and begged the newcomer to be seated. At once the man next to him rose too, and insisted that the fox take his place instead, since it was nearer the fire — the seat of honor. Then the third, with a great many polite phrases, pressed him to change places as well, and so, with much ceremony and flourishing, he was passed along the circle, always working closer to the fire, where a huge cauldron stood with the good cheer still cooking in it. The fox was by no means unwilling to occupy the highest place in the gathering, and besides, he was anxious for a peep into the kettle, because he had his suspicions that he might be disappointed of the delicacies he had been counting on.

So, step by step, he was ushered nearer and nearer the great blazing fire, until with one deft push and shove he was hoisted into the boiling kettle.

His feet were dreadfully scalded, but he leaped out and ran home to his lodge, howling and crying with the pain. His grandmother, whom he lived with, as animals do, demanded an account of the whole affair. When he had faithfully told her every circumstance — for, unlike the civilized animals, it never occurred to him to tell his grandmother a story — she scolded him severely.

"You have committed two great faults," she said. "In the first place, you were very rude to the chief, who was kind enough to invite you, and by returning insult for civility you made enemies who were determined to punish you. In the second place, it was very unbecoming of you to be so forward about taking the place of honor. If you had been content to keep your seat modestly near the door, you would have escaped the misfortune that has come upon you."

None of this was much comfort to the poor fox, who went on whining and crying most piteously while his grandmother, her lecture finished, set about binding up his wounds. A little dancing is believed to add great power to any medicine or dressing, so once the dressing was on, the grandmother fell to dancing with all her might, round and round the lodge.

When she was nearly worn out, the fox said, "Grandmother, take off the bandages and see whether my legs are healed."

She did as he asked, but no — the burns were still raw. She danced and danced again. Now and then, as he grew impatient, she would take off the coverings to see what effect the remedies were having. Finally, toward morning, she looked, and sure enough, the burns were completely healed. "But, oh!" she cried, "your legs are as black as coal! They were so badly burned that they will never come back to their own color!"

The poor fox, who like many another brave was vain of his legs, fell into a fit of lamentation.

"Oh! my legs! My pretty red legs! What am I going to do? The young girls will all despise me. I shall never dare show myself among them again!"

He cried and sobbed until his grandmother, tired out by her exercise, fell asleep. By then he had settled on his plan of revenge.

He got up and stole softly out of the lodge, and, making his way quickly toward the chief's village, he turned his face toward the principal lodge and barked. When the people inside heard that sound in the stillness of the night, their hearts trembled. They knew it meant sorrow and trouble for one of their number.

Very soon afterward the chief's beautiful daughter fell sick, and she grew rapidly worse and worse in spite of medicines, charms, and dances. Finally she died. The fox had not meant to bring misfortune on the village in that shape, because he loved the chief's beautiful daughter, so he stayed in his lodge and mourned and fretted over her death.

Preparations were made for a magnificent funeral, but the dead girl's friends were in great perplexity. "If we bury her in the earth," they said, "the fox will come and disturb her remains. He has barked her to death, and he will be glad to come and finish his revenge."

They took counsel together and decided to hang her body high in a tree for a burying place. They reckoned the fox would go groping about in the ground and never lift his eyes to the branches over his head.

But the grandmother had been at the funeral, and she came back and told the fox everything that had been done.

"Now, my son," she said, "listen to me. Don't meddle with the remains of the chief's daughter. You have done mischief enough already. Leave her in peace."

As soon as the grandmother was asleep that night, the fox went out roaming. He soon found the place he was looking for, and came and sat under the tree where the young girl had been put. He gazed and gazed at her the whole night long, and she looked as beautiful as she had in life. But when day broke and the light let him see more clearly, he saw that decay was doing its work — that instead of a beautiful thing she was a loathsome one.

He went home sad and wretched and spent the whole day mourning in his lodge.

"Have you disturbed the remains of the chief's beautiful daughter?" was his grandmother's anxious question.

"No, grandmother" — and he said not another word.

So it went on for many days and nights. The fox always took care to leave his watch at the first light of dawn, because he knew her friends would suspect him and come early to see that all was well.

In time he noticed that the young girl looked less and less hideous in the morning light, and that little by little she was taking back the look she had worn in life, until at last her beauty and her look of health had entirely returned.

One day he said, "Grandmother, give me my pipe, so I can have a smoke."

"Ah!" she cried, "you are beginning to be comforted. You have never smoked since the death of the chief's beautiful daughter. Have you heard some good news?"

"Never you mind," he said. "Bring the pipe."

He sat down and smoked, and smoked. After a while he said, "Grandmother, sweep your lodge and put everything in order, because today you are going to have a visit from your daughter-in-law."

The grandmother did as she was told. She swept the lodge and arranged it with all the taste she had, and then the two of them sat down to wait for the visit.

"When you hear a sound at the door," said the fox, "you must give the greeting, and say, Come in."

When they had been sitting there for some time, the grandmother heard a faint rustling sound. She looked toward the door. To her surprise, the mat that usually hung there as a curtain was rolled up, and the door stood open.

"Peen-tee-geen n'dau-nis!" she cried.

Something like a very faint shadow seemed to glide in. Gradually it took on a more definite outline. As she looked and looked, she began to make out the form and the features of the chief's beautiful daughter, but it was a long time before she seemed like anything real, and took her place in the lodge like a creature of flesh and blood.

They kept the matter very quiet, because they would not for the world have had the bride's father or her friends learn what had happened. Before long, though, it began to be rumored about that the chief's beautiful daughter had come back to life and was living in the Red Fox's lodge. How it ever got out was a mystery, since the grandmother of course never spoke of it.

Be that as it may, the news caused great excitement in the village. "This must never be," they all said. "He barked her to death once, and who knows what he may do the next time?"

The father took a firm line at once. "The Red Fox is not worthy of my daughter," he said. "I had promised her to the Hart, the finest and most elegant of all the animals. Now that she has come back to life, I mean to keep my word."

So the friends all went in a body to the Red Fox's lodge. The bridegroom, the bride, and the grandmother put up all the resistance they could, but they were overpowered by numbers, and the Hart having conveniently waited outside where there was no danger, the chief's beautiful daughter was put on his back, and he ran off with her through the forest to carry her to his own home. When he reached his lodge door, though, he turned his head — and there was no bride in the place where he expected to see her. He had thought his load very light from the start, but he had put that down to her being a spirit come back from the dead. It had never entered his head that she had slipped off her seat at the very beginning and, in the middle of the uproar, had slid back unnoticed to the husband she had chosen.

One or two more attempts were made after that by her friends to get the young creature back, but they all failed. Then they said, "Let her stay where she is. It is true the Red Fox caused her death, but by his watching and his care he coaxed her back into life again, so she rightfully belongs to him." And so the Red Fox and his beautiful bride lived a long time together in great peace and happiness.
