The great bugbear of this road, Ma-zhee-gaw-gaw Swamp, was the next thing to be got through. We reached it about nine o'clock. It lay spread out in front of us, a vast stretch of marsh about half a mile wide and of endless length, partly under water, with black knobs sticking up here and there above the surface to give the animals an uncertain foothold as they crossed. Where there was no water there was instead a bed of black oozy mud that looked as though it might give way under a foot and let it sink at every step to who knows what depth.

This was what we now had to cross. All three of the gentlemen went ahead of me, each one hoping, as he said, to pick out the safest and firmest path for me to follow. First one and then another would call, "Here, madam, come this way!" "This is the best path, wifie, follow me." But often Charlie knew better than any of them, and chose a path on his own judgment that turned out to be the best of the lot.

On he went, picking his way so slowly and carefully — pausing on one little hillock, then on another, and now and then turning aside to avoid a patch of mud that looked more than usually suspicious — that the whole party got some distance ahead of me. When I raised my eyes, which I had been keeping pretty firmly on my horse's feet, I saw my husband on foot, struggling to lead his horse by the bridle out of an awkward spot it had got into; Mr. Wing and his great white floundering animal lying on their sides in the mud, the rider doing everything he could to get himself free of the stirrups; and Dr. Philleo standing a little way off from his horse, which was doing its best to get up out of a deep bog it had pitched itself into. It was a formidable sight! They all shouted at once, "Oh, don't come this way!"

"Believe me," I called back, "I have no intention of it. Charlie and I know better." And trusting to the sagacious creature, he picked his way carefully along and carried me safely past the whole dismounted company. I could not resist a little triumphant flourish of my whip as I looked back at them and watched them get into their saddles again.

We had been at this unpleasant business for three hours, and still we were not over the "Slough of Despond." At last we drew near the far edge of it. Here ran a deep stream, five or six feet across. The gentlemen dismounted as they came up to it and began debating what was to be done.

"Jump off, jump off, madam," cried Mr. Wing, and "Jump off, jump off," echoed Dr. Philleo. "We are just working out how to get you across."

"What do you think about it?" asked my husband.

"Charlie will show you," I said. "Come, Charlie." And as I lifted his bridle smartly, with a pat on his neck and an encouraging chirp, he cleared the stream as lightly as a deer and landed me safe on solid ground.

Poor Mr. Wing had come off worst of the party. The clumsy animal he rode seemed to be of the opinion that whenever it got into difficulty there was nothing to be done but lie down and submit to its fate, and its rider, not being especially light or agile, was generally underneath and half buried in the mire before he had quite decided on his course of action.

So it was a wise move on his part, when he got to the little stream, to plunge in and wade across, washing off as much as he could of the morning's adventures from himself and his horse; and the other gentlemen, having no alternative, decided to follow his example.
