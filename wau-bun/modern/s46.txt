Fort Dearborn at that time was made up of the same buildings it has now. They were of course in better repair then, though even so they were pretty run down. They had gone up in 1816 under the supervision of Captain Hezekiah Bradley, and there was a story going around that he cared so patriotically about the Government's interests that he made the soldiers whittle wooden pins instead of using spikes and nails to fasten the timbers together — and that he even called on the junior officers to help with the building alongside the soldiers, whose job it actually was. If that is true, the captain must have been under the impression — an understandable one in a man who had spent a long time on the frontier — that the Government thanks its servants for going overboard on economy.

The fort was closed in by high pickets, with bastions at every other corner. Large gates opened north and south, and there were small posterns here and there for the convenience of the people inside. The bank of the river running west, now covered over by the light-house buildings and shut in by docks, was then where the garrison kept its root-houses. Beyond the parade-ground, which stretched south of the pickets, were the company gardens, well stocked with currant-bushes and young fruit-trees.

The fort stood at what you would naturally take to be the mouth of the river. It wasn't. In those days the river made a turn there, swinging around the point of land the fort was built on and running south, joining the lake about half a mile further down. The fort buildings stood on the right bank; the left bank was a long spit of land running out from the northern shore, of which it was part. In 1833 the United States Engineers, sent to build a harbor at that spot, cut through this piece of the left bank and threw out the piers. After that the water flowed over the long tongue of land and kept eating into the southern bank, stealing many valuable acres from it — while the same action of that huge body of water was steadily building land up on the north side of the harbor.

Jean Baptiste Beaubien's house stood in this period between the gardens and the river-bank, and further south again there was a rickety tenement put up many years before by Mr. John Dean, the sutler of the post. Not long after Chicago began to grow, the lake gradually ate away the foundations of that building and it toppled over backward down the bank, where it lay for a long while — a sad sight.

On the north bank of the river, directly across from the fort, stood my husband's family home. It was a long, low building with a piazza running the length of the front, a row of four or five rooms. A broad green space was fenced in between it and the river, shaded by a line of Lombardy poplars. Two enormous cottonwood trees stood behind the house, and one of them is still there as an old landmark. A fine, well-tended garden ran off to the north, and around the place were the various buildings that belonged to the establishment: dairy, bake-house, a lodging-house for the Frenchmen, and stables.

A great stretch of sand-hills grown over with stunted cedars, pines, and dwarf willows lay between the house and the lake, and the lake at that time was no more than thirty rods away.

Going on from there along the north bank of the river, the first place you came to was the Agency House — "Cobweb Castle," as people had christened it during the long years a bachelor lived there, and the nickname stuck to it forever after. It stood where the southwest corner of Wolcott and N. Water Streets is now. Plenty of people will still remember it: a solid, compact little building of hewed and squared logs, with a center, two wings, and — strictly speaking — two tails, because once there was no more room to add on at the sides, the additions went on the back, wherever an empty spot could be found.

These appendages did not spoil the look of the place from the front. But when, in the course of the town's improvement, somebody maliciously ran a street directly along the rear of the building, the whole establishment with its comical little attachments became a permanent source of entertainment to everyone walking past. No matter. Plenty of pleasant, happy hours were spent under that odd-shaped roof, as a good many of Chicago's early settlers can tell you.

Grouped around the Agency House was a cluster of log buildings where the various people in Government employ attached to it lived — the blacksmith, the striker, the laborers. Most of them were Canadians or half-breeds, with the occasional stray Yankee to get everything moving by sheer activity and enterprise.

There was one more house on the north side of the river, built by a former resident named Miller, but he had gone off to the Rivière du Chemin, or Trail Creek, which was just then beginning to be called Michigan City. His house, which stood near the forks of the river, was empty at this time.

There was no house at all on the southern bank between the fort and "The Point," which is what the forks of the river were called then. The ground there was low wet prairie, barely walkable in the driest part of the summer and flatly impassable the rest of the year. A muddy little stream — a slew, as they call it in this country — wound around from about where the Tremont House stands now and emptied into the river at the foot of State Street. At the Point, on the south side, was a house Mark Beaubien had just finished. It was a showy white two-story building with bright-blue wooden shutters, and the whole little circle at Wolf Point admired it. A canoe ferry was kept there to carry people across the south branch of the river.

Facing down the river from the west there was, first, a small tavern kept by Mr. Wentworth, known to everyone as "Old Geese" — not because there was anything slow about him, but as a compliment to one of his own pet expressions. Near him stood two or three log cabins lived in by Robinson, the Pottowattamie chief, and some of his wife's relations. Billy Caldwell, the Sau-ga-nash, stayed here sometimes too, with his wife, who was a daughter of Nee-scot-nee-meg, one of the most famous chiefs of the nation. A little way off from these houses was a small square log building put up originally as a school-house, though it also served as a place of worship whenever a traveling minister turned up.

The Clybourn family had already settled near where they live now, on the North Branch, and they called their place New Virginia. Four miles up the South Branch stood an old building that had once been an object of great interest, because it was the scene of some stirring events during the troubles of 1812. It was known as Lee's Place, or Hardscrabble. A settler named Heacock was living there at this time.

Since the roads were bad most of the year, people generally traveled between the fort and the Point by boat, rowing up the river, or by canoe with somebody skilful at the paddle. Canoes were also how the people at the fort and the people at the Agency House kept up with each other.

There were two companies of soldiers in the garrison at this time, but one of the officers, Lieutenant Furman, had died the autumn before, and several of the others were away on furlough. With Major Fowle and Captain Scott both gone, the command fell to Lieutenant Hunter. Besides him there were Lieutenants Engle and Foster, the second of them unmarried. Dr. Finley, the post surgeon, was away as well, and Dr. Harmon, a gentleman from Vermont, was filling in for him.

My husband's mother, his two sisters, and his brother were living at the Agency House. The family home near the lake was occupied by J.N. Bailey, the postmaster.

A Mr. and Mrs. Forbes, who kept a school, lived in the Dean House. Gholson Kercheval ran a small trading business out of one of the log buildings at Wolf Point, and John S.C. Hogan managed the sutler's store in the garrison.

There was also a Mr. See, newly arrived in the country and living at the Point, who now and then held forth in the little school-house on a Sunday — less to the edification of his listeners than to the merciless slaughter of the King's English.

I think that list covers every white inhabitant of Chicago at a time less than fifty years ago. To many people who read these pages, all these particulars will no doubt seem dull. But to anyone who visits Chicago, and still more to anyone who comes to make a home there, it may be worth something to look back at how it started, to think about the almost magical change a handful of years has brought about, and from that past to guess at the extraordinary prosperity ahead of it.
