We now found ourselves in much more varied country than any we had travelled through so far. Gently swelling hills, lovely valleys, and bright sparkling streams were what the landscape was made of. But there was very little animal life about. Now and then a shout from whoever was leading the party — we travelled Indian file, as the custom is — would turn our attention to a herd of deer "loping," as the Westerners say, off through the forest, or the horses would be given an extra spur at the appearance of some small dark object far ahead of us on the trail. But the game always managed to disappear before we could get to it, and leaving the beaten track for a proper hunt was out of the question.

Soon after midday we came down a long sloping knoll, and a sudden bend brought us full in view of the beautiful sheet of water that the French call Gros-pied, the natives call Maunk-suck, and we called Big-foot, after the chief whose village looks down over it. Bold swelling hills pushed out into the clear blue expanse, or drew back a little to leave a green, level nook as a resting place for the home of man. Along the near shore ran a bright gravelly beach, with here and there a pure, sparkling little stream crossing it to join the larger body of water.

On rising ground at the foot of one of the bold bluffs in the middle distance stood a cluster of neat wigwams, which, with the gardens around them, were not the least pleasing feature of the picture.

A shout of delight burst out of the whole party without anybody meaning it to, when this charming landscape came into view. "It is like the Hudson, only not so bold — no, it is like the lake of the Forest Cantons in the picture of the Chapel of William Tell! What could anyone imagine more enchanting? Oh, if only our friends back East could enjoy it with us!"

We stopped a long while to admire it, then spurred on around the head of the lake, and were soon climbing the broad platform of ground on which the village of Maunk-suck, or Big-foot, stood.

The people, who had watched us coming from a long way off, were all gathered in front of their wigwams to greet us if we were friends, and to do whatever the occasion called for if we were not. It was the first time such a sight had ever presented itself to their wondering eyes. Their greetings were no less warm than we had expected. "Shaw-nee-aw-kee" and his mother — who was known all through the tribe by the touching name of "Our friend's wife" — were made most kindly welcome, and a lively talk began, of which I could follow only as much as was carried by gesture. So I amused myself by taking a careful survey of everything in front of me.

The chief was a big, raw-boned, ugly Indian, with a face swollen by drink and a sinister, unpleasant expression. He had a gay-colored handkerchief on his head, and was otherwise dressed in his best, as a compliment to the strangers.

This was the chief that Chambly — now called Shaw-bee-nay — along with Billy Caldwell and Robinson, was sent to by Dr. Wolcott, their Agent, during the Winnebago war of 1821, to do everything they could to keep him and his band from joining the hostile Indians. They managed it, with some difficulty, and by doing so they were doubtless the means of saving the lives of every settler living exposed out on the frontier.

Among the various groups of his people, nobody caught my attention as forcibly as one young man with a handsome face and a figure that was striking even where everyone was well-made and shapely. He too had a gay handkerchief on his head, a shirt of the brightest lemon-colored calico, silver ornaments in abundance, and — what made his outfit look most fanciful of all — one legging of blue and the other of bright scarlet. I was not ignorant of what that particular touch in his wardrobe meant: a heart suffering from the tender passion. The flute he carried in his hand confirmed it, while the joyous, animated expression on his face showed just as plainly that he was not a despairing lover.

I could imagine him just back from the hunt, loaded with game, which he had carried, as the custom is, into the lodge of the young lady and thrown down at her parents' feet with a careless, magnificent sort of air, as much as to say, "Here is some meat — a mere trifle, but it will give you an idea of what you could expect with me for a son-in-law." I had no doubt at all that the girl had stepped forward and gathered it up, as a sign that she accepted the offering and the man who brought it along with it. There was nothing in the looks or the manner of any of the young women standing around us to show which was the happy one, and though I peered anxiously into every face, I could not detect so much as a self-conscious blush. So I had to content myself with picking out the youngest and prettiest of the group, and going on weaving my romance to my own satisfaction.

The village stood inside a ring of hills so steep, with gorges so narrow and sheer, that it looked almost impossible to climb them even on horseback. How, then, could we hope to get a four-wheeled carriage up? That was the point my husband and the Pottowattamies were now discussing. There was no choice but to make the effort, taking the pass the villagers pointed out as the most practicable. Petaille went first, and I followed on my favorite, Jerry. It was a scramble of a kind that is not often taken on — almost straight up, through what seemed to be the dry bed of a torrent, now full of loose stones and hardly offering one secure foothold from the bottom to the summit. I clung to the mane, at times literally with both arms around Jerry's neck, and to the encouraging shouts and cheers of the people below, we finally got safely to the top, nearly out of breath, and sat there looking down to see how the next party would manage.

The horses had been taken out of the carriage, and the luggage inside it had been put on the shoulders of some of the young Indians to be toted up the steep. Ropes were now tied to the sides of the carriage, and a whole crowd of our red friends, led by our two Frenchmen, were put on to haul. Two or three more took their places behind it, to hold it and keep it from slipping backward. Then the labor began. Such pulling! Such shouting! Such clapping of hands from the spectators of both sexes! Such a stentorian word of command or encouragement from the bourgeois! Every so often there would be a slight halt, a wavering, as if carriage and men were all about to go tumbling backward into the plain below — but no, they would recover themselves, and after incredible efforts they too arrived safely on the tableland above. In time everything was landed up there, and when our friends had been paid to their satisfaction, the goods and chattels were collected, the wagon repacked, and we set off for our camp at Turtle Creek.

What with the effort and excitement of that laborious climb, and the increasing heat of the sun, that afternoon's ride was more uncomfortable than anything we had felt before. We were truly glad when our guide's whoop, and the sight of a few scattered lodges, told us we had reached our camping ground. We picked a beautiful, secluded spot beside a clear, sparkling stream, and when we had dismounted and seen that our horses were made comfortable, my husband gave his men their instructions and then led me off to a private place where I could lay aside my hat and my mask and bathe my flushed face and aching head in the cool, refreshing water. I had never felt anything so welcome, so delicious. I sat down and leaned my head against one of the tall trees overhanging the stream, and was nearly dreaming when I was called to come and take our evening meal.

The Indians had brought us a present of some fine brook trout, which our Frenchmen had cooked in the most tempting way possible, and by the time the bright moon came up and we were ready for bed, headache and fatigue had both disappeared.

One of the most charming things about travelling this way is the joyous, noisy life of the forest at first light, when all the feathered tribe comes out to pay its cheerful respects to the opening day.
