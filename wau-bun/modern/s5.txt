The point of our early walk was to see the Mission-house and school that the Presbyterian Board of Missions had established here a few years before. It mattered a great deal to Mr. and Mrs. Stuart, and the state it was in just then, along with everything it promised to become, was enough to gladden any philanthropic heart. They had lived on the island for years and had watched it change, under God's blessing on Christian effort, from a worldly, dissipated place into one where you could almost say religion was every man's business. The mission was the beloved child of the handful of Protestant families clustered around it, and their common center of interest. Between the drive and good management of Mr. and Mrs. Ferry and the encouragement of the congregation, the school had a fine reputation, and it was a pleasure to see what a little schooling and religious training could do — how it took the mischief and trickiness out of the half-breed children and woke up the flat, stolid patience of the full-blood Indian ones.

These were the golden days of Mackinac. It was the headquarters of the American Fur Company and the entrepôt of the entire Northwest. Every scrap of the trade — supplies and goods going one way, furs and the products of the Indian country coming the other — passed through the parent house here or through its many outposts, which were strung along Lakes Superior and Michigan, down the Mississippi, and into country still more remote than that.

Almost everybody knows that with the exception of the Miamis and the Wyandots, every tribe had held to a firm alliance with the British Crown from the day the old French possessions were handed over to it. American independence did not change the policy of the tribes, and our own Government never managed to win their friendship or to buy it. Great Britain, it must be said, bid high to keep them. Every year the leading men of the Chippewas, Ottawas, Pottowattamies, Menomonees, Winnebagoes, Sauks and Foxes — and of tribes from even farther off — traveled from their distant homes to Fort Malden in Upper Canada to collect their annual presents from their Great Father across the water. Keeping them on the payroll that way was a masterstroke, and in the last war between the two countries it let the people who practised it do terrible damage with allies like these.

The presents were worth having: blankets, broadcloth and strouding, calicoes, guns, kettles, traps, silver-work — arm-bands, bracelets, brooches, ear-bobs — looking-glasses, combs, and every other sort of trinket, and they were handed out with no stinginess at all.

The Fur Company's magazines and storehouses at Mackinac were where all the upper tribes came to sell what they had and buy what they needed, including everything on that list and also ammunition, which — along with money and liquor — their British friends very commendably left out of their own gifts.

Besides furs of every kind, often extremely valuable ones — beaver, otter, marten, mink, silver-gray and red fox, wolf, bear and wild-cat, musk-rat and smoked deer-skins — the Indians brought maple sugar by the quantity, considerable amounts of Indian corn and petit-blé, beans, and the folles avoines, the wild rice. The women added their own goods to the pile: moccasins, hunting pouches, mococks — little birch-bark boxes embroidered with porcupine quills and packed with maple sugar — mats woven neatly and made to last, and toy models of Indian cradles, snowshoes, canoes, and the rest of it.

In those days it was nothing unusual to see a hundred canoes or more coming in on the island at once, loaded with goods for trade. Add to that the squadrons of big Mackinac boats arriving constantly from the outposts with the furs, peltries and buffalo robes the distant traders had collected, and you begin to get some idea of how large the American Fur Company's operation was, how central its position, and how vast the circle of human beings tied to it either directly or at a distance.

It is no wonder that a philanthropic mind, looking out over these uncultivated heathen races, reached forward to a time when — through the tireless devotion of the white man's energies and an unending sacrifice of self and fortune — his red brethren might climb the scale of civilization, and Education and Christianity would walk hand in hand and make "the wilderness blossom as the rose."

Not one of those good souls, rejoicing over the success of their work at Mackinac, had any idea what was coming. In less than twenty-five years there would be nothing left of all those numerous tribes but a few scattered bands, squalid, degraded, with hardly a trace remaining of the character they once had. Their lands would be coaxed or torn away from them. The graves of their fathers would be turned up by the plough. They themselves would be driven farther and farther toward the setting sun, until they were literally begrudged a place on the face of the earth.

Our visit to the Mission school was short, because the Henry Clay was leaving at two o'clock and we still had to see what we could of the village and the country around it, and then dine with Mr. Mitchell, an old friend of my husband's. As we strolled along the white gravel road, one after another of the old inhabitants' houses was pointed out to me. There was the home of Madame Laframboise, an Ottawa woman whose husband had taught her to read and write, and who had spent the rest of her life putting what she had learned to work for the young people of her own nation. She kept a class of pupils at her house every day and gave them lessons in those subjects and in the principles of the Roman Catholic religion, to which she was deeply devoted. She was a woman of tremendous energy and enterprise, tall and commanding, with the most dignified bearing you can imagine. After her husband was killed — murdered away at his trading post by a Winnebago named White Ox — she took to visiting the posts herself, overseeing the clerks and the engagés, and satisfying herself that the business was being run regularly and at a profit.

The Agency-house, with its unusual luxuries of a piazza and gardens, sat at the foot of the hill the fort was built on. It was a lovely spot, in spite of the stunted, dwarfish look of everything anyone tried to cultivate in that cold northern latitude.

The collection of rickety, primitive-looking buildings the Fur Company's officials worked out of reflected no great credit on the architectural skill of my husband, who told me he had supervised putting them up when he was hardly more than a boy.

Besides those there were the houses of the Dousmans, the Abbotts, the Biddles, the Drews and the Lashleys, stretching away along the base of that beautiful hill, which was crowned by the white walls and buildings of the fort. The climb to it was so steep that on the sheer face nearest the beach they had built staircases to get up from below.
