The ground and the trees and the brush were all far too soaked with the heavy rain that had fallen for us to consider camping, so we made arrangements to stow ourselves in our little saloon for the night. Getting a fire lit was a difficult business, but in a wild, undisturbed place there are always some scraps of dry branch and some tufts of grass down in the underbrush that the rain has not reached, and with the help of the spunk, or light-wood, travelers always carry plenty of, we finally had a comforting fire burning bright.

After that chilly, tedious day it was a pleasure to gather around it, to sit on the ends of the burning logs and watch the Frenchmen putting our supper together: the kettle nestled into a little nook of glowing coals; slices of ham browning and crisping on the forked sticks, the broches, that the voyageurs cut so deftly and set up around the burning brands; the savory mess of pork and onions hissing in the frying pan, which is always a temptation to a hungry Frenchman. It really does take a wet, cold day of travel taken nearly on an empty stomach, the way ours had been, to let a person enjoy that most sociable of meals — a supper — to the very fullest.

The bright sun went down through those brilliant banks of cloud you only see in our Western skies, promising a fine day tomorrow, and with that comforting assurance we were glad to take leave of it, and shortly afterward of each other.

We had barely gotten up the next morning at the bourgeois's call when we found we had company: a tall, well-built, fine-looking young mitiff — a half-breed — with two or three Indians. Their greetings to their "father" and their new "mother" were loud and joyful. They were the first Winnebagoes I had ever seen, and they were decidedly not the finest specimens of their tribe. The mitiff, one more shoot off the wide-spreading Grignon tree, was carrying an invitation to us from Judge Law, who was camped a few miles above with one or two Green Bay friends, to come and take breakfast with him in his tent. We had never dreamed of finding white neighbors out here, but our being nearby could hardly stay a secret from them as long as there was a single Indian in the neighborhood. So, waiting only for the soldiers to finish their breakfast, we pushed on for the Butte des Morts — or, as Mrs. A insisted on calling it to the last, Betty More's.

The Judge's white tent shone in the morning sun as we came up on the little rise it stood on. The river was full of canoes, most of them paddled by women. There were plenty of Indians on the banks, all of them carrying guns and hunting gear, because the air in every direction was full of flocks of teal, which at that season are both abundant and delicious. The huge fields of wild rice here and in the little lake below make this their favorite ground in the autumn months. What that rich food does to the birds is to make the flesh so fat, so white and so tender that a young sportsman is always warned to shoot only at the ones flying very low — if you hit them high up, they are bruised to pieces by the fall and are not fit to eat.

We were hemmed in by a little fleet of canoes that closed around us, the women chattering and laughing and pushing forward their small wooden bowls of fresh cranberries as a welcome for me.

I amused myself throwing crackers to them. Some landed in the canoes and some in the water, and then what scrambling and shouting there was — hands and paddles both pressed into service, and a loud triumph from whichever woman got to a floating one first.

Among the Indians Shaw-nee-aw-kee was now shaking hands with, all of them apparently old friends, were a great many fine, straight, well-made men, every one of them built to endure the fatigue and hardship their way of life demands. One of them was describing with a lot of gesturing how much game there was in the neighborhood, and he was clearly delighted to be handed a quantity of ammunition, with which he left immediately to make his boasting good.

After a short walk we reached the tent, where I was introduced to Judge Law and to a pleasant little gray-haired French gentleman named Porlier. Several voyageurs and half-breeds were nearby, the first group hard at work, the second mostly lounging and doing whatever they had to do with a sort of listless indifference.

The contrast between the all-alive manner of the one and the apathy of the other was striking.

After a short conversation among the party, breakfast was announced, and we went into the tent and took our seats on the ground around the Indian mat that served as a table.

The place of honor at the head of the table went to me, of course, so I could look around the whole circle of the company and also enjoy a fine view out the open door of the tent and observe everything going on at the side table outside. Judge Doty sat across from me with his back to the opening, and the other gentlemen to either side. Our waiter was the tall mitiff who had brought the morning's message. He was still in the same outfit — calico shirt, brightly colored sash around his waist, and on his head a straw hat banded with black ostrich feathers, which is the usual dress of his class.

The tin cups that were to hold our coffee were set around, then breakfast plates of the same metal with knives and forks, and then the food, the most conspicuous item being a large tin pan of boiled ducks.

The Judge, wanting to demonstrate, I suppose, that even in this vast wilderness a certain fastidiousness had not been left behind, picked up the plate that had been put in front of him, saw something stuck to it that did not entirely please him, and handed it back over his shoulder to Grignon with a request that it be wiped carefully. Grignon obliged by pulling a black silk barcelona handkerchief out of his shirt front, where it had been tucked away snugly against any occasion that might arise, giving the tin a furious polishing, and handing it back. The Judge looked at it with a smile of approval and glanced around the table as if to say, "You see how I choose to have things done," and applied himself to his breakfast.

In those days the trail to Fort Winnebago ran from the shore opposite Butte des Morts, through Ma-zhee-gaw-gaw swamp and past Green Lake, and it was lucky for the Judge that his horses were standing ready for him to mount and ride as soon after breakfast as possible. Otherwise I am afraid the story I would have been tempted to tell would have made his whole day's ride an uncomfortable one.

We had barely finished breakfast when our hunter, the one who got the ammunition, came back with about fifty fine ducks he had shot in a little over an hour. From then until the end of our journey we were never short of those excellent birds.
