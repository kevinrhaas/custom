When one of their people dies, the news is the signal for a general mourning and lamentation. In some cases, I am sorry to say, it is also the excuse for a general carouse — whenever the means and the equipment for one can be found.

The relatives weep and howl with grief, and the friends and acquaintances keep them company out of sympathy. A few of them are sent off to call on their Father, to tell him what has happened and to ask him for some presents to help them, as they put it, "dry up their tears."

We had a visit of that kind one morning, not long after the payment was over.

The man whose death they had come about was a drunken little Indian whom the French people around called "Old Boilvin," because he looked like an Indian Agent of that name at Prairie du Chien. "He had been fishing," they said, "on the shores of one of the little lakes near the Portage, and, having taken a little too much 'whiskee,' had fallen into the water and been drowned." Nothing of him had been found but his blanket on the bank, so there could be no funeral ceremonies — but his friends were ready to put on a great lamentation for him.

Their Father gave them tobacco, knives, calico, and looking-glasses, in proportion to what he judged their reasonable grief might be at the loss of such a worthless vagabond, and off they went.

In spite of the strict rules on the subject, there was no difficulty at all in getting a keg of whiskey from one of the traders who were still around. Armed with that and the rest of their treasures, they gathered at an agreed spot not far from the scene of the disaster, sat down with the keg in the middle of them, and set to work at their affliction. The more they drank, the louder their grief got, and the faster the tears ran.

In the middle of all this, a little figure came toward them — bent and staggering, covered in mud and completely disheveled, his face full of wonder and sympathy — and started in: "Why? What? What? Who's dead?"

"Who's dead?" they repeated, looking up in astonishment. "Why, you're dead! You were drowned in Swan Lake! Didn't we find your blanket there? Come on, sit down and help us mourn."

The old man did not wait to be asked twice. He took a seat and cried and drank along with the rest of them, weeping and lamenting as bitterly as anyone there, and the strange scene went on as long as any of them still had the power to form words, or there was any whiskey left.
