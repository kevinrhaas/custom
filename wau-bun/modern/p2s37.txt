Four years had gone by since little Nelly was captured. She had a naturally warm and affectionate heart, so the boundless tenderness of the people she lived among had called out the same feeling in her. She loved and revered the chief and his mother, and she had learned their language and their customs so completely that she had all but forgotten her own.

She had become so much one of the tribe that the memory of her home and her family had nearly faded out — all of it except her mother. Her mother she had loved with all the strength of feeling that belonged to her warm, ardent character, and her heart still clung to her with a fondness that no amount of time or change could destroy.

Then came the peace of 1783 between Great Britain and the United States. A general pacification of the Indian tribes followed from it, and fresh hope rose again in Mr. and Mrs. Lytle.

They moved with their family to Fort Niagara; the Great Council-Fire of the Senecas was close by, on the American side. Colonel Johnson readily undertook a fresh negotiation with the chief, but to make every chance of success certain, he again went in person to the village of the Big White Man.

His visit could not have been better timed. He arrived among them during the Feast of the Green Corn. That observance resembles the Jewish Feast of Tabernacles so strikingly that it, along with other customs, has led many people to believe the Indian nations are descended from the lost ten tribes of Israel. It made the time a season of general joy and festivity. Every other occupation was set aside to make room for socializing out in the open air or in arbors built out of green tree branches. Everyone turned out in his gala dress. The little adopted child's outfit was a petticoat of blue broadcloth bordered with gay-colored ribbons, and over it a sack, or upper garment, of black silk trimmed with three rows of silver brooches — the middle row, running from her throat to the hem, of large brooches, and the rows from the shoulders down of brooches no bigger than a shilling-piece, set as close together as they would go. Around her neck were countless strings of white and purple wampum, an Indian ornament made from the inner surface of the muscle-shell. Her hair was clubbed behind and loaded with beads of every color. Scarlet cloth leggings and deerskin moccasins embroidered with porcupine quills completed the costume.

Colonel Johnson was received with all the consideration owed to his position, and to the long friendship that had existed between him and the tribe.

Seeing that the good cheer of the festival had warmed and opened everyone's heart, he used an interview with the chief to dwell on the parental love that had made his little sister's father and mother give up their friends and their home and come hundreds of miles, on the single hope of now and then being able to look at her and hold her. The chief's heart softened as he listened, and he was brought to promise that he would come to the Grand Council soon to be held at Fort Niagara, on the British side of the river, and would bring his little sister with him.

He exacted a promise of his own in return: not only that no effort would be made to reclaim the child, but that no proposal to part with her would even be put to him.

The time finally came when little Nelly, her heart bounding with joy, was put on horseback to ride with her Indian brother to the Great Council of the Senecas. She had promised him that she would never leave him without his permission, and he relied on that word of hers completely.

As band after band of chiefs and warriors came in to meet their Father, the agent, at the council-fire, how the anxious hearts of the parents beat, hope one moment and fear the next! The officers of the fort had kindly given them quarters for the time being, and the ladies, whose sympathies were thoroughly stirred, had gone with the mother to the council ground and joined her in the long watch for the first sight of the band from the Alleghany River.

At last they were made out, coming up out of the forest on the opposite side, the American side. The commanding officer sent boats across to bring over the chief and his party. The father and mother, with all the officers and ladies around them, stood on the grassy bank waiting for them to come in. They had seen at a glance that the little captive was with them.

As he was about to step into the boat, the chief said to some of his young men, "Stand here with the horses and wait until I come back."

He was told that the horses would be ferried across and taken care of.

"No," he said, "let them wait."

He held his darling by the hand the whole way across — until the river was crossed, until the boat touched the bank, until the child sprang forward into the arms of the mother she had been kept from for so long.

When the chief saw that outburst of affection, he could hold out no longer.

"She shall go," he said. "The mother must have her child again. I will go back alone."

With one silent gesture of farewell he turned and stepped aboard the boat. No argument and no pleading could get him to stay for the council. Once he had reached the other side of the Niagara he mounted his horse, and he and his young men were soon lost in the depths of the forest.

After a stay of a few weeks at Niagara, Mr. Lytle began to fear that the Big White Man's resolve would give way and that steps would be taken to deprive him of his child a second time. So he made up his mind to move again. He took the first chance to cross Lake Erie with his family and settled near Detroit, where he went on living from then on.

Little Nelly never saw her friend the chief again, but she never forgot him. To the day she died she remembered her brother the Big White Man, and her friends and playmates among the Senecas, with tenderness and gratitude.
