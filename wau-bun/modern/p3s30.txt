The danger had come so close now that my husband decided to send his family to Fort Howard, which was believed to be well out of the enemy's range. I pleaded to be allowed to stay, and it did no good — he was firm.

"I must not leave my post," he said, "while there's any danger. My leaving might be the signal for the Winnebagoes to ally themselves with the Sauks at once. I'm certain that as long as I'm here my presence acts as a restraint on them. You want to stay and share the danger with me. If you did, it would expose us both to certain destruction in case of an attack. With the help of my friends in both tribes I could hope to save my own life if I were alone; but surrounded by my family, that would be impossible — we should all fall victims together. My plain duty is to send you somewhere safe."

A chance to do it came soon. Paquette, the Interpreter, who was also an agent of the American Fur Company, had occasion to send a boatload of furs to Green Bay, on their way to Mackinac. Mr. Kinzie had the boat fitted out as comfortably as an open boat of that kind can be — a tent-cloth stretched over a framework of hoop-poles down the middle and lined with a dark-green blanket — put an abundant store of provisions and other comforts on board, and committed us to the joint care of my brother Arthur and our faithful blacksmith, Mâtâ.

Mâtâ was a tall, gaunt Frenchman with a freckled face, a great mass of crisp sandy hair, and an incurable determination to speak English. His command of the language was somewhat limited, and he made a burlesque of it by adding an s to nearly every word and delivering each phrase with a jerk.

"Davids," he used to say to the little yellow fiddler after an evening's frolic at the Interpreter's, "Davids, clear away the tables and the glasses, and play fishes-hornspikes." He was a kind, affectionate creature, and there was no limit at all to his devotion to "Monsieur Johns" and "Madame Johns."

Besides these two protectors, three trustworthy Indians, the chief of them called Old Smoker, were engaged to escort our party. The boat's crew were all French engagés in the Fur Company's service — six gay-hearted, merry fellows who lightened their work with their pipes and their songs, and who would have taken it as a great compliment if the ladies listening had joined in the singing. Our hearts, alas, were too heavy by then to share in their enjoyment.

The Fourth of July, the day we left home, was a gloomy one indeed, both for those going and for the one left behind. Who knew whether we would ever meet again? Some of our circle had been through Indian warfare before, and what they had seen justified the saddest forebodings. There wasn't even the consolation of knowing for certain that this step would make us safe. The Sauks might possibly be on the other side of us, and the route we were taking might perhaps — though it wasn't likely — carry us right into the middle of them. So it's no wonder our leave-taking was a solemn one, a parting that everybody felt might be for this world.

Not everybody, though. The gay, cheerful Frenchmen laughed and sang and cracked their jokes, and "assured Monsieur John that they would take Madame John and Madame Alum safe to the Bay, in spite of Sauks or wind or weather."

So we set off on our trip. The Fort stayed in sight for many miles, as the course of the river carried us now nearer its walls and now farther from them, and it wasn't until nearly midday that we caught our last glimpse of home.

At the voyageurs' noon meal, or pipe, an alarming discovery was made: no bread had been put on board for the crew. Nobody could tell how the oversight had happened. One man was certain a large quantity had been brought from the garrison bakery for their use that very morning; another had actually seen the sacks of loaves standing in Paquette's kitchen. However it was, there we were, many miles out, with no provisions for the six Frenchmen except some salt pork, a few beans, and some onions. There was a consultation over the emergency. Should they go back to the Portage for supplies? The same danger that had made their departure necessary was still there, and they had been ordered to make all possible speed. On examination, we found that the store of bread and crackers laid in for our own party was far more than we could possibly need, and we thought there would be enough to allow the Frenchmen rations until we reached Powell's, at the Butte des Morts, the day after next, where we would certainly be able to get a fresh supply.

That decided, we went on with our trip, always in complete silence, because a song or a loud laugh was now strictly forbidden until we had passed the last edge of the country where the enemy might possibly be. We had been warned beforehand that a certain point, where the low marshy meadows the river had been running through rise into firmer, higher ground, was the border of the Menomonee territory, and the spot where the Sauks would most likely be met with if they had fled north of the Wisconsin toward the Chippewa country.
