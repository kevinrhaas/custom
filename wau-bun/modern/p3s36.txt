When Lieutenant Hunter arrived, having got leave of absence so that he could escort us, everything was quickly put in order for our return to Fort Winnebago. There was no Mackinac boat to be had, so we took a Durham boat instead. That kind is longer and shallower, with no way of rigging an awning or any other shelter over the middle, but its size suited our party better — besides our own family it included Lieutenant and Mrs. Hunter, the wife of another officer now stationed at Fort Winnebago, and our cousin, Miss Forsyth. We resigned ourselves, as you would expect, to fairly tight quarters.

Our crew was part Frenchmen and part soldiers, and once everything was ready we set off on a fine bright morning in the last part of July.

Our second day of rowing and poling by turns brought us to the Grande Chûte early in the afternoon.

The custom there is to get out at the foot of the rapids, climb the high bank, and walk around the fall while the men haul the boat up through the foaming water.

Most of our party had already stepped ashore when one of the other ladies and I were struck by a sudden idea.

"Let's stay in the boat," we said, "and be pulled up the Chûte." The rest of the company went on, and we sat and watched the men getting ready with great interest. They were soon over the side and into the water, and, with a strong rope tied to the bow, they all put their weight into pulling as they marched slowly along with their heavy load. The cargo — only our trunks and stores, nothing of any great weight — had not been taken out.

Up we went, now and then getting a tremendous bump off a hidden rock, and often splashed by a burst of foam as the waves roared and boiled around us.

The men kept as close as they could to the high, steep bank, where the water ran smoothest. At the head of the cordel was a cheerful simpleton of a Frenchman who kept turning his head to grin with delight at how plainly we were enjoying ourselves.

And we were in high spirits. "Isn't this wonderful?" one of us cried. "I only wish — "

Whatever the wish was, it was cut off by a shout and a crash. "Look out, Robineau! Mind where you're taking the boat!" came the cry, but too late. Paying more attention to the ladies than to his job, the leader had steered us straight into a sharp, jutting tree that hung out from the bank. The first pull ripped the side out of the boat, which immediately began to fill.

My companion and I jumped for the nearest rocks that showed above the foam. Our screams and the men's shouts brought Lieutenant Hunter and some Indians who were up on the bank plunging down to get us. They carried us to land in their arms, while the men worked hard at fishing up the contents of the boat, thoroughly soaked by now.

We scrambled up the high bank in a wretched state, to join in the general mourning over what the accident had probably cost us.

"Oh, my husband's new uniform!" cried one. "Oh, the miniatures at the bottom of my trunk!" sighed another. And "Oh, the silk dresses, and the ribbons, and the finery!" made up the general chorus.

Nobody gave a thought to the provisions, though on our way to shore we had watched the barrel of bread and the tub of ice — which Lieutenant Hunter had thoughtfully brought along for us to eat and drink — go sailing off on the dancing waves. Among the boxes brought to land and hauled up the steep bank was one holding several loaves of sugar and some packages of tea, which I had bought from the sutler at the post as our winter's supply. The young Indian who was carrying it set it down, and soon pointed out to me a thick white stream oozing out of the corners. I made signs for him to taste it. He dipped a finger in, and when he realized what it was he shouted to his companions in delight. Then I pointed at his hatchet and motioned him to open the box. He did not need to be asked twice — it was in pieces in no time.

Then I beckoned over all the rest of the youngsters, who were standing about full of wonder, and such a scrambling and shouting of delight followed that it put every one of us, and the boys most of all, into fits of laughter. Bowls, dippers, hands, anything at all that would hold even the smallest amount, were pressed into service. The squaws were the busiest of the lot. Those who had nothing better took the sturdiest scraps of the blue paper the sugar had been wrapped in, and in a moment nothing was left but the wet yellow bundles of tea and the splinters of the box that had held it.

By this time fires had been made, and before long the things out of the trunks were spread over every shrub and bush in sight. Luckily the box with the new uniform in it had been stacked high above the others in the middle of the boat and had taken very little damage. But the wardrobes in general were in a sad state.

There was not a white article left. Everything was mottled — blue, green, red and black running together in streaks, and dripping from the ends and corners.

To make things worse, the rain began to fall, as rain likes to do at the worst possible moment, and soon the half-dried clothes had to be gathered out of the smoke and bundled away in a thoroughly discouraging condition.

The tent was put up, wet as it was, and the blankets, wrung out and partly dried, were spread on the ground for us to sleep on.

A Hamburg cheese that had been part of my stores was voted to me for a pillow, and after a supper whose best feature was a piece of one of the wet loaves that had stayed in a barrel wedged too tight to float away, we went off to our rest.
