"Anybody who likes may take my place in the carriage," our mother said. "I have travelled on horseback so many years that I find any other way of getting about too tiring."

And so, in spite of her sixty years, she got up onto sister Margaret's pacer with the agility of a girl of sixteen.

Lieutenant Foster had left us early in the morning, feeling he had to rejoin his command. Now, having seen us ready to start with a clear sky overhead and everything right and tight for the trip, our friend the Sau-ga-nash said goodbye and turned back toward Chicago.

We went on through a lovely country of alternating glade and forest until we came to the Fox River. The current ran clear and rippling along, and as we came down the steep bank to the water, the question that occurs to any traveler in unfamiliar country came up: is it fordable?

Petaille, who knew the ground, had still not appeared. Lecuyer knew nothing at all about it. The troops had obviously gone ahead of us by this very trail. True enough — but they were on horseback. The problem was whether we could get the carriage through. It should be remembered that the doubt was not about how deep the water was, but about how hard the bottom of the stream was.

It was agreed that two or three of us on horseback should make the trial first. My mother, Lecuyer and I went carefully across to the far bank, each of us picking a different place to come out of the water so as to find the firmest spot. The bottom was hard and firm until we got near the shore, and then it gave a little. But with one more step, each of us was on dry ground.

"Est-il beau?" called my husband, who was driving.

"Oui, monsieur."

"Yes, John, come across just here, it is perfectly good."

"No, no — go a little farther down. See the white gravel just there — it will be firmer still over there."

Those were the contradictory directions he got. He took the second one, and when he was one step short of the bank, both horses went down until not much more than their backs was showing.

The white gravel turned out to be a bed of treacherous yellow clay, which had gleamed up through the water and produced that unfortunate illusion.

The horses were nearly suffocating in the mud and water, and they struggled frantically, making desperate efforts to get themselves free of the harness. My husband sprang out onto the pole. "Somebody give me a knife!" he cried. I was back in the water in a moment, and going as close as I dared, I handed him mine out of the scabbard around my neck.

"Whatever you do, don't cut the traces," his mother called.

He cut through some of the side-straps. Then, just as he reached the end of the pole and was stretching forward to separate the head-couplings, one of the horses gave a furious plunge, which made the other one rear and throw itself almost over backward. My husband was between them. For a moment we thought he was gone, trampled down by the panicking animals — but he soon showed himself again, so covered in mud and water he was hard to make out. Harry, who was near him, now sprang forward onto the pole with the agility of a cat, and in an instant, with the sharp jack-knife he had ready, cut the straps that held their heads.

By this point the horses were lying afloat on the water, one of them apparently dead and the other seemingly gasping out his last breath. But the moment they realized their heads were free, they both made another furious effort to get loose from the pole, which was still attached to them by the neck-strap.

That failed, so they tried something else. With a few well-judged twists and turns they managed to wrench the pole apart, and finally carried it off in triumph back across the river and up the bank, where they stood waiting to see what the next steps would be.

Here was a predicament. A few hours earlier we had thought ourselves badly enough off because some of our horses were missing. Now something much worse had happened. The wagon was in the river, the harness was cut to pieces and — worse — had been carried off in the most independent manner by Tom and his companion, the pole was twisted into fragments, and there was not so much as a stick on our side of the river to replace it with.

At that moment a whoop from the opposite bank, answered by two or three hearty ones from our own party, announced that Petaille Grignon was back. He dismounted and took charge of the horses, which were resting after their exertions under a shady tree, and by this time Lecuyer had crossed the river and joined him in bringing the delinquents back.

Meanwhile we had been doing our best to look after our sister Margaret. She and her little son Edwin had been in the wagon when it went down, and getting them out and bringing them ashore on horseback had been no easy job. The agitation and excitement threw her into a fit of the ague, and she now lay blue and shaking in the long grass of the little prairie that ran along the bank. The tent had been packed at the back of the wagon and was far too soaked with mud and water to be used as shelter — all we could do was spread it out in the sun to dry. We opened an umbrella over our poor sister's head, and then began a discussion of ways and means to repair the damage. The first thing was to cut a new pole for the wagon, and for that the master and the men had to recross the river and choose an iron-tree out of the forest.

Then there was the harness. With sensible foresight, a little box had been stowed under the seat of the wagon containing an awl, waxed ends, and various other small conveniences exactly suited to an emergency like this one.

It went question and answer, like Cock Robin:

"Who can mend the harness?"

"I can. I learned to make shoes when I was a young girl, as an accomplishment, and I can certainly now, as a matter of usefulness and duty, put all those wet, dirty pieces of leather back together."

So we all sat down on the grass, in the shade of the only two umbrellas we could muster.

I stitched away diligently, and blistered my hands rather badly in the process, I have to admit.

A suitable young tree had been brought in, and the hatchets — which nobody ever travels in the woods without — were busy shaping it, when a peculiar hissing noise was heard and instantly the cry went up: "Un serpent sonnette! A rattlesnake!"

Everybody sprang to their feet, even the poor shaking invalid, just in time to see the creature glide past within three inches of my mother's feet, while the men set about the spot it had left with whips, missiles, and anything else that would add to the commotion.

This little incident turned out to be an excellent cure for the ague. One excitement drives out another, and applying that (on the homoeopathic principle), sister Margaret improved so much that by the time all the damage had been repaired, she was ready to take her place in the cavalcade, as bright and cheerful as the rest of us.
