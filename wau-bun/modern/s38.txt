The next day the sun came up clear and bright. We were rested and full of energy, and we looked forward to getting under way again, sure that nothing more would go wrong on the road. Mr. Hamilton generously offered to ride with us as far as his nearest neighbor's — a trifling twenty-five miles. From Kellogg's on to Ogie's Ferry on the Rock River, he said, the road was so well traveled that there was no danger of our losing our way a second time.

The miner who owned the wife and baby — and who was, on that account, a bit more civilized than the rest of them — took leave of us by "wishing us well out of the country, and that we might never have occasion to return to it!"

"I pity a body," he said, "when I see them making such an awful mistake as to come out this way, for comfort never touched this Western country."

Mr. Hamilton was as pleasant a companion as he had been the day before, and an absolutely reckless rider. He kept up such a gallop that if I had not traded my pony for the fine, noble Jerry, I would have been in real danger of getting left behind.

Well mounted as we all were, he sometimes came close to leaving us behind altogether. We were among the branches of the Pickatonick now, and the country had stopped being prairie and turned rough and broken. We went dashing along, down ravines one minute and through narrow passes the next, and as I came through behind the others I left pieces of my veil hanging on the branches that stuck out and tangled together overhead. Once my hat got caught, and if my husband had not jumped to my rescue I would have shared Absalom's fate — Jerry was so set on holding his place in the race that he would very likely have done exactly what the mule did under that unfortunate prince.

We never once halted on the road, and since we kept up the same pace until three in the afternoon, there is no question that by the time we got to "Kellogg's" we had covered at least thirty miles. One of the worst irritations of the whole ride was the behavior of that little beast Brunet. Up to then he had always been used as a saddle-horse, and he was used to a place in the file near the guide at the head of it. He did not care at all for being demoted to the back as a pack-horse. So every time we came up on a stream, where the file broke apart so each rider could pick his own place to ford, the same thing happened without fail: just as I was reining Jerry into the water, Brunet would come charging past and throw himself into our very footsteps. Plunging, snorting, splashing water all over me, sometimes even startling Jerry into a jump sideways, he more than once had me on the very edge of being pitched into the stream. After an adventure or two of that kind I learned to hold back and let the maddening little animal go first, but it did me no good at all. What he was passionate about was going into the water at precisely the moment Jerry did, and in the end I had to make a bargain with young Roy to get down and hold him at every stream until I had gotten safely across.

"Kellogg's" was a comfortable house standing just inside the edge of a pleasant "grove of timber," which is what Western travelers call a small forest. Mrs. Kellogg turned out to be a very respectable-looking matron, and she soon let us know that she was from the city of New York. She seemed proud and delighted to be entertaining Mr. Hamilton, for whose family, as she made a point of telling us, she had in earlier days been in the habit of doing needlework.

The good woman gave us an excellent dinner and afterward settled me into a rocking-chair beside a big fire with the Life of Mrs. Fletcher to keep me entertained, while the men went over the place, looked at Mr. Kellogg's stock, and gave their own animals a careful going-over. We had meant to push on to Dixon's that same afternoon, but the snow started falling again and we had to content ourselves where we were.

Meanwhile Mr. Kellogg, finding that we were bound for Chicago, made up his mind to come along, having, as he said, some business of his own to do there. So Mrs. Kellogg set about getting him ready to start with us in the morning. I argued hard for staying one more day: the next day was Sunday, and I objected to traveling on it. But given the necessities of the case, the uncertainty of the weather, and how important it was to get through this wild country as fast as possible, my objections were overruled. All I could win was a delay in starting until late enough in the afternoon to leave us just time to ride the sixteen miles to "Dixon's" before sunset.

Mr. Kellogg's preparations did not take long. He would carry only two days' provisions, he said, because at his brother-in-law Dixon's we would get our supper and our breakfast, and he knew perfectly well that the road from there to Chicago could be done in a day and a half.

By that calculation we had enough left of our own stores to see us to the end of the trip, but my husband took the precaution of asking Mrs. Kellogg to bake us another bag of biscuits, in case of accidents. He also suggested to Mr. Kellogg that it might be prudent to lay in something more than his short allowance. The good man objected that he did not want to load his horse any heavier than he absolutely had to, seeing that at this season of the year we were carrying fodder for the animals on top of the rest of their load. It will be seen that we had reason to be glad of our own foresight.

The previous night had made me a good deal less fastidious than I had been when I set out. So when we were shown to our sleeping-quarters, and I found that we were to share them with six men, travelers like ourselves, my only feeling was thankfulness that every bed came with a full set of blue checked curtains, which made a perfectly tolerable substitute for a dressing-room.
