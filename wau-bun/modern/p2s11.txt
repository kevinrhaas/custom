Among the chiefs there were several who shared their tribe's general hostility toward the Americans, but who still kept a personal regard for the troops at this post and for the few white citizens of the place. These chiefs did everything they could to cool the young men's desire for revenge and turn them from their bloody plans, and it did no good at all.

On the evening after the council, Black Partridge, a conspicuous chief, came into the commanding officer's quarters.

"Father," he said, "I come to deliver up to you the medal I wear. It was given me by the Americans, and I have long worn it in token of our mutual friendship. But our young men are resolved to imbrue their hands in the blood of the whites. I can't restrain them, and I won't wear a token of peace while I am compelled to act as an enemy."

If any further proof had been wanting, this alone would have shown that doomed little company how right their gloomy expectations were. Even so, they went steadily on with the preparations that had to be made; and in the middle of all the horror of their position there was no shortage of gallant hearts who tried to keep up hopes of escape in their downcast companions — hopes they were far from holding themselves.

Of the ammunition, only twenty-five rounds had been kept back, besides one box of cartridges carried in the baggage-wagons. In any real danger that would have been far too little; but with a hard march ahead of them, and in the poor state they were in, the troops could not burden themselves with more.
