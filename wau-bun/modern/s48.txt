He was born in Quebec in 1763. His mother had been married before, to a gentleman named Haliburton. The one daughter of that first marriage was the mother of General Fleming, of Nicholas Low, Esq., and of Mrs. Charles King of New York, and she is described as a lady of remarkable beauty and accomplishment. Mr. Kinzie was the only child of the second marriage. His father died while he was still a baby, and his mother married a third time, to a Mr. Forsyth, after which they moved to the city of New York.

When he was ten or eleven he was put in school at Williamsburg, Long Island, along with two of his half-brothers. Every Saturday a negro servant was sent out from the city to bring the children home until Monday morning. One Saturday the messenger arrived at the school to find the whole place in an uproar. Johnny Kinzie was missing. They searched in every direction and turned out every corner. It was no use — Johnny Kinzie was nowhere to be found.

The heavy news was carried home to his mother. Some thought the boy had drowned; others that he had wandered off and would come back. Weeks went by, then months, and eventually he was given up for lost and mourned. All that time the boy was carrying out a plan he had made long before: to go to Quebec, the city he was born in, and make his own way in the world.

He had somehow managed to get himself across from Williamsburg to New York City, and finding a sloop bound for Albany at one of the North River docks, he took passage on her. On the way up the river a gentleman noticed him and, taking an interest in the small solitary passenger, asked him what his business was.

He was going to Quebec, he said, where he had some friends.

Did he have the money to get there?

Not much, but he thought he could manage.

As luck would have it, the gentleman was going to Quebec himself. He took the boy under his wing, paid his expenses the whole way, and finally said goodbye to him in the streets of the city — where the boy was, in fact, a complete stranger.

He wandered about for a while, looking into various stores and workshops. Eventually he went into a silversmith's shop, liked the look on the master's face, and asked him whether he wanted an apprentice.

"What, you, my little fellow? What can you do?"

"Anything you can teach me."

"Well, we'll give it a try and see."

The trial went well. He stayed in his kind friend's family more than three years, until his parents — who had necessarily come back to Canada when they moved to Detroit — found out where he was living, and he was given back to them.

There were five younger half-brothers, all named Forsyth. In the old family Bible we find this heartbreaking entry about something that happened after the family had settled in Detroit: "George Forsyth was lost in the woods 6th August, 1775, when Henry Hays and Mark Stirling ran away and left him. The remains of George Forsyth were found by an Indian the 2d of October, 1776, close by the Prairie Ronde."

There is something strangely cruel in the fact that this unhappy mother was called on twice to suffer the same kind of blow — losing a child in a way that is worse than death, because it leaves room for every horror the imagination can supply. Here is how the little brother was lost. Coming home from school one evening, he met the colored servant-boy riding out to the common to fetch the cows. The school-house stood quite near the old fort, and everything past it to the west was a wild, uncultivated tract they called the Common. The child begged the servant to take him up and give him a ride, but the servant refused and told him to go straight home. Two other boys, somewhat older, were with him, and the three of them followed the negro for some distance, hoping to talk him into it. As the light went, the two older boys turned back, but the little one kept on. The negro never saw the child again on his way home, and no word of him was ever heard, for all the diligent searching the whole small community did — until, as the record says, an Indian found his remains the following year. There was nothing to identify them by except the auburn curls of his hair and the little boots he had been wearing. He must have died very soon after losing his way, because the Prairie Ronde was too close to the settlement for him to have missed the calls and the sounding horns of the search party, if he had still been alive.

As Mr. Kinzie grew up, his enterprising, adventurous streak kept him living mostly on the frontier. He went into the Indian trade early, with posts at Sandusky and Maumee. Around the year 1800 he pushed further west, to St. Joseph's, Michigan. That same year he married Mrs. McKillip, the widow of a British officer, and in 1804 he came to make his home at Chicago. That was the year Major John Whistler built the first fort there.

Little by little he set up more remote trading-posts, all of them feeding the parent post at Chicago: at Milwaukie with the Menomonees, at Rock River with the Winnebagoes and the Pottowattamies, on the Illinois River and the Kankakee with the Pottowattamies of the Prairies, and with the Kickapoos in what was called Le Large — the huge district that was afterward made into Sangamon County.

Every trading-post had its superintendent and its full complement of engagés, its string of pack-horses and its outfit of boats and canoes. From most of the stations the furs and peltries came in to Chicago on pack-horses, and the goods needed for the trade went back out the same way.

The ships that came in spring and fall — rarely more than two or three a year — brought the supplies and trade goods, and carried the furs already collected up to Mackinac, the depot of the Southwest and American Fur Companies. At other times of year the furs were sent up there by boat, coasting around the lake.
