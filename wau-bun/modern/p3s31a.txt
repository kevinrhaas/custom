Some good rowing soon brought us to the place where the river runs into that beautiful sheet of water, Winnebago Lake. There was very little wind when we reached the lake, but the Frenchmen hoisted their sail anyway, hoping to save themselves the labor of rowing across. They whistled for a wind with all the force of their lungs, and it did no good. They appealed to La Vierge, with a comical mixture of fun and reverence, and that did no good either. As a last resort, somebody finally suggested that their only chance was to win over the goddess of the winds with an offering of some cast-off piece of clothing.

Guardapié, the crew's chief spokesman, went around and applied to everyone. Alas, not one of the poor voyageurs could boast of a spare article of any kind. A few old rags were eventually rummaged out of the little hole in the bow where the food and the clothing and the dirt are all kept together, and cast into the waves. For a moment everybody flattered himself that the experiment had worked — the sail fluttered, swelled a little, and then flapped idly down against the mast again. The party were in despair, until Julian and Edwin, after a whispered consultation together, stepped forward as messengers of mercy. In a trice they had divested themselves of jacket and vest and were offering the next garment down to help raise the wind.

At first there seemed to be a doubt in the boatmen's minds whether they ought to accept so magnificent an offer. Then they gave the garments a preparatory shake, discovered that the contribution was worth rather less than they had imagined, and, with a great deal of shouting and laughing, consigned them to the waves. To the boys' huge delight and astonishment, a breeze sprang up at that very moment and carried the little vessel beautifully along over the water, about half the distance to Garlic Island. By then the charm was exhausted, and it turned out to be impossible to renew it by repeating the offering. Every expedient was tried without success, and in the end, with a number of rather disrespectful remarks about the lady whose aid they had invoked, the Frenchmen had to fall back on their oars until they reached the island.

Two or three canoes of Winnebagoes landed at the same time, and their owners came straight over with an offering of some sturgeon they had caught in the lake. Since this promised to be an agreeable change at the noontide meal — for the Frenchmen, at any rate — it was decided to stop and kindle a fire to cook it. We took advantage of the interval to recommend to the boys a stroll over to the far side of the island, where the clear, shallow water and the pebbly beach were a temptation to a refreshing bath. While they made use of it, under the supervision of Harry, the black boy, we amused ourselves gathering the fine red raspberries that the island was full of.

Our enjoyment was cut short, though, by our discovering that the whole place — vines, shrubs, and apparently the very ground itself — was infested with wood-ticks by the million, a little insect that fastens on the skin and then works its way into the very flesh, causing a swelling and an irritation that are exceedingly painful and even dangerous. The alarm was sounded to bring the boys back at top speed to the open and more frequented part of the island. But we soon found we hadn't left our tormentors behind. All through the day we kept on being conscious of them, and we weren't free of the effects of their attacks for several days afterward, since the ones that had managed to bury themselves in the flesh had to be taken out with the point of a penknife or a large needle. After we had eaten our dinner, we stepped aboard our boat, and the wind having risen, the breeze carried us to the far verge of the lake and into the entrance of the river, or, as it was called, the Winnebago Rapids.

On the point of land to the right stood a cluster of neat bark wigwams. This was Four-Legs' village.
