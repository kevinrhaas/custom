All these unlucky events had delayed us so much that our afternoon's ride was a short one, bringing us no farther than the shore of a beautiful sheet of water now known as Crystal Lake. Its clear surface was covered with loons, and with Poules d'Eau, a kind of rail that at certain seasons is everywhere in this part of the country.

The Indians have Aesop's own genius for drawing animal life and character, and among them there is a fable or a legend to account for every peculiarity in the looks, the habits or the temperament of every kind of creature there is.

The little rail's back is very concave — hollow. Here is how the Indians say it came to be that way. STORY OF THE LITTLE RAIL, OR Poule d'Eau. Most of the Northwestern tribes believe there is an invisible being who corresponds to the Genie of Oriental stories. Nan-nee-bo-zho is not exactly the father of evil, but he is a spirit whose business it is to punish what is wrong. He is also described as forever busy trapping the animals that come his way and making examples of them.

One fine evening, as he was walking along the bank of a lake, he saw a flock of ducks sailing about and enjoying themselves on the blue water. He called out to them:

"Ho! Come into my lodge with me and I will teach you to dance!" Some of the ducks said among themselves, "That is Nan-nee-bo-zho — let us not go." Others thought differently, and since what he said sounded fair and his voice was persuasive, a few of them turned their faces toward the shore. All the rest soon followed, and with a great deal of pleasant quacking they trooped along after him and went into his lodge.

Once they were inside, he first took an Indian sack with a wide mouth and tied it by the strings around his neck, so that it hung down over his shoulders with the mouth left open. Then he stood himself in the middle of the lodge and arranged the ducks in a circle around him.

"Now," he said, "you must all shut your eyes tight. If anybody opens his eyes at all, something dreadful will happen to him. I am going to take my Indian flute and play on it, and when I give the word you will open your eyes and start dancing, the way you see me doing."

The ducks did as they were told, squeezing their eyes shut and keeping time to the music by stepping from one foot to the other, all of them impatient for the dancing to begin.

Before long there was a sound like a smothered "quack," but the ducks did not dare open their eyes.

Again and again the flute music would break off and a gurgling cry of "qu-a-a-ck" would be heard. There was one little duck, much smaller than the others, who at this point could not resist opening one eye, carefully. She saw Nan-nee-bo-zho playing his flute, holding it with one hand, and stooping a little every so often to seize the duck nearest him, which he throttled and stuffed into the bag on his shoulders. So she edged out of the circle a little, and worked her way nearer the door, which had been left partly open to let in the light, and cried out, "Open your eyes — Nan-nee-bo-zho is choking you all and putting you in his bag!"

With that she flew, but Nan-nee-bo-zho pounced on her. His hand closed on her back, and yet with a desperate effort she tore herself loose and got out into the open air. Her companions came flying after her, quacking and screaming. Some got away, and some fell victim to the sprite.

The little duck had saved her life, but she had lost her beauty. Ever after that she kept the shape she had been forced into in her moment of danger: her back pressed down in the middle, and her head and neck stretched out unnaturally forward into the air.
