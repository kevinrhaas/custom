The members of Mr. Kinzie's family who had stayed in the boat near the mouth of the river were carefully guarded by Kee-po-tah and another Indian. They had seen the smoke, then the blaze, and right after that the sound of the first tremendous volley reached them. Then everything was confusion. They understood nothing of it until they saw an Indian coming toward them from the battle-ground, leading a horse with a lady on it who seemed to be wounded.

"That is Mrs. Heald," cried Mrs. Kinzie. "That Indian will kill her. Run, Chandonnai" — to one of Mr. Kinzie's clerks — "take the mule that is tied there, and offer it to him to release her."

By this time her captor was pulling the bonnet off her head so that he could scalp her. Chandonnai ran up and offered the mule as a ransom, with the promise of ten bottles of whiskey as soon as they reached his village. The whiskey was a strong temptation.

"But," said the Indian, "she is badly wounded — she will die. Will you give me the whiskey in any case?"

Chandonnai promised that he would, and the bargain was struck. The savage put the lady's bonnet on his own head; and after some squaws tried and failed to take her shoes and stockings off her, she was brought aboard the boat, where she lay moaning with the pain of the many bullet-wounds she had in both arms.

The horse Mrs. Heald had been riding was a fine, spirited animal, and the Indians had wanted to have it undamaged, so they had aimed their shots to disable the rider without hurting her mount.

She had not been lying in the boat long when a young Indian with a savage look about him was seen coming toward them. A buffalo robe was hastily pulled over her, and she was warned to make no sound of complaint if she valued her life.

The heroic woman stayed perfectly silent while the savage came near. He had a pistol in his hand, which he rested on the side of the boat while he looked prying around with a fearful scowl. Black Jim, one of the servants, was standing in the bow. He seized an axe that lay near him and signed to the man that if he fired, he would split his skull, and told him the boat held nobody but the family of Shaw-nee-aw-kee. At that the Indian withdrew. It came out afterward that the man he was hunting for was Mr. Burnett, a trader from St. Joseph's, with whom he had an account to settle.

When the boat was finally allowed to go back to Mr. Kinzie's house, and Mrs. Heald had been taken inside, her wounds had to be dressed.

Mr. Kinzie asked an old chief who was standing by — a man who, like most of his tribe, had some skill in surgery — to get a ball out of the poor woman's arm.

"No, father," he replied. "I can't do it — it makes me sick here" — putting his hand on his heart.

So Mr. Kinzie did the operation himself, with his penknife.
