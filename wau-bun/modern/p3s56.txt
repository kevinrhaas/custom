The time had now come when we began to think seriously about leaving our pleasant home and going to live at Detroit, while we made arrangements to settle permanently at Chicago.

When this news was passed on to our Winnebago children, it brought out great lamentations and demonstrations of regret. They came flocking in from all the country round to find out whether what they had heard was true, and to beg us earnestly to go on living, and die, among them.

Of them all, nobody seemed so crushed as Elizabeth, our poor Cut-Nose. When we first told her what we meant to do, she sat for hours in the same spot, wiping away the tears that kept finding their way down her cheeks with the corner of the chintz shawl she wore pinned across her bosom.

"No! I never, never, never shall I find such friends again," she would cry. "You will go away, and I shall be left here all alone."

Wild-Cat too, the fat, jolly Wild-Cat, gave way to the loudest lamentations.

"Oh, my little brother," he said to the baby on the morning we left, when he had insisted on taking him and setting him on his fat, dirty knee, "you will never come back to see your poor brother again!"

And having had an extra glass for the occasion, he wept like a baby.

It was with heavy hearts that on the morning of the 1st of July, 1833, we said goodbye to the long procession that followed us to the boat, which was waiting to carry us to Green Bay. There we were to meet Governor Porter and Mr. Brush and go on to Detroit under their escort.

When they had finished their tender farewells, they turned to go with their father across the Portage, on his way to Chicago, and for a long time afterward we could see them winding along the road, and hear their loud lamentations at a parting they knew would be forever.
