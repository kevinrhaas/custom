"Tshah-ko-zhah?" (What is it?)

It was Hoo-wau-ne-kah, the Little Elk. He spoke fast, and sounded badly shaken. I couldn't follow him, and I lay there trembling, dreading to hear what his errand was once it had been interpreted. Now and then I could pick out the words Sau-kee (Sauks) and Shoonk-hat-tay-rah (horse), and they weren't very reassuring.

The trouble, as I soon learned, was this. A fresh trail had been seen near the Petit Rocher, on the Wisconsin, and the people at the villages on the Barribault were in a state of great alarm, afraid it might be the Sauks. It looked as though a hundred horses or more had gone by along that trail. Hoo-wau-ne-kah had been sent off at once to tell their Father and ask his advice.

After hearing him out, his Father told him that the trail was undoubtedly General Henry's troops, who were said to have come north looking for the enemy. Since the marks of the horses' hoofs showed, by his own report, that the animals had been shod, that was proof enough that it wasn't a Sauk trail. He thought the people at the villages had no reason to feel uneasy.

"Very well, Father," Hoo-wau-ne-kah said. "I'll go back and tell my people what you say. They'll believe you, because you always tell them the truth. You aren't like us Indians, who sometimes deceive each other." And with that he went back to his friends, much comforted.

Now that the picketing and the other defences were finished, and a detachment of troops had arrived at our fort from Fort Howard under Lieutenant Hunter, the fort seemed the safest place to be. So every evening, regularly, just before dusk, we formed our line of march for the other side of the river and went to the quarters that had been assigned us inside the garrison, leaving our own house and belongings in the care of the Frenchmen and our friends the Winnebagoes.
