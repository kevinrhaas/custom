Michilimackinac — that jewel of the Lakes. It looked bright and beautiful when we walked out the next morning. The rain had moved off and left everything glittering in the sun coming up over Lake Huron far to the east. In front of us lay the lovely bay, still not quite settled after the storm, dotted with canoes and with the boats of fishermen already setting their nets for the trout and whitefish that are the treasure of that water. Along the beach stood the wigwams of the Ottawas who had come to the island to trade. The people came out to look at us, and then a shout of welcome went up — they had recognized Shaw-nee-aw-kee. Seven years of living among them had made my husband known by sight to every single one of them.

A handshake and a firm "Bon-jour — bon-jour" is the standard greeting between an Indian and a white man.

"Do the Indians speak French?" I asked my husband.

"No. It's a habit they picked up from the French traders over many years of dealing with them."

The Canadian engagés were every bit as warm. Each one came trotting over to pay his respects to "Monsieur John" and to unload a long string of good wishes in a patois I could not begin to follow. I had to take on faith all the blessings being showered on "Madame John." The only part of it I understood was the hope that I would be happy and contented in my vie sauvage.
