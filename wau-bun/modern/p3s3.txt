Our horses picked up their pace as we came near a beautiful wooded knoll, which was how we knew this was to be our resting place for the night. These animals seem to know a good camping ground by instinct, and this was one of the loveliest imaginable.

Near the lake, the cold, late season had left the trees looking as pale and yellow as unfledged goslings. Here they were bursting into full leaf. The ground all around was carpeted with flowers, and we could not stand the thought of having them crushed by the felling of a tree and the pitching of our tent among them. The birds were sending out their sweetest notes in the warm, lingering sunlight, and the opening buds of the young hickory and sassafras filled the air with scent.

Nothing could have been more perfect than our enjoyment of that beautiful woodland retreat after our ride in the blazing sun. The children were in ecstasies. They loved finding ways to make themselves useful — stacking up the saddles, breaking off boughs for the fire, filling the little kettles with water for Petaille and Lecuyer, the two Frenchmen, who were putting our supper together.

They found the awkward way the horses moved once they had been spancelled endlessly funny. Everything was new to our little nephew Edwin, and Josette, who had already made more than one trip on horseback to St. Joseph, showed all the pride of a seasoned traveler in explaining to him whatever was strange or unaccountable.

They were not the last ones up when the call of "how! how!" came the next morning.

The fire was built up again, the preparations for breakfast began, and the Frenchmen were sent off to bring in the horses so we could get an early start.

Harry and Josette played their parts, under our direction, in getting the simple meal ready, and we were soon sitting down around the table-mat, each with a cup and a knife. The meal was over. No men and no horses appeared. When another half hour had gone by, my husband took Harry and set out to look for the missing ones.

The day wore on, and one man after another would turn up to report on his progress. Petaille and Lecuyer eventually brought in two of the horses, but the others were nowhere to be found. After a while Mr. Kinzie and Harry came back, soaked to the knees by the dew on the long prairie grass, and with no news at all. The men were sent out again once they had had their breakfast, and came back as unsuccessful as before.

Those of us at the camp had spent the whole morning speculating about the missing animals. Could the Indians have stolen them? Hardly. These people rarely stole anything in peacetime, and never from our family, whom they regarded as their best friends. The horses were bound to turn up. Most likely they had been carelessly tied the evening before and had been able to wander farther than they usually did.

We held a council, and it was decided to send Grignon back to Chicago to get fresh horses from Gholson Kercheval and return as fast as he could. If he found our camp deserted when he got back, he could take it that we had found the horses and gone on to Fox River, where he would certainly catch up with us.

He had not been gone an hour when the vexatious animals came hopping slowly out of a point of woods to the north of us — a spot every one of the searchers swore he had gone over again and again — and headed straight for the place where we were. They walked up as demurely as if nothing had happened, and seemed rather surprised to be met with a hearty scolding instead of the usual pats and endearments.

It was the work of a very short half hour to strike and pack the tent, stow away the mats and kettles, saddle the horses, and mount up for our journey.
