There were no boats ready to go up the river. Our old friend Hamilton promised to have one got ready at once, but time went by and no boat was made ready.

It was now the beginning of November. We were passing our time very pleasantly with the Irwins and the Whitneys, and at the house of Colonel Stambaugh, the Indian Agent, but the delay was still inconvenient and annoying.

I suggested making the trip on horseback. "Certainly not," was the answer I got every time. "No mortal woman has ever gone that road, unless it was some native on foot, and no woman ever could."

"But suppose we set out in the boat and get frozen in on the way. We can't spend the winter out there, and we couldn't possibly find our way to a human home. We have had one experience of that kind already. Isn't it better to assume that I can do what you and other men have done?"

Dr. Finley, the post-surgeon at Fort Howard, heard the question being argued and immediately offered me his favorite horse, Charlie. "He is very sure-footed," the doctor said, "and first-rate in a marsh or a difficult stream."

So it was settled that we should go by land, and as soon as our old Menomonee friend Wish-tay-yun — as good a guide on land as on water — could be sent for, we set off, leaving our trunks to be forwarded by Hamilton whenever it should suit him to carry out his intention of sending up his boat.

On the morning we left we waited until late for our fellow-travelers, Mr. Wing of Monroe and Dr. Philleo of Galena, but when they did not join us we decided to waste no more time, confident that we would all meet at the Kakalin sometime that evening.
