When we got up the next morning we could hardly take it in that only twelve miles of prairie now lay between us and Chicago le Désiré, as I couldn't help calling it.

We could see clear across the wide plain, and at the very far edge of it stood two tall trees. My husband pointed them out to me: he had planted them himself as a boy. They had already grown high enough to serve as landmarks, and they stayed in sight the whole way as we followed the beaten road. I kept saying to myself, over and over, "That is where the friends I have been longing to see are living. That is where all our trials and hardships come to an end."

A Mr. Wentworth joined us on the road, and we asked him how the family was — we had had no news of them for a long time. When we reached Chicago he took us to a little tavern at the forks of the river. That part of the place was then called Wolf Point, after an Indian named Moaway, or the Wolf, who had lived there.

"Good heavens," said the old landlady at the little tavern, "what dreadful cold weather you must have had to travel in! Why, two days ago the river was wide open here, and now it's frozen hard enough for people to ride across it on horseback!"

Even so, my husband did not care to risk the horses, so he decided to leave them behind and go on foot to his mother and sister's house, about half a mile away.

We set off walking, first across the ice and then along the north bank of the river. As we came near the house, Genevieve, a half-breed servant of the family, caught sight of us. She did not stop to greet us — she flew into the house shouting, "Oh! Madame Kinzie, guess who has come? Monsieur John and Madame John, all the way from Fort Winnebago on foot!"

In another minute we were in the arms of our dear, kind friends. A messenger was sent off to the garrison for the rest of the family, and for that day at any rate I was the wonder and admiration of the whole circle, "for the dangers I had seen."
