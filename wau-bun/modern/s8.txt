We picked a bad moment to arrive at Green Bay. A treaty was being made between the United States Government and the Menomonees and Waubanakees, so the place was full — not only the treaty commissioners with their clerks and officials but traders, claimants, travelers and idlers beyond counting. Most of them were packed into the only hotel in town, a decent-sized house near the riverside, and when we came into the long dining room, cold and dripping from an open boat, the assortment of humanity in it entertained us enormously. There were groups of people sitting everywhere. Newcomers like ourselves stood around, because there were nowhere near enough chairs for everybody who wanted feeding. The landlord sat calm and indifferent with his hands in his pockets, the picture of Pennsylvania Dutch phlegm.

His fat, capable wife trotted around the room, stopping one moment to scold somebody who — "burn his skin!" — had not done his job, and the next laughing so hard at some joke made to her that her sides shook.

She welcomed us warmly enough, but when we asked, "Can you put us up?" the answer was, "Not I. I've got twice as many people now as I know what to do with. I've had to turn my own family out of their rooms, what with the commissioners and the load of folks that's come in on us."

"Then what are we to do? It's too late and too stormy to go up to Shanty-town looking for lodgings."

"Well, sit yourselves down and take your supper, and we'll see what we can do."

And she did in fact manage to find us a little corner, and we were glad to take shelter in it from the crowd.

A thin board partition was all that separated us from the room where General Root of New York, one of the treaty commissioners, was staying. The steamer we came on had brought the mail, which in those days was a rare blessing to a distant settlement, and all the dispatches the General received around bedtime had to be opened and read before he could go to sleep. His eyes were weak, so his secretaries did the reading. He was also a little deaf. Through that flimsy partition the contents of every letter and every remark he made about it came through unpleasantly clear, because he kept telling his secretary to speak up.

"What's that, Walter? Read that over again."

We coughed. We hemmed. We knocked over several pieces of furniture. It was no use — they were far too absorbed to notice anything happening around them, and if we had been politicians we would have gone to bed holding every secret of the working-men's party, with plenty to make capital out of.
