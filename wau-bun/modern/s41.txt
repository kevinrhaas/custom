We still had the trail to guide us, and we kept following it until about nine o'clock, when we came out of a wood onto a broad, fast river. There was a cluster of Indian wigwams on the far bank, and since the trail led straight down to the water it was fair to assume the stream could be forded. We got no chance to test that, though, because the banks were so lined with ice — piled up tier on tier when the river broke up the week before — that we hunted in vain for any way down the bank to the water.

The men shouted again and again, hoping some stray inhabitant of the village might be within earshot with his canoe. Nothing answered but the echoes. What was to be done? I looked at my husband and saw the worry on his face, though he still kept speaking cheerfully. "We will follow this cross-trail down along the bank of the river," he said. "There must be Indians wintering somewhere near, in one of these points of wood."

I have to admit that our prospects dismayed me a little, but I kept up a show of courage and did not let my low spirits show. The whole party was dull and gloomy enough as it was.

We kept along the bank, which stood well up above the water and had a thick wood bordering it a little way off. All at once my horse, who was mortally afraid of Indians, began to jump and prance, snorting and pricking up his ears as if an enemy were at hand. I screamed with delight to my husband, who was at the head of the file: "Oh, John! John! there are Indians near — look at Jerry!"

At that instant a little Indian dog ran out from under the bushes at the roadside and started barking at us. No sound was ever more welcome. We rode straight into the thicket, and going down into a little hollow we found two squaws crouched behind the bushes, trying to hide themselves from us.

They looked greatly relieved when Mr. Kinzie spoke to them in the Pottowattamie language: "What are you doing here?"

"Digging Indian potatoes" — a kind of artichoke.

"Where is your lodge?"

"On the other side of the river."

"Good — then you have a canoe here. Can you take us across?"

"Yes — the canoe is very small."

They took us down the bank to the water's edge where the canoe was. It was indeed very small. My husband explained to them that they would have to take me across first and then come back for the rest of the party.

"Will you trust yourself alone over the river?" he asked me. "You see that only one can cross at a time."

"Oh, yes" — and I was soon laid in the bottom of the canoe, flat on my back looking up at the sky, while the older woman took the paddle in her hand and knelt at my head, and the younger, a girl of fourteen or fifteen, took her place at my feet. There was just enough room for me to lie like that, with each of them kneeling in the opposite ends of the canoe.

While all this was being arranged, Mr. Kinzie questioned the women about where we were. They knew no name for the river but "Saumanong." That was not much help, being the general term for any large stream. But he gathered that the village we had passed higher up on the other side was Wau-ban-see's, and from that he knew we were on the Fox River, and probably about fifty miles from Chicago.

The woman assured him, when he asked, that Chicago was "close by."

"What that means," he said, "is that it isn't as far off as Canada. We must not get our hopes up."

The men set about unpacking the horses, and meanwhile I was paddled across the river. The old woman went straight back, leaving the younger one with me for company. I sat down on the fallen trunk of a tree in the middle of the snow and looked out across the dark water. I am not ashamed to confess the weakness — for the first time on the whole trip, I shed tears. It was not hunger that brought them, or fear, or cold. It was the complete desolation of spirit, that sickness of heart which "hope deferred" always brings on, and which of all evils is the hardest to bear.

The poor little squaw looked into my face with a wondering, sympathetic expression. She was probably working out in her own mind what a person who rode such a fine horse and wore such a comfortable broadcloth dress could possibly have to cry about. I pointed to a place beside me on the log, but she preferred to stand and gaze at me with the same pitying look. Before long another young woman joined her, and after a short bout of chattering in which I was obviously the subject, the two of them trotted off into the woods and left me to my own solitary reflections.

"What would my friends back East think," I said to myself, "if they could see me now? What would poor old Mrs. Welsh say — she who warned me that if I came away this far West I would break my heart?

Wouldn't she be delighted to see how likely her prediction was to come true?"

Those thoughts pulled me together. I dried my tears, and by the time my husband was across with his party and all his horses and luggage, I had my cheerfulness back and was ready for fresh adventures.
