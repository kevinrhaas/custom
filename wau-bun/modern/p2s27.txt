Things were getting critical, and all the prisoners had to be moved somewhere safer than the frontier now seemed likely to be. So it was decided to send Mr. Kinzie to the mother country. Nothing has ever come to light that explains General Proctor's conduct toward this gentleman. He had been taken out of the bosom of his family, where he was living quietly on the parole he had been given and protected by the terms of the surrender. He had been kept in confinement for months. Now he was put on horseback under a strong guard, who announced that their orders were to shoot him through the head if he so much as offered to speak to anyone on the road. He was tied onto the saddle so that he could not escape, and off they set for Quebec. One small incident on the way helps to show how our citizens were treated at that period, without exception, by the British army on the Northwestern frontier.

The saddle Mr. Kinzie rode on had not been fastened properly, and with the rough motion of the animal under it, it turned, twisting the rider into an extremely awkward and painful position. His limbs were tied, so he could not free himself, and the men in charge of him made him ride that way until he was nearly worn out before they had the humanity to release him.

When he reached Quebec he was put on board a small vessel to be sent to England. A few days out at sea the vessel was chased by an American frigate and driven into Halifax. She set sail a second time, sprang a leak, and had to put back.

The attempt to send him across the ocean was given up then, and he was returned to Quebec. Soon afterward another step was taken, just as inexplicable as his arrest: he was released, along with Mr. Macomb of Detroit, who was also confined at Quebec, and both were given leave to go back to their friends and families, though the war was not yet over. It may be that in the treatment these gentlemen got, the British commander-in-chief was sheltering behind the plea that they were "native-born British subjects," and that perhaps, when it came out that Mr. Kinzie really was a citizen of the United States, it was thought safest to let him go.

Meanwhile General Harrison had reached Detroit at the head of his troops. He landed on the 29th of September. All the citizens went out to meet him, and Mrs. Kinzie, leading her children by the hand, was among them. The General walked home with her and took up his quarters there. On arriving he was introduced to Kee-po-tah, who happened to be visiting the family at the time. The General had met the chief the year before, at the Council at Vincennes, and the meeting was a very cordial and interesting one.
