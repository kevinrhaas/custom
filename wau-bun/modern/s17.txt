Our camp that night was the most charming one you could imagine. Because of the heavy work the men had put in earlier in the day we made only a short run in the afternoon, and after seven or eight miles of pulling we drew in at a beautiful opening among the trees a little below the Little Butte.

The soldiers now went about camping in earnest, felling a big tree for their fire and setting to work cooking enough food for the next day's run — a long one, twenty-one miles across Winnebago Lake. Our Frenchmen did the same. The fire caught in the light dry grass all around us, and in no time everything was blaze and crackle.

Luckily the wind was strong enough to carry the flames all one way, and besides, there was not enough fuel there to make them anything to worry about. We hopped up onto the fallen logs and dignified the whole small, contained business with the title of "a prairie on fire." The most serious consequence was that it burned up all the dry grass, several armfuls of which, spread under the bearskin in my tent the night before, had made a great improvement to my bed.

Our supper was a genuine delight, taken at the pleasant sunset hour under the tall trees beside water that ran past murmuring, and when the broad bright moon came up and poured its light over the whole scene — so wild and yet so beautiful in that vast solitude — I felt that the friends I had left behind might well envy me.

But everything ends, including at last my enthusiasm for the beauty around me, and, reluctantly, I closed the tent and took my place inside, close enough to the fall of the canvas that I could lift it now and then and peek out at the night.

After a while everything was quiet. The men had gone silent and seemed to have turned in, and we were just going under when we heard a heavy tread outside and then a blunt voice.

"Mr. Kinzie! Mr. Kinzie!"

"Who's there? What is it?"

"I'm Krissman. Didn't you mean, sir, for the men to have any liquor tonight?"

"Of course I did. Hasn't Kilgour given out your rations?"

"No, sir. He says you didn't say anything particular about it, and he wasn't going to come ask you if you forgot. But I thought I wouldn't be bashful — I'd just come and ask."

"Quite right. Tell Kilgour I'd like him to serve out a ration apiece."

"Thank you, sir." He sounded delighted. "I'll tell him."

Krissman was becoming quite a character with us.

A few miles of rowing the next morning brought us to Four-Legs' village at the entrance to Winnebago Lake, a picturesque cluster of Indian huts spread out over a pretty green glade and shaded by fine tall trees.

We were properly in the Winnebago country now, and I soon learned that the odd-sounding name of the place came from the principal chief of the nation, who lived there. The people were away, most likely gone to their wintering grounds. Here we said goodbye to our friend Wish-tay-yun, having reached the edge of his country.

"Bon-jour, Chon!" — John — "bon-jour, maman." A hearty handshake finished his farewell as we pushed off into the lake, leaving him smoking his kin-nee-kin-nick and waiting until the spirit moved him to start his long Indian trot toward home in the Menomonee country.

Our sunshine seemed to leave with him. The sky, which had been bright and serene until then, clouded over, and instead of the delightful voyage we had been promising ourselves across the silver water of the lake, we had to stay shut up under the canvas, peering out now and then to catch a glimpse of the country through pouring rain.

It was no more than you would expect on an autumn day, but we were unreasonable enough to find it tedious, so to pass the time and take the edge off my disappointment my husband told me some pieces of his early history that had to do with Four-Legs.

When he was living at Prairie du Chien in the employ of the American Fur Company, the chiefs and other Indians from the Upper Mississippi used to come down often to sell furs and peltries and to buy merchandise, ammunition, trinkets and so on.

As happens to everyone not yet acclimated, he came down with chills and fever. One day, in an unusually bad bout of the fever, a chief of the Four-Legs family — a brother of the one I have mentioned — came into the Company's warehouse to trade. There is no ceremony or restraint among the Indians, so the moment Four-Legs heard that Shaw-nee-aw-kee was sick he went straight to him to offer his sympathy and prescribe the proper remedies.

Anyone who has ever had the ague and the raging fever that follows it knows how unendurable the drawn-out conversation of a thoughtless person can be, and will have no trouble believing that the longer Four-Legs prattled on, the higher the patient's fever climbed and the more unbearable the pain in his head and back and limbs became.

At last the old man got to his point. It was not good, he said, for a young man who was sick and far from his family to be without a home and a wife. He had a fine daughter at home, handsome and healthy, a first-rate nurse, and the best hand in the whole tribe at trapping beaver and musk-rats. He was coming down again in the spring and would bring her with him, and Shaw-nee-aw-kee would see that he had told no lies about her. Should he go now, and bring his daughter next time?

Stunned by all that insistent chatter and wanting nothing on earth but rest and quiet, poor Shaw-nee-aw-kee agreed eagerly, and the chief left.

His illness had come so close to tipping into delirium that for a while the young man forgot the interview and the offer completely. It came back to him some months later, when Four-Legs turned up bringing a woman of mature years and a positive Hecate for ugliness. She was carrying an enormous pack of furs on her shoulders, and she came over with that awkward criss-cross walk of hers and threw it down at his feet — the Indian way of announcing what she understood the relationship between them to be.

The conversation with her father came back to him in a flash, and he began to see he had gotten into a position that was going to take some skill to get out of.

He told one of the young clerks to pick up the pack and carry it into the magazine where the furs were stored. Then he went on calmly talking with the chief about other things.

Miss Four-Legs sat for a while with a sulky, dissatisfied look on her face. Finally she burst out:

"Humph! He takes no more notice of me than if I was nobody!"

He turned to the clerk again. "Give her a calico shirt and half a dozen bread-tickets."

That did nothing for the gloom on her face. Seeing he would have to raise the subject himself, the father said:

"Well, I have brought you my daughter, according to our agreement. How do you like her?"

"Ah, yes — she's a very nice young woman, and she'd make a first-rate wife, I've no doubt. But do you know, a very strange thing has happened since you were here? Our father, Governor Cass, has sent for me to come to Detroit so he can send me among the Wyandottes and the other nations to learn their customs and manners. Now if I go, and I'll have to go, I'll be gone two or three years — maybe four. And then what? Why, people will say Shaw-nee-aw-kee married Four-Legs' daughter and then hated her and ran off from her, and everybody will laugh at her, and she'll be ashamed. It would be better to take some good, valuable presents — blankets, guns and so on — and marry her to one of her own people, who will always stay by her and take care of her."

The old man was shrewd enough to see that his best move was to make the best bargain he could get. I have no doubt it cost a round sum to settle the matter to the injured lady's satisfaction, though I have never managed to find out how much. This much I do know: the young gentleman was careful not to make his next bargain in a fit of the ague. And the lady up on the Mississippi is called by his name, in derision, to this day.

About midway across the lake we passed Garlic Island, a lovely spot that deserves a better name. It belonged, along with the village on the opposite shore, to Wild Cat — a fat, jolly, good-natured fellow, nothing like the formidable animal his name suggests.

He and his band were away, like their neighbors at Four-Legs' village, so there was nothing to break the monotony of the sail. It was too wet for singing, and the men, though wrapped in their overcoats, looked like drowned chickens. They had to work their oars unusually hard just to keep warm, which probably meant they felt the dullness and listlessness of that cold, rainy October day less than we did.

Toward evening the sun came out. We had passed into the Fox River and were just entering that beautiful little sheet of water called Butte des Morts Lake, at the far end of which we were to camp for the night.

The water along the shore was green with fields of wild rice. Gathering it, right at that season, is important work for the Indian women. They push their canoes into the thick stands of rice, bend the stalks over the side with their paddles, and beat the ripe husks off into a cloth spread in the bottom of the canoe. Then it is rubbed to separate grain from husk and winnowed in the open air, and then it goes into their cordage bags and is packed away for the winter. The grain is longer and more slender than Carolina rice and greenish-olive in color, and while it makes a pleasant food it is not especially nourishing. The Indians like it best as a soup with birds or venison added.
