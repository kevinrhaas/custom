When he got to Fort Winnebago, our first business was to fit him out with a complete wardrobe, which was put in a box in his sleeping-room and given into his own keeping. There are no words for his delight when these valuable possessions were entrusted to him. Every spare minute went into contemplating them. Every now and then Tomah would go missing, and he was always found sitting beside his little trunk, folding and refolding his clothes, laying them out lengthwise, then crosswise, the happiest creature alive.

Our next step was to teach him to be useful. The little jobs we gave him at first were ones we thought would not be entirely new to him, but we soon noticed that whenever there was anything in the shape of work, Tomah slipped off to bed, even if he had not had his supper yet. One evening he was given some fish to scale. It was just getting dark, and Tom, as usual, went straight to bed.

The cook came in to ask what was to be done. I had to call in my husband as interpreter. He sent for Tomah, and when the boy came into the parlor Mr. Kinzie said to him in Pottowattamie, "There are some fish in the kitchen, Tomah, and we want you to scale them."

"Now?" said Tom, with a look of amazement. "It is very late."

A young lady who was visiting us, Miss Rolette, understood the language and could not help bursting out laughing at the simplicity with which it was said, and we all joined her. Tom looked a little offended at that, but once he understood that it was the white custom to scale fish at night and put salt and pepper on them, he was soon reconciled to doing his duty in the matter.

His next job was laying the table. There was a best set of china that only came out when we had company, and a best set of teaspoons that I kept in a bureau drawer in my own room upstairs. I made a habit of keeping that drawer locked and putting the key under a small clock on the mantelpiece. The first time I showed Tomah how to arrange things for visitors, I had brought the silver down and put it on the table myself.

Soon afterward we were expecting company to tea again, and I explained to Tomah that the best china was to be used. Imagine my surprise when I walked through the dining-room a little later and saw not only the new china but the "company silver" on the table as well. I asked our mother, who could talk with him, to look into the matter.

Tomah said, very coolly, "He got the silver where it was kept."

"Did he find the drawer open?"

"No — he opened it with a key."

"Was the key in the drawer?"

"No — it was under that thing on the shelf."

"How did he know it was kept there?"

That was the one thing Mr. Tomah declined to say. We never could find out whether he had watched my movements at some point. Nobody had ever seen him in that part of the house, and yet there was hardly an article you could name whose whereabouts Tomah did not know. If anyone was at a loss to find something, it was always, "Ask Tomah — he will tell you." And in fact he did.

He was a great source of amusement to the young officers. We were to have a tea-party one evening, with all the families and the young officers from the Fort. To make Tomah look as professional as possible, we made him a white apron with long sleeves to wear while he helped Mary and Josette carry round the tea — because I have to admit that Tomah's clothes were not kept in nearly such good order out of the trunk as they were in it.

Tom was delighted with his new costume, and with his new employment as well. He carried it off to perfection, since he never had the least trouble imitating anything he saw someone else do. After tea we had some music. I was standing by the piano, where one of the ladies was seated, when Lieutenant Vancleve said to me quietly, "Look behind you a moment."

I turned. There sat Tom between two of the company, as stately as could be, his white apron smoothed down and his hands clasped in front of him, listening to the music and on the best possible terms with himself and everyone around him. Julian and Edwin could hardly keep from laughing out loud, but they were afraid to do or say anything that might make him move before the company had had the full benefit of the sight. It was voted unanimously that Tomah should be allowed to stay and enjoy the pleasures of society for one evening; but with his usual restlessness he got tired as soon as the music was over, and took his leave of the company without ceremony.
