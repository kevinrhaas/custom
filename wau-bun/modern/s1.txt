It was a dark, rainy evening in September of 1830 when we went aboard the steamer Henry Clay, bound for Green Bay. Every friend we had in Detroit had congratulated us on our luck. We were being spared the little schooners, which at that time were the ordinary way of reaching the few far-flung settlements on Lakes Huron and Michigan, and everybody agreed that being spared them was a mercy.

Each of them had a story, too — his own bad luck or a friend's — about those unreliable crossings. Long strandings on the St. Clair flats. Head-winds off Thunder Bay that blew for days. Dead calms at Mackinac or the Manitous that went on and on. The story that made us feel luckiest of all had the advantage of being true: a relative of ours left Detroit in June and reached Chicago the following September. Three months to do what a sail-vessel can sometimes manage in four days.

None of it would have stopped me. I was going to see that country, and more than see it — I was going to live in it, and it had been a country of romance to me for as long as I could remember. It began with the letters of a relative I loved, describing his home and his way of life in the Indian country, and later with his wonderful account of the tour he made with General Cass in 1820 to the head-waters of the Mississippi. If I am honest it began even earlier than that, back when I stood at my teacher's knee and spelled out that long word, Mich-i-li-mack-i-nac. The enormous lakes. The prairies that did not end. The huge forests. All of it had a grip on my imagination. Now I was going to see it. Now it was going to be home.

We rode down to the quay through the dark back streets in a cart — the only vehicle that could get through the mud of Detroit's unpaved streets in those days — and we laughed the whole way. We laughed again going down the narrow, nearly vertical stairway into the little room they called the Ladies' Cabin. We thought the accommodations were splendid. Compared with what we had been promised, they were the height of comfort and convenience, especially since the only other people in the cabin were one lady and two small girls.

For the first twenty-four hours nothing could have been pleasanter. Some of the passengers were officers we knew, old friends. We had plenty of books. The men read aloud now and then, admired the enormous lonely country going by, the primeval woods, the water running out to the horizon without a single sail on it, and then went back to their cigars and their euchre to kill the hours.
