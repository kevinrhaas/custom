The next morning was a bright and lovely one for that season of the year, and we said goodbye to Mr. and Mrs. Dixon in high spirits. For the first few miles we rode along the beautiful rolling banks of the Rock River, going east the whole time, holding to the beaten path — the road, rather — that leads to Fort Clark, or Peoria. The Sauk trail, we had been told, would cross this road about six miles along.

After we had gone what we judged was the full six miles, we came on a trail running northeast, and we held a conference about whether this was likely to be the one we were looking for.

Mr. Kinzie thought it bore too far to the north, and that it was also too faint and hard to make out for a trail used so heavily, and by such a large body of Indians on their yearly journeys.

Plante was positive that it was the exact spot where he and "Piché" had struck into the great road on their trip to Fort Winnebago the year before. "On that very rising-ground at the point of woods, he remembered perfectly well stopping to shoot ducks, which they ate for their supper."

Mr. Kellogg would not commit himself, and took first one man's side and then the other's.

Since Plante was "the guide," and so confident of being right into the bargain, it was decided that we would follow him — not without some grumbling, though, from the bourgeois, who every so often called a halt to talk over the state of affairs.

"Now, Plante," he would say, "I am sure you are leading us too far north. Why, man, if we keep on in this direction, following the course of the river, we shall bring up at Kosh-ko-nong, instead of Chicago."

"Ah! mon bourgeois," the light-hearted Canadian would answer, "would I tell you this is the road if I weren't quite certain? Only one year ago I traveled it, and can I forget so soon? Oh, no — I remember every foot of it."

But Monsieur Plante was convinced of his mistake when the trail brought us out at the great bend of the river, with its bold rocky bluffs.

"Are you satisfied now, Plante?" Mr. Kinzie asked. "With your permission, I will play pilot myself from here on." And he struck off from the trail, heading as nearly due east as he could.

The weather had changed and turned intensely cold, and we knew that the delay we had run into was no trifling matter, even supposing we were on the right road at last. We had not added to our stock of provisions at Dixon's, because we wanted to carry all the forage we could for the horses — the scanty pickings around our camping-grounds did not make them anything like a meal. But we were buoyed up by the hope that we were on the right path at last, and we rode on until dark, when we reached a comfortable "encampment" at the edge of a grove beside a small stream.

Oh, how bitterly cold that night was! The salted provisions I was used to eating gave me an unbearable thirst, and my husband had gotten into the habit of setting the little tin coffee-pot full of water at the head of my bed when we turned in. That night it was frozen solid long before midnight. We were wrapped up so well in blankets that we did not suffer from the cold while we were inside the tent, but the open air was severe beyond anything.

March 15th. — The bourgeois had us up at first light to get ready to start. We had to find the Sauk trail that day, whatever it cost. What would become of us if we failed to? It was a question nobody cared to ask, and certainly one that nobody could have answered.

As soon as we left our camp we found ourselves entering a marshy stretch of country. Wild geese, brant, and ducks by the myriad rose up screaming as we came on. The lakes and ponds further off were black with them. But the shallow water we were trying to make our way across had frozen in the severity of the night to a thickness not quite enough to carry the horses — just enough to cut their feet and ankles at every step as they broke through it. Sometimes it was so hard to get forward that we had to turn back and go around the head of the marsh, which added to how miserable our situation was, because we knew that for all our diligent traveling we were making very little actual progress.

Once that swampy country was finally behind us, we came onto firmer ground, mostly open prairie. But now a new trouble hit us. The weather had moderated, and a blinding snowstorm came on. With no trail we could rely on and no compass, the only thing we had to steer by was the sun, and now the air was so thick that we could not tell what quarter of the sky he was in.

We kept going anyway, and it must have been a wandering course. After many miles of that, we came on an Indian trail, deeply worn, running at right angles to the way we were headed. The snow had stopped, and as the clouds thinned we could make out where the sun was and see that the trail ran north and south. What were we to do? Was it safer to keep on east, or was it likely that this new path would drop us onto the direct one we had been hunting for so long? And if we took the trail, should we go north or south? Mr. Kinzie was for south. He thought we were still too far north — somewhere around the Grand Marais, or Kish-wau-kee. Mr. Kellogg and Plante were for taking the northerly direction. Plante was positive that his bourgeois had already come too far south — that we must in fact be somewhere near the Illinois River by now. Finding himself outvoted, my husband gave in, and we turned our horses' heads north, very much against his will. A few miles on, though, he made up his mind all at once. "You may go north if you please," he said, "but I am convinced that the other course is right, and I shall face about — follow who will."

So we wheeled around and rode south again, and many a long and weary mile we traveled, with nothing to break up the ride but the fretful remarks of poor Mr. Kellogg. "I am really afraid we are wrong, Mr. Kinzie. I feel pretty sure that the young man is right. It looks most natural to me that we should take a northerly course, and not be stretching away so far to the south."

Mr. Kinzie turned a deaf ear to all of it. The Frenchmen rode along in silence. They would sooner have cut off their right hands than show any opposition to the bourgeois once he had stated his decision. It would never have crossed their minds to offer an opinion or a remark unless they had been invited to.

The road had run for many miles through the prairie, and finally, winding around a point of woods, it brought us out all at once on an Indian village. The whole party gave a shout of joy — but no shout came back. Not so much as a dog barking a friendly welcome, as we galloped up to the wigwams. It was all as silent as the grave. We rode around and around, then got down and looked inside several of the spacious huts. They had plainly been deserted a long time. Nothing was left but the bare bark walls; everything that could be called furniture had been stripped out by the owners and carried off to their wintering-grounds, to be brought back in the spring when they came again to plant their corn-fields and take up their summer cabins.

Our disappointment can be imagined better than described. We mounted with heavy hearts and went on our way once more, the snow falling again and adding to how uncomfortable our position was. Finally we halted for the night. We had known for some time that our stock of provisions was not enough for another day, and here we were — nobody knew where — in the middle of woods and prairies, certainly a long way from any human home, with barely enough food for a thin evening meal.

The poor dogs came whining around us to beg their usual share, but they had to content themselves with a bare bone, and we went to bed with the feeling that if we were not actually hungry yet, we certainly would be tomorrow.

The next day came. Plante and Roy had a bright fire going and a good pot of coffee for us. That was our whole breakfast, because when we shook the bag out and turned it inside out, all we could get out of our stock of bread was three crackers, which the rest of the party insisted I put in my pocket for my dinner. I was very much touched by Mr. Kellogg's kindness — he pulled a piece of tongue and a slice of fruit-cake out of his wallet and said "he had been saving for the lady since the day before, for he saw how matters were a going."

Poor man! It would have been well for him if he had listened to Mr. Kinzie and provided himself at the outset with a larger store of provisions. As it was, what he had brought with him ran out early on the second day, and he had been boarding with us for the last two meals.
