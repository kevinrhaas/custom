That night we were to camp somewhere not far from the Four Lakes. The heat and the day's riding had worn us out, and we watched anxiously for the clumps of willows and alders that would mark the spot where we would find water. We did not feel up to pushing on all the way to the bank of the nearest lake. It would have taken us too far off our direct course anyway.

When we finally came on a spot that would do, we congratulated each other that this was to be our last night on the road. The next day we would be at Winnebago!

The trip had been a delight from beginning to end — one long stretch of high spirits and enjoyment. The various mishaps had been vexing at the moment, but in the end they only added to the fun. Still, human nature being what it is, we were glad to trade the excitement for the quiet of home.

The next morning's ride was a quieter business than anything that had come before, because early on we started across what was known as the "Twenty-mile Prairie" — and I hope I may be allowed to point out that miles are astonishingly long on the prairies. Crossing it was like crossing the desert, minus the sand. Mile after mile of unbroken open ground — not a tree, not a living thing but ourselves.

The sun, as if to make up for two months of keeping out of sight, came out twice as bright as usual. There is no such thing as carrying an umbrella on horseback, though the people in the wagon were able to take shelter under one.

Our mother's energy had kept her in the saddle up to that day, but now she was fairly obliged to give in and hand her place on little Brunet over to sister Margaret.

So we went on, one little knoll rising up beyond the last, and from the top of each of them in turn we hoped to make out the distant woods, which were to us the promised land.

"Take courage," came the encouraging words, repeated often. "Very soon you'll begin to see the timber."

Another hour would drag heavily by.

"Now, when we get to the rising ground just ahead, look sharp."

We would look sharp — nothing but the same unchanging landscape.

There weren't even any streams to take the edge off the feverish thirst that came of being tired and impatient.

Finally a whoop from Shaw-nee-aw-kee broke the silence we had been riding along in.

"Le voilà!" (There it is!)

Our less practised eyes couldn't pick out the faint blue strip edging the horizon at first, but it grew and grew on us, and the fatigue and every other discomfort faded away in proportion.

By the time we reached "Hastings's Woods," a fine forest with a clear, sparkling stream running through it, we were in high spirits.

The green leaves and the cool water were as good as food and drink to us, but we didn't let ourselves forget that the day was wearing on and that we had to finish the trip before sunset if we possibly could. So we soon braced ourselves to go on, though we would gladly have lingered another hour.

Thanks to the heat of the past week, the marsh at Duck Creek was in a very different state from a few months earlier, when I had had the bad luck to go down into its icy water.

We got over it without trouble and soon found ourselves on the bank of the creek.

The stream was thought to be fordable at that point in any weather, and even if it hadn't been, most of our party would have thought nothing of it. To the ladies, though, the subject seemed to call for some consideration.

"This water looks very deep — are you sure we can cross it on horseback?"

"Oh, yes! Petaille, you go first, and let us see how the water is."

Petaille did as he was told. He was mounted on a horse built like a giraffe, and by sticking his feet straight out sideways he certainly managed to get through the stream without much of a wetting.

It was clear the water would come up into the wagon, but that mattered less, since if the worst happened the passengers could climb up onto the seats.

My horse, Jerry, was taller than average, so I was soon across, with no inconvenience beyond having to pull my feet out of the stirrups and tuck them up snug against his mane.

Sister Margaret was still on Brunet. She was advised to trade him for one of the taller horses, but while the matter was being debated the perverse little wretch settled it himself by taking to the water without any ceremony at all, following the example of the other animals.

He was soon out of his depth, and we were alarmed and entertained at once to see his rider draw her feet out of the stirrups with remarkable skill and perch herself on top of the saddle, where she held her position and steered her stubborn little mount safely to land.

That was the last of our adventures. A pleasant four-mile ride brought us to the Fort just as the sun was throwing his last light across the glowing landscape, and when we reached the ferry the friends who were waiting for us took us straight to Major Twiggs's hospitable house.
